The Kill Fee Clause: Getting Paid for Work That Never Ships

A client hires you for a six-week project. You clear your calendar, turn down two other prospects, and do two weeks of solid work. Then the client emails: "We are going in a different direction. Let's pause this." Two weeks of your time, gone, with nothing to show and an awkward invoice to send.

This is the scenario a kill fee clause exists for. And most freelancers have never heard of it.

What a kill fee actually is

A kill fee is a contract term that guarantees you get paid a percentage of the project fee if the client cancels before completion. It is standard in publishing, advertising, and illustration, and it works everywhere else too. The idea is simple: you reserved your time and turned away other work, so the cancellation costs the client something, not everything.

A typical structure looks like this: 25% if cancelled before work starts, 50% if cancelled after the first milestone, 75% or full fee if cancelled after the second. The numbers are negotiable. The principle is not.

Why clients almost never object to it

The kill fee sounds aggressive until you say it out loud to a client. Try this exact framing:

"My terms include a kill fee schedule. If the project is cancelled after work starts, the fee covers the portion completed plus a reservation charge for the time I held. This is standard practice in my industry."

In practice, serious clients agree without blinking, because the clause only triggers if they cancel. Clients who push back hard on a kill fee are telling you something about how likely they are to cancel. That information is worth more than the project.

Where to put it and what it must say

The clause belongs in your contract or terms of engagement, signed before work starts. A verbal agreement to a kill fee is worth almost nothing when the cancellation email arrives. The written version needs three elements:

The trigger. Define exactly what counts as cancellation: the client pausing, postponing beyond a stated number of days, or changing the scope so drastically the original project no longer exists. Scope drift that quietly doubles the work should also trigger a re-scope conversation, which is a kill fee in spirit.

The schedule. Write the percentages plainly: 25% before start, 50% after milestone one, and so on. Tie the stages to deliverables the client already agreed to, so there is no argument about how far the work got.

The timeline. State when the kill fee is due: usually within 15 or 30 days of cancellation, on the same terms as a normal invoice. A kill fee with no due date is a suggestion.

How to enforce it without a fight

When the cancellation email comes, respond within 24 hours, professionally and briefly. Reference the clause by name and number, state the amount, and attach the invoice. Example:

"Thanks for letting me know. Per section 4 of our agreement, the kill fee for cancellation after milestone one is $1,500. I have attached the invoice, due in 15 days. Happy to discuss if you have questions."

Most clients pay. The fee was agreed in advance, the number is smaller than the full project, and arguing about it would cost them more in time than it saves. If a client refuses, you are in the same position as with any unpaid invoice: follow your escalation ladder, and the written clause makes every step stronger, because you are not arguing about whether payment is owed. You are pointing at a signed line.

The mistake that kills the clause

A kill fee only works if it is signed before work begins. Every week, freelancers discover they needed one at exactly the moment it is too late to add it. Make it a default line in your contract template, not a special addition for risky projects. The clients you are most comfortable with are the ones who will cancel politely and pay the fee without argument. The clause is not about distrust. It is about making the awkward conversation unnecessary.

One line in your contract, agreed in five seconds at the start, turns the worst cancellation into a minor inconvenience. Freelancers who bill kill fees once never sign a contract without one again.
