The Client Who Pays Late Because Their Own Client Has Not Paid Them: How to Break the Chain Without Losing the Work

The excuse sounds reasonable, and that is what makes it dangerous. Your client has not paid you because their client has not paid them, and they ask you to wait, as if your invoice were part of their cash flow problem rather than a separate agreement. The logic feels fair on the surface: money is stuck upstream, so it must be stuck downstream too. But your contract is not with their client. It is with them, and their client's payment behaviour is their business risk, not yours. The moment you accept the chain as a reason to wait, you become an unpaid lender to a business you never vetted, with no visibility into when, or whether, the upstream money will arrive. You can be sympathetic to a client in a tight spot and still hold your own terms, but only if you separate the sympathy from the schedule. Understanding their problem is good client service. Funding it is not.

1. Name the chain for what it is: their risk, not your term

The first step is a mental one, and it has to happen before you reply. Your client's late payment to you is not caused by their client's late payment to them in any contractual sense. They chose their client, they set their terms with that client, and they carry the risk of that client's behaviour, just as you carry the risk of theirs. When you treat the chain as a shared problem, you quietly rewrite your own terms to match their client's terms, which you never agreed to and cannot influence. Reply with that separation intact: you understand the pressure they are under, and your invoice terms stand as agreed, because your work was delivered on schedule and your costs do not pause while their client delays. This is not a lack of empathy. It is the boundary that keeps a sympathetic freelancer from becoming an interest-free credit line for a chain of businesses that never asked you to join it.

2. Ask for the facts, not the story

A chain excuse is usually told as a story: the big client is slow, the money is coming, just a little more time. Stories are hard to chase, because every new chapter resets the deadline. Ask for the facts that turn a story into a date. When was the upstream invoice issued, what are its terms, and when is it contractually due? Has your client already chased it, and what did the upstream client actually commit to, in writing? You do not need their client's name or their private correspondence, and you should not ask for it. You need enough to judge whether the wait is days or months, and whether your client is actively managing the problem or simply passing it down the line. A client who can answer those questions is managing a real delay. A client who cannot, or who answers with another story, is using the chain as a shield, and your reply should treat it that way, with a firm date rather than an open-ended wait.

3. Offer a short bridge, never an open-ended wait

If the client is genuinely caught in a short delay and the relationship is worth preserving, a bridge can be good business, but only if it is short, written, and priced. Agree to a specific new date, no more than two weeks out, in writing, with the full amount due on that date and no further extension built in. Do not accept a bridge that says "when our client pays us," because that date does not exist and you cannot enforce it. If the bridge stretches beyond two weeks, ask for a partial payment now, as a sign of good faith and a test of whether the client can pay anything at all. A client who cannot pay a fraction now is telling you something important about their cash position, and you should hear it before you agree to wait for the whole amount. A bridge with a date and a partial payment keeps the relationship warm without handing your schedule to a stranger upstream.

4. Protect the next job before you finish this conversation

The chain excuse, once accepted, tends to repeat, because the client's business model has not changed. Before you agree to any bridge, fix the terms that would stop the next invoice from joining the same chain. Shorten the payment term, ask for a deposit on the next project, or split the next job into milestones with payment before the next stage begins. Put it plainly: you are happy to work together again, and future work will be on shorter terms, because your business cannot carry upstream delays. Clients who value the work will accept this without argument, because it is a business term, not a punishment. Clients who push back hard are telling you that the chain is not a one-off problem but their normal way of operating, and that is information you need before you take the next job, not after you have delivered it.

5. If the chain never breaks, escalate on your own schedule

There comes a point where sympathy has to end and process has to take over, and that point is the date you set, not the date their client eventually pays. If the bridge date passes with no payment, do not ask for another update on the upstream client. Escalate exactly as you would for any other late invoice: a firm written reminder with a new pay-by date, a pause on further work, and then the formal steps in your escalation ladder, including a late fee if your terms provide for one and a final notice before any third-party step. The chain does not change the escalation, because your claim is against your client, and their claim is against theirs. Keeping the two claims separate is what protects you legally as well as commercially. A client who knows you will escalate on your own schedule, regardless of what happens upstream, is far less likely to pass the next delay down to you.

The pattern underneath

A client who pays late because their own client has not paid them is asking you to join a chain you never agreed to, and the way to break it is to name the risk as theirs, ask for facts instead of stories, offer a short written bridge rather than an open-ended wait, fix the terms on the next job before this conversation ends, and escalate on your own schedule if the bridge date passes. Sympathy is free, and you should give it generously. Credit is not free, and you should never give it to a business you never vetted, on terms you never set, for a date nobody can name.
