The Client Who Insists on Their Vendor Portal: Stop It Eating 30 Days

Vendor portals are where invoices go to wait quietly. The client says "just upload it to the portal" and thirty days later nobody has looked at it, because a portal is a filing cabinet, not a payment system. Portals do not pay you. People inside the portal pay you, and only after the portal's own machinery finishes.

The portal itself is not the enemy. The delay comes from everything around it: registration you never completed, a missing tax form flagged three weeks late, a PO number nobody gave you, an approver who left and was never replaced. Each of these costs you a week. Together they cost you a month or more.

Register at kickoff, not at first invoice.

The single biggest portal delay is onboarding. Clients request your tax forms, banking details, and insurance certificates through the portal, then take weeks to approve them, all before you can even submit. Complete registration the week the project starts, before any work is done. Treat it as part of the contract, not part of the invoice.

Get the PO before the portal.

Many portals will not accept an invoice without a purchase order number, and POs are issued by someone different from your day-to-day contact. Ask at kickoff: "Who issues my PO, and what do they need from me?" A portal invoice without a PO sits in an error queue that nobody monitors. The PO is the actual payment trigger; the portal is just where it lives.

Submit the invoice, then chase the human.

The portal gives you a submission timestamp. That is all it gives you. After submitting, send your normal payment-chase email to the human who approved the work, with the portal submission reference in the subject line. Portals have no urgency; humans do. Never assume "submitted" means "seen".

Learn the portal's approval chain on day one.

Ask your contact: "Once I submit, whose desk does it go to, and in what order?" Then ask for the names and email addresses of each approver. When the invoice stalls at step three, you email step three's person directly instead of refreshing the portal dashboard. This one question has saved more invoices than any feature any portal ships.

Put a portal-failure clause in your terms.

Add one line to your payment terms: if the client fails to provide portal access, PO numbers, or approvals needed to submit within 14 days of project start, payment terms run from the invoice date regardless. Portals become delay machines mostly when the client's side is unprepared. This clause puts the cost of that unpreparedness on the right side.

Keep your own records outside the portal.

Portals get upgraded, accounts get locked, and history vanishes when vendor records are archived. Keep your own copies of every submitted invoice, submission timestamp, and approval email. If a dispute arises, your records are the paper trail, not their portal's export.

The pattern underneath

A portal is a process, and processes stall. Your job is not to fight the portal but to work the humans inside it, starting at kickoff instead of day 30. Registration, PO, named approvers, and your own records turn a 60-day black box into a normal 14-day payment.
