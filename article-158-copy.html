The Client Who Asks You to Invoice a Different Company: How to Stop the Entity Shuffle

You did the work for one company. You agreed the price with one person. Then the invoice arrives back with a new instruction: "Actually, bill this one instead." A different company name, a different address, sometimes a company you have never heard of, and always a reason that sounds harmless. Tax reasons. Accounting reasons. "Our other entity handles payments." The freelancer who accepts the shuffle has just changed who owes them money, usually to an entity with no contract, no relationship, and no assets. The shuffle is rarely an accident. It is a way to move a debt somewhere it is harder to collect, and the only safe answer is to refuse it politely, firmly, and before it happens.

1. The debt belongs to whoever signed the deal

A payment obligation follows the contract, not the invoice. If you agreed the work with Bright Studio Ltd, then Bright Studio Ltd owes you, and an invoice addressed to Bright Holdings LLC does not transfer that debt. It replaces it with a claim against a stranger. The client knows this, even when the request sounds casual. The shuffle usually appears at the worst possible moment: after the work is delivered, when you are tired, when you want the relationship to end well, and when refusing feels awkward. That timing is the point. The rule to hold is simple: invoice the entity named in your agreement, and no other. If there is no written agreement, invoice the entity you actually worked for, the one whose people briefed you, approved the work, and received the deliverables. An invoice is a record of a real transaction. The moment it names a company that was never part of that transaction, it stops being an accurate record and starts being a favour, and favours in invoicing are how freelancers lose money.

2. Ask the one question that ends most shuffles

When the request arrives, do not argue about tax or accounting. Ask one question: "Could you confirm in writing that the original company remains liable for this invoice?" A legitimate administrative change has a clean answer: yes, of course, here is the confirmation, and the new entity is just the payment vehicle. A shuffle does not. The client will go quiet, or change the subject, or insist the new entity is the only one that can pay. All three answers tell you what you need to know. If the original company will not confirm it still owes you, then the shuffle is not administration. It is a transfer of your risk to a shell, and your answer is no. Keep the question short, warm, and in writing. "Happy to update the invoice details if you can confirm Bright Studio Ltd remains liable for payment." You have now made the client choose between a clean paper trail and an admission, and most shuffles die at exactly that choice.

3. Never reissue an invoice to a stranger mid-chase

The most dangerous version of the shuffle arrives while you are already chasing payment. The invoice is overdue, the client is slow, and then comes the helpful offer: "Reissue it to our new company and we will pay it this week." The freelancer who reissues has just reset the clock, replaced a debtor they have history with for one they do not, and handed the original company a defence: "We do not owe that invoice, it was reissued elsewhere." Never reissue an overdue invoice to a different entity. If the client genuinely restructured, the correct paperwork is a written assignment of the debt, signed by the original company, confirming it remains liable until the new entity pays. That document is the client's job to produce, not yours to invent. Until it exists, the original invoice stands, the original debtor stands, and your chase continues against them. A reissued invoice is not progress. It is a new debt with a worse debtor and a reset clock.

4. Put the entity rule in writing before the next job

The shuffle is easy to refuse once and hard to refuse repeatedly, so fix it in your setup. Before work starts, your agreement should name the contracting entity in full, its registered address, and the person authorised to approve payment. Add one sentence: "Invoices will be issued to the contracting entity named above, and payment must be received from that entity." This sentence costs nothing and saves everything, because it turns a future shuffle request into a breach of an agreed term rather than a negotiation. On every invoice, repeat the entity name exactly as it appears in the agreement. When the client later says "bill this one instead," you are not being difficult. You are following the agreement they signed. Clients who run legitimate multi-entity businesses will accept the sentence without a blink, because their own paperwork already works that way. The client who objects to it has told you something useful before any work begins.

5. If the shuffle already happened, unwind it fast

If you already reissued an invoice to a different entity, do not wait to see whether it gets paid. Write to both entities immediately, in one email, stating the facts: the work was performed for the original company, the invoice was reissued at their request, and you are reinstating the original invoice. Ask the original company to confirm in writing that it remains liable. Keep the tone factual, not accusatory, and keep every reply. If the new entity pays, the problem is solved, and you keep the record anyway. If it does not, you still have the original claim intact, because you moved quickly to correct the record before the original company could rely on the reissue. Time matters here. A reissue left uncorrected for months becomes the story, and the original company will eventually argue the debt moved with it. A correction sent within days is just bookkeeping. The freelancer who unwinds a shuffle fast loses nothing but a few minutes. The freelancer who waits loses the debtor.

The pattern underneath

An invoice must name the company that owes you, and no other company, for any reason, at any stage. The entity shuffle works because it arrives dressed as administration, at the moment you least want an argument. Refuse it with one question about liability, never reissue an overdue invoice to a stranger, put the entity rule in your agreement before the next job, and unwind any shuffle that already happened before it hardens into the story. The freelancer who gets paid is not the one who is most flexible about paperwork. They are the one whose paperwork always points at the company that actually owes them.
