The Client Who Says the Bank Blocked the Payment: How to Tell a Real Block From a Stall

The message arrives a few days after the due date, and it sounds reasonable: "We tried to pay, but the bank blocked the transfer." There is no apology for the delay, because in the client's telling there is nothing to apologise for. They tried. The bank stopped them. They will try again, or they are waiting for the bank, or the payment is being reviewed, and the implication is clear: the delay is not their fault, so there is nothing to chase and nothing to fix. Sometimes that is true. Banks do block transfers, for reasons that have nothing to do with the client. And sometimes it is a stall line, the kind that buys a client two free weeks while you wait politely for a bank that was never involved. Telling the difference matters, because the two situations need opposite responses: a real bank problem needs your help and a little patience, while a stall needs a deadline and a consequence, and treating one like the other wastes either your goodwill or your money.

The good news is that a real bank block leaves traces, and a made-up one does not. A transfer that a bank actually stopped produces a reference number, a reason code, a rejection notice, a date, and a timeline for the money coming back. A stall produces none of those things, only a story that gets vaguer every time you ask for a detail. This article is about asking for the details in a way that is professional, not accusatory, and about what to do with each answer you get.

1. What a real bank block actually looks like

Start with what can genuinely go wrong, because knowing the real reasons makes the fake ones easier to spot. The most common real block is a sanctions or fraud screening hold: the bank's automated system flags a payment because the amount, the destination country, the recipient name, or the payment reference matches a pattern it is required to check, and the payment sits in review while a human looks at it. These reviews usually resolve in one to three business days, and the bank tells the sender the payment is under review, with a reference number for the case. A second real reason is wrong payment details: a mistyped account number, an old sort code, a SWIFT code for a closed branch, or a name that does not match the account holder. The bank rejects the payment, the money returns to the sender, usually within three to five business days, and the sender gets a rejection notice with a reason.

A third real reason is a limit: daily transfer limits, first-time recipient limits, or business account limits that cap how much can leave in one day. These are common on smaller business accounts, and they are easy to verify, because the client can simply tell you the limit and the date the next transfer window opens. A fourth is a compliance hold on a first transfer to a new payee, which some banks impose for 24 to 72 hours, and a fifth is a genuine technical failure, which is rare and short, and which the bank will confirm in writing if asked.

Notice what all five have in common: each one produces something checkable. A reference number, a rejection notice, a return date, a limit with a number attached, or a named review with a named end date. A client with a real bank problem can produce at least one of these without effort, because the bank gave it to them. A client with a story cannot produce any of them, and the reason they cannot is that there is nothing to produce. That is the whole test, and it is a fair one, because you are not asking them to prove their innocence. You are asking them to share the information their bank already gave them.

2. The verification ask: four facts, one message

The verification ask should be short, warm, and specific, and it should be sent in writing, in the same thread as the invoice, so the answer is on the record. The message asks for four facts: the date they attempted the payment, the exact reason the bank gave, the reference number for the blocked payment or the case, and the date the money is expected back in their account or the date the bank says the review will finish. Here is a version you can copy and adapt: "Thanks for letting me know. To help me track this on my side, could you share the date you sent it, the reason the bank gave, and the reference number for the payment or the review? And when does the bank say the funds will be back with you or the review will finish? Once I have those, I can hold the invoice until that date and we will know exactly where we stand."

That message works because it is helpful, not hostile. It assumes the client is telling the truth, it gives them a clear list of things to share, and it sets a natural deadline: the date the bank gives them. A client with a real problem will usually reply within a day with at least two of the four facts, often with a screenshot of the bank's message, and the details will be consistent: the date will be recent, the reason will match one of the real reasons above, and the reference number will look like a reference number. A client with a stall will reply with a vaguer story, a promise to check, or a new detail that contradicts the first one, and the four facts will never quite arrive.

Watch for the three classic stall tells. The first is the moving date: "the bank blocked it on Monday" becomes "we tried last week" becomes "it was around the end of the month", and each version is a little less checkable than the one before. The second is the expanding cast: first it was the bank, then it was the bank's fraud team, then it was a compliance department in another country, and each new character pushes the resolution further away. The third is the refusal to share anything checkable: "I cannot share the reference for security reasons" or "the bank does not give reference numbers" are both untrue in practice, because banks give references for exactly this situation, and a client who will not share one is a client who does not have one.

3. The 48-hour proof deadline

When the client replies, whatever they say, set a deadline for proof, and put it in writing. Forty-eight hours is fair: it is enough time for anyone to open their banking app, find the payment, and send you a screenshot or a reference number, and it is short enough that a stall cannot live on it for weeks. The wording stays warm and practical: "No problem at all. Could you send the reference or a screenshot by Thursday? That way I can note it against the invoice and hold any further follow-up until then." That sentence does two jobs: it gives the client a specific, easy action, and it tells them that the follow-up pauses only until Thursday, not indefinitely, so the stall has a visible end date.

If the proof arrives, read it carefully. A real rejection notice names the payment: the amount, the date, the recipient, and the reason, and it comes from the bank, not from the client. A screenshot of a banking app should show the payment as failed, returned, or under review, with a date that matches the story. If the screenshot shows a payment as "processing" or "sent" with no failure, the bank has not blocked anything, and the payment is simply in transit, which means the client should be able to tell you when it will land. If the screenshot is cropped so tightly that the date, the amount, or the status is hidden, ask for the full view, and say why: "I can see the payment, but the date and status are cut off. Could you send the full screen? I want to make sure I am tracking the right transfer."

If the proof does not arrive by the deadline, do not ask again in the same way. Move to the next step, which is offering an alternative way to pay, and the escalation that follows if the alternative is not used. Asking a third time for the same reference number is how a two-week stall becomes a two-month stall, because each request resets the clock and gives the client another 48 hours of free waiting. The deadline is the deadline, and when it passes, the conversation changes.

4. Offer a second rail, and watch what happens

The fastest way to resolve a bank block, real or invented, is to offer a second way to pay. If the bank transfer is blocked, a card payment, a PayPal payment, a Wise transfer, or a payment through a second bank will almost always work, because a block on one rail rarely affects the others. Offer the alternative plainly, with the link or the details ready to use: "If the transfer is stuck, here is a card payment link for the same amount. That should clear straight away, and we can sort the bank side out afterwards." A client with a real bank problem will usually take the alternative, because they want the invoice settled as much as you do, and the block was genuinely in the way. A client with a stall will find a reason the alternative does not work either: their card is with another department, PayPal is not approved, Wise is not on their system, and each refusal narrows the options until the only remaining way to pay is the one that is supposedly blocked.

That pattern is the clearest signal you will get. A client who refuses every way to pay is not a client with a payment problem. They are a client with a paying problem, and the bank story was the cover for it. At that point, stop solving the bank problem and start solving the payment problem: restate the amount, the due date, and the next step in one short message, and give a firm date for the alternative payment. "The invoice is 2,000, due on the 15th, and it is now 10 days overdue. Please pay by card using this link by Friday, or let me know which day this week the transfer will clear. If neither happens by Friday, I will pause the next phase of work until the balance is settled." That message is firm without being angry, it gives the client two ways to close the matter, and it names the consequence in advance, which is what makes it work.

If the client pays by the alternative rail, note the lesson and move on. The bank block may have been real, and the alternative rail solved it, which is a good outcome for everyone. But remember the pattern for next time: this client now has two rails on file, and the next invoice can offer both from the start, so a single blocked rail can never again be a reason to wait.

5. When the story repeats

A bank block that happens once is a banking event. A bank block that happens on the second invoice, and the third, is a payment strategy, and it needs to be treated as one. The pattern to watch is not the excuse itself, because the excuse will change: the bank blocked it, then the finance system was down, then the approver was on leave, then the bank blocked it again. The pattern is that every invoice needs a story, and every story needs two weeks, and the payment always arrives eventually, just late enough to be a problem. That is the same pattern as the client who pays only when chased, and the fix is the same: change the terms so the story has nowhere to live.

The terms that work are the ones that remove the bank from the critical path. A deposit before work starts means the first payment is already in your account before the bank story can begin. Milestone billing means no single invoice carries the whole project, so a blocked transfer delays a phase, not the entire fee. A shorter payment term means the story has less time to run before the payment is officially late, and a late fee that starts on the due date means the story costs the client money, which is the fastest way to find out whether the bank is really the problem. And a clause in your terms, in plain words, that payment is due on the stated date regardless of the client's bank, payment system, or internal process, gives you something to quote when the story arrives for the third time, so the answer is the contract speaking, not you being unsympathetic.

There is also a prevention step that costs nothing and saves the most time: verify the payment details before the work starts, not after the invoice is overdue. At the scoping stage, confirm the billing contact, the payment method, the bank details, and the approval process, in writing, and ask the client to confirm them back. A client who confirms the details in advance has far less room to claim later that the bank details were wrong, and a client who will not confirm them has told you, before you have done any work, that payment will be a problem. That is useful information, and it is cheapest when you learn it before the project starts.

The pattern underneath

"The bank blocked it" is a perfect stall line, because it sounds technical, it blames a third party, and it asks you to wait for a process you cannot see. The way to answer it is not to argue about banks. It is to ask for the four facts a real block always produces, a date, a reason, a reference, and a return date, and to give the client 48 hours to share them. If the facts arrive, help the client solve the real problem, offer a second rail, and note the lesson for the next invoice. If the facts never arrive, or the second rail is refused, or the story repeats on the next invoice, stop solving the bank problem and solve the payment problem instead: a firm date, a named consequence, and terms that put a deposit and milestones between you and the next story. A bank that really blocked a payment leaves a paper trail. A story that leaves no trail is not a bank problem, and once you know that, you will never again spend two weeks waiting for a bank that was never involved.
