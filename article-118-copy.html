Charging Interest on Late Invoices: The Math, the Wording, and When It Backfires

Late fees work. But most freelancers get the math wrong, the wording wrong, or both, and a badly charged fee costs more in goodwill than it earns in dollars. Here is how to do it right.

The math first

A late fee has to be stated in your terms before the work starts. Charging a fee the client never agreed to is not a policy; it is a surprise, and surprises get disputed. One line in your quote or contract does the job: "Invoices unpaid after [X] days accrue a late fee of 1.5% per month."

The 1.5% monthly rate is the most common default in freelance contracts. It works out to about 18% annually, which is enough to sting without looking punitive. Some freelancers use a flat fee per month ($25 or $50); flat fees are simpler to invoice but sting less on large amounts. Percentage fees scale with the debt, which is fairer and easier to defend.

One honest warning: do the math on the fee you actually enforce. A 1.5% fee on a $2,000 invoice is $30. That will not change anyone's behavior by itself. The fee's real power is the signal: it tells the client this invoice is tracked, metered, and serious. The clients who respond to fees respond to the principle, not the dollars.

The wording that holds up

State the fee in plain language, in writing, before the work begins. The line belongs in your payment terms, your quote, and your first invoice. Three versions:

  • Simple: "A late fee of 1.5% per month applies to invoices unpaid after 30 days."
  • Firm: "Late payments accrue interest at 1.5% per month from the due date."
  • With a cap: "A late fee of 1.5% per month applies after 30 days, capped at 10% of the invoice total."

The capped version is worth considering. It answers the client's first objection ("how much can this grow?") before they ask it, and it costs you almost nothing, since you will enforce the fee long before the cap matters.

Never charge a fee that was not agreed. If your terms say Net 15 and nothing about fees, you can shorten future terms and add a fee clause going forward, but you cannot invent a fee for the invoice already out. Retroactive fees are unenforceable in practice and poison the relationship for nothing.

Enforcing it without drama

Enforcing means invoicing the fee as its own line item on the next statement, not deducting it from goodwill in your head. Send it with the same tone as the invoice: factual, dated, payable. "Per the payment terms agreed on [date], a late fee of $[X] applies to invoice [#] ([X] days overdue at 1.5%/month). The updated balance is $[Y]."

Many freelancers waive the fee the first time a client pays late and say so out loud: "I am waiving this month's late fee as a one-time courtesy. The fee applies from next time." That sentence turns an enforcement moment into a boundary moment, which is more valuable than $30.

When it backfires

Fees backfire in exactly two situations. First, when the fee surprises the client. If it was not in your terms, do not charge it. Fix the terms for next time. Second, when you weaponize it against a client who is otherwise good. A loyal client who pays five days late twice a year does not need a fee lecture; they need a gentle nudge. Save the meter for the clients who are genuinely testing you. A fee policy is a tool, not a personality.
