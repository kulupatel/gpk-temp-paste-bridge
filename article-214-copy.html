The Client Who Asks for an Invoice With No Due Date: How to Put a Date Back In

It usually arrives as a small, friendly request: "Can you leave the due date off this invoice? We will pay it when it is processed." Nothing about it sounds like a refusal. There is no argument, no dispute, no complaint about the work. But an invoice with no due date is not an invoice. It is a request for payment with no deadline attached, and a payment with no deadline is a payment that can always wait until next week, because next week never breaks a promise nobody made.

A due date is not aggression. It is the one piece of information that turns an invoice from a document into an obligation. Without it, you cannot chase politely, because there is nothing to chase against. You cannot charge a late fee, because nothing is late. You cannot pause work, because no term has been broken. The client has not refused to pay. They have simply removed the only thing that would ever make non payment a problem. So the job is to put a date back in, without turning a friendly client into an argument.

1. Name what the missing date actually costs you

Before you reply, be clear with yourself about why the request matters, because your reply needs to carry that clarity in one calm sentence. Without a date, your cash flow has no shape. You cannot plan around money that might arrive, you cannot tell a genuinely slow payer from a stalling one, and every follow up you send sounds like nagging instead of a reminder, because you are asking for something that was never promised.

Say that, briefly, and without apology: "I keep a due date on every invoice so we both know when payment is expected. It protects the schedule on both sides." That framing matters. You are not accusing the client of planning to delay. You are describing a system that works for both of you. Most clients accept it at once, because the request to remove the date was often a habit from their internal process, not a strategy. The ones who push back are showing you that the open ended invoice was the point.

2. Offer a date they can live with, not a fight about the principle

If the client says their system cannot take a due date, or that their finance team works in cycles, do not argue about whether a date is allowed. Offer a date that fits their cycle and still gives you a deadline: "I can set the due date to the end of your next payment run, 30 November, and note that it is payable on that run." You have conceded the timing. You have not conceded the existence of a date.

The key is that the date is written on the invoice and in your covering message, in plain words, with a day and a month. A phrase like "payable on processing" is not a date. A phrase like "due 30 November" is. If the client wants a longer term, negotiate the term openly: 30 days instead of 14, or payment on the next run, whatever fits. A long date you both agreed is infinitely better than no date at all, because a long date can be chased, and no date cannot.

3. Never let the invoice leave without a date in your own records

Sometimes a client insists, and the relationship matters enough that you consider sending the invoice as requested. If you do, never let their version be the only record. Keep your own dated copy, send a covering email that states the expected date in your own words, and log that date in your tracker on the day you send it: "Invoice 215 sent 3 November, expected payment by 30 November per email of 3 November."

That email is your due date, even if the PDF carries none. It reads: "As discussed, invoice 215 is attached. I have noted payment by 30 November, in line with your payment run." If the client does not correct that date, their silence is a record too. And when 30 November passes, your chase is not a fresh demand. It is a reminder of a date you stated in writing and they did not dispute. You have rebuilt the deadline outside the document that omitted it.

4. Make the next invoice carry the date as a matter of course

One dateless invoice is a favour. Two is a precedent. So make sure the next invoice, and every invoice after it, carries a due date without any discussion. Put your standard terms on the quote, on the invoice template, and in the covering email, so the date is never a point of negotiation again: "Payment due within 14 days of invoice date."

When the same client asks again, your answer is short and boring, which is exactly how you want it: "My invoices all carry a due date, so I will keep the usual 14 days on this one." Boring is good. Boring means the term is a system, not a personal demand, and systems are much harder to argue with than people. Over time, the client stops asking, because the answer is always the same, and the invoice always has a date.

5. Know the line where a missing date means no deal

There is a version of this request that is not friendly at all: a client who will only work with you if invoices carry no date, who removes dates from every document, and who treats any deadline as an insult. That client is not asking for flexibility. They are asking for an open ended obligation, and an open ended obligation is the one term you should never accept, because it removes every remedy you have if payment never comes.

For that client, the answer is a polite no with an alternative: "I am happy to agree longer terms, 30 or even 45 days, but I do need a date on the invoice. If that does not work for your process, I understand, and we can leave this project here." Losing a project that would never have paid on time is not a loss. It is a project you declined before it cost you weeks of chasing with nothing to chase against.

The pattern underneath

An invoice with no due date feels flexible and functions like a payment that can never be late. Name what the missing date costs, offer a date the client can live with, keep your own dated record if you ever concede, make the date automatic on every future invoice, and refuse the open ended version outright. The client keeps a process they can follow, and you keep the one thing that makes an invoice enforceable: a day, a month, and a promise attached to both.
