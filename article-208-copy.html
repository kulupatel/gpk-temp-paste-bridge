The Client Who Asks for Proof of Payment for Work They Have Not Paid For: How to Answer Without Creating a False Record

The request sounds reasonable: "Can you send me proof of payment for this invoice?" The problem is that no payment exists. The invoice is open, the money never arrived, and the client wants a document that says otherwise, perhaps for their accounts, perhaps for a tax filing, perhaps to show a third party. Sending it would create a false record with your name on it. Refusing it badly can sound like an accusation. This is a request to handle with precision, not with politeness alone.

A proof of payment is a statement about a fact: money was received, on a date, against a numbered invoice. If that fact is false, no version of the document is safe. A receipt, a confirmation email, a stamped invoice, or a message saying "all settled" all do the same damage once they leave your inbox. The client may use the document in ways you never see, and you will be the one who signed it.

1. Check the record before you reply

Before answering, check the facts. Search your bank, your payment app, and your tracker for the invoice number, the client name, and the amount. Payments do arrive under unfamiliar names, through new rails, or against the wrong reference, and accusing a client of inventing a payment that actually cleared is a mistake you cannot easily undo. If a payment exists somewhere, match it and say so. If it does not, you now reply from certainty rather than suspicion.

If the payment is partial, say that exactly: the amount received, the date, and the balance outstanding. A partial receipt is honest and useful, and it answers the client's accounts question without overstating anything. Never round a partial payment up into a full settlement, even as a favour, because the rounded version is the one that gets forwarded.

2. Answer with the true status, in writing

The reply should state the invoice status plainly, with numbers: "Invoice [number] for [amount], issued [date], is currently unpaid. No payment has been received against it as of today. I can issue a receipt immediately on receipt of payment." That sentence does three jobs. It refuses the false document without using the word refuse. It gives the client a true statement they can use. And it creates a written record that you did not confirm a payment that never happened.

Do not add speculation about why the client asked. They may have confused this invoice with another, their finance team may have misfiled it, or they may be testing whether you will sign anything. Your reply does not need to decide which. The facts are enough, and facts are much harder to argue with than motives.

3. Offer the documents you can honestly send

A refusal alone can stall the conversation, so offer the true alternatives in the same message. You can send a copy of the invoice, a statement of account showing the open balance, a pro forma invoice if their accounts team needs a document to raise payment, or a receipt template marked clearly as awaiting payment. Each of these helps the client's process without misrepresenting the facts.

If the client says their system requires a receipt before it will release payment, that is a process problem on their side, not a reason to falsify yours. The honest bridge is a statement of account plus a note that a receipt will follow on clearance. Most finance teams accept this once it is offered, because what they actually need is a document to attach to a payment run, not a false confirmation.

4. Do not let urgency rewrite the record

This request often arrives with pressure: an auditor waiting, a month-end close, a manager who needs the file today. Urgency is the oldest reason people sign things they should not. A deadline on the client's side does not change what is true on yours, and a document created under pressure is still your document afterwards.

If the client insists, repeat the true status once, briefly, and stop negotiating the wording. Do not agree to a receipt "just for internal use", a confirmation "that does not count as a receipt", or a message that says "paid" with a wink. Internal documents leak, informal confirmations get forwarded, and a payment confirmation with a qualifier still reads as a confirmation to everyone except you. The line is simple: no payment, no proof of payment.

5. Protect the next invoice from the same request

If a client has asked once, they may ask again, so make the next invoice harder to misuse. Put the payment status on every invoice and every statement: the invoice number, the amount, the due date, and, once paid, the receipt date and method. A client who already holds a clear statement of account has less reason to ask for a separate proof document, and a clear paper trail makes any future false claim easy to answer.

Add one line to your terms for future clients: receipts are issued on cleared payment only. It sounds obvious, and that is why it works. When the request comes, you are not inventing a policy in the moment. You are pointing at a rule the client agreed to before the invoice existed.

The pattern underneath

A request for proof of an unpaid invoice is a request to lend your credibility to someone else's paperwork. The answer is not suspicion and it is not compliance. It is a checked record, a true status stated in writing, honest alternative documents, and a line that urgency cannot move. Say what is true, offer what is real, and let the receipt arrive when the money does.
