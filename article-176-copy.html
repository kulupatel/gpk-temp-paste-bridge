The Client Who Pays Part of the Invoice and Goes Quiet on the Rest: How to Chase the Balance Without Reopening the Whole Job

Half the invoice is paid, and then silence. The client is not disputing the work, not asking for changes, not complaining about anything. They simply stop responding about the balance. It is an awkward position, because the relationship still feels friendly, and chasing the rest feels like accusing someone who has already paid you something. But a partial payment is not a payment. It is a payment plus an unpaid balance, and the balance is the part that quietly becomes a write-off if you let the conversation drift. The trick is to chase the balance as its own small, clear job, without reopening the whole project or turning a friendly client into an opponent.

1. Treat the balance as a separate invoice in your own head

The first mistake is to keep thinking of the job as one invoice that is "mostly paid". Mostly paid is unpaid, and your follow-up should reflect that. Work out the exact balance, the date the part payment arrived, and the original due date, and then treat the balance as the only amount under discussion. Your messages should name one number, the balance, and one date, the date it was due. Do not re-litigate the whole project, the scope, or the original price. The client already accepted all of that by paying part of it. When you write, you are not asking them to agree the work was worth doing. You are asking them to finish a payment they already started. That framing is calmer for both sides, and it gives the client a simple action: pay this amount, and the account is closed.

2. Thank them for the part payment, then name the balance plainly

Start with the part payment, not with the silence. A short note that thanks them for the payment received, states the amount received, states the balance outstanding, and gives a clear way to pay it, is hard to ignore and easy to act on. Something like: thanks for the payment of this amount on this date, which leaves this balance outstanding from the original invoice, due on this date. Here is the payment link again, and the account will be closed as soon as the balance clears. No lecture, no list of the messages they did not answer, no question about why they stopped. Clients who went quiet often went quiet because the next step felt complicated or embarrassing. A message that makes the next step simple, and that treats the part payment as good faith rather than a trick, is the message most likely to get a reply.

3. Do not do more work until the balance clears

This is the rule that protects you from the second half of the problem. A client who owes a balance and asks for a small extra, a tweak, a new version, or help with something related, is asking you to extend credit to an account that is already overdue. Say no kindly, and tie the no to the balance: happy to help with that, and I will get to it as soon as the outstanding balance is settled. This is not punishment. It is the same rule any supplier follows, and clients understand it instantly when it is stated as a normal business practice rather than a personal judgement. Every extra you do while a balance is outstanding makes the balance harder to collect, because the client now owes you for two things and feels further behind. Keep the account to one number, and keep new work on the other side of paying it.

4. Set one firm date, and say what happens on it

A balance without a date drifts, because there is always a reason this week is busy. Give the balance a single firm date, in writing, and say what will happen on that date in plain, non-threatening language. For example: if the balance is not received by this date, I will pause any further work and pass the account to my standard late-payment process, which includes a late fee and a formal payment request. You do not need to threaten legal action to be taken seriously. You need a date and a consequence that you are genuinely prepared to follow through on. If the date passes and you extend it silently, you have taught the client that your dates are suggestions. If the date passes and you follow through, even with just a formal written demand, you have taught them that your dates mean something. The second lesson is worth more than the balance itself, because it governs every future invoice.

5. Keep the record short, and keep the door open

Every message about the balance should be short, dated, and saved. Date, amount received, balance outstanding, due date, payment method. No long explanations, no emotion, no recap of the project history. A short record is easy to forward to a bookkeeper, an accountant, or a formal process later, and it is easy for the client to act on now. At the same time, keep the door open for a genuine reason: if cash flow is the problem, a short, dated instalment plan for the balance, agreed in writing, beats silence every time. Two payments on agreed dates, with the account closed on the second, is a real outcome. An open-ended promise to pay soon is not. The difference is dates in writing, and your willingness to hold them.

The pattern underneath

A partial payment feels like progress, and it is, but only if the balance follows. Name the balance as its own number, thank the client for what arrived, do no more work until it clears, set one firm date with a consequence you will follow, and keep the record short and the door open to a dated plan. The goal is not to win an argument about the original job. The client already settled that argument by paying part of it. The goal is to finish the payment, close the account, and keep a client you can work with again, on terms that are paid in full next time.
