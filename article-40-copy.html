The Year-End Chase: How to Collect Overdue Invoices Before Clients Go Quiet for the Holidays

Every December, accounts-payable departments go quiet. People take leave, budgets close, and invoices that were "almost paid" in November slide into January. If you have overdue invoices, the weeks between now and the shutdown are your last real window to collect them. Here is how to use it.

Know your enemy: the AP shutdown calendar

Most companies do not stop paying in December; they stop processing new things. The pattern looks like this: mid-November, finance starts clearing the books for year-end. Early December, many AP teams work with skeleton staff. The last two weeks of December, approvals stall because the people who approve are on leave.

The practical consequence: an invoice that enters the approval chain in the second week of December may not emerge until mid-January. So your real deadline is not December 31. It is the date their AP team stops processing, which is usually earlier than they admit.

Ask the one question nobody asks

Email your billing contact in the first week of November with this: "What is your AP cutoff date for payments processed before the holidays?" You are not chasing. You are planning. Clients answer this question readily, and the answer gives you your actual deadline for every outstanding invoice.

While you are at it, ask a second question: "Is there anything you need from me to get [invoice number] into the next payment run?" Missing attachments, unsigned timesheets, and unapproved line items are the most common reasons an invoice sits. November is the month to clear them.

Run the November chase calendar

For invoices already overdue, compress your normal escalation into a four-week year-end sprint:

Week 1 (early November): the firm restart. A single email restating the overdue amount, the original due date, and a specific pay-by date tied to their cutoff. "To land in your pre-holiday payment run, I need this cleared by [date]."

Week 2 (mid-November): the phone call. December voicemail is a black hole. People still answer phones in November. Call, be warm, confirm the invoice is in the queue, and ask what would move it into the next run.

Week 3 (late November): the constructive path. If they cannot pay in full, offer a short, written arrangement: a substantial first payment now, the balance on a fixed date in January. Get it signed. A written plan beats a verbal "we will sort it in the new year" every time.

Week 4 (early December): the final notice. If nothing has moved, send your final notice with a clear January escalation date. You are not threatening; you are documenting. January-you will need this paper trail.

Do not start new escalations in the last two weeks of December. There is nobody home to escalate to.

Make full payment the easy option

Year-end is when clients are most willing to clear small balances, because a clean ledger is its own reward. Make it frictionless: one email with the total owed, every invoice number, the payment link, and a single confirmation line. "Paying these three invoices by [date] closes the year clean for both of us." Many clients will clear the whole stack just to stop thinking about it.

If they push to January, get the commitment in writing: the exact amount, the exact date, the named person who will authorize it. "We will take care of it in January" without a date and a name is a ghost story.

The January rule

Whatever is still unpaid on January 2 gets the full escalation treatment immediately. No grace period, no "let us settle back in." Clients who survive the holidays owing you money have had six weeks to pay. Your January 2 email restarts the ladder at the firm stage, not the friendly one.

There is one exception: clients who signed a written payment arrangement in December. Honor it to the letter. If they miss the first scheduled payment, the arrangement is void and the full balance is due. That was the deal.

What not to do in December

Do not send threats in the week before Christmas. They will not be read, and you will look unreasonable to the person who reads them in January. Do not offer discounts for year-end payment unless the math works (a small, time-limited discount for immediate full payment can beat a January chase, but never discount below your actual cost of waiting). And do not do new work for an overdue client in December on the promise of a January cleanup. The cleanup rarely comes.

The year-end chase is not about being aggressive. It is about being early. The freelancers who collect in December are the ones who asked about cutoff dates in November, called in mid-November, and sent their final notices in early December. Everyone else is writing their first firm email on January 6, to an inbox that has six weeks of backlog in front of it. Be early.
