The Client Who Pays in a Different Currency Without Agreeing a Rate: How to Lock the Currency Up Front

You quote in your own currency, the client pays in theirs, and the amount that arrives is less than the invoice. Nobody stole anything. The bank converted at its own rate, took its own fee, and the shortfall lands on you. It feels too small to argue about, so you absorb it, and then it happens on the next invoice, and the next, until a quiet percentage of every international payment simply disappears. Currency is a term of the agreement, just like the due date and the late fee, and if it is not agreed up front, the client's bank will agree it for you, in their favour.

1. Name the currency on every quote and invoice

The fix starts before any work begins, with one line: all prices are in a named currency, and payment is due in that currency. Put it on the quote, on the agreement, and on the invoice itself, in plain words, not a symbol that can be misread. If the client needs to pay in a different currency, that is a conversation, not a surprise at payment time: agree who bears the conversion cost, and agree the rate source and the date it is fixed, before the invoice is sent. A client who pays in another currency without discussion is not necessarily acting in bad faith. Often their finance system simply pays in its home currency by default. The default only becomes your problem when you never named yours.

2. Decide who bears the conversion cost, in advance

Conversion has a cost, and someone always pays it. The cleanest rule is that the client bears it: the amount you receive, after conversion and fees, must equal the invoiced amount. State this plainly, because clients who pay internationally are used to it from other suppliers, and it is far easier to agree before work starts than to reclaim a shortfall after payment. If a client insists that you bear the cost, price it in: add a stated international payment allowance to the quote, so the cost is visible and agreed, rather than deducted silently by a bank. What does not work is absorbing the cost invoice after invoice and hoping the client notices. They will not notice, because from their side the invoice was paid in full.

3. Chase a shortfall the same way you chase a late payment

When a payment arrives short because of conversion, treat it as an outstanding balance, not as a rounding error to forget. Send a short, factual note: the invoice was for this amount in this currency, the amount received was this, and the balance is this. Attach the invoice and the payment receipt, and give a date by which the balance is due. Most shortfalls are resolved by this note alone, because the client's finance team simply did not know the conversion had eaten part of the payment. If the shortfall is genuinely tiny, you may choose to write it off, but write it off in writing, as a one-off, and record it. An unrecorded write-off becomes the client's expectation that every international invoice will be short, and that you will never mention it.

4. Watch for the currency pattern

A single currency shortfall is information, and it is worth recording alongside the invoice: the currency paid, the rate applied, the fee deducted, and the shortfall. Sometimes the pattern is a single client's bank with poor rates, and the fix is a different payment route, such as a payment link that settles in your currency. Sometimes the pattern is your own pricing: you quote internationally without naming a currency, and every client fills the gap with their own. Record the pattern, and change the term that causes it. A freelancer who forgets last project's shortfall will quote the next project the same way, and the bank will take the same cut, again, quietly.

5. Write the currency term into the next agreement

The lasting fix is a single line, agreed before work starts: invoices are issued in a named currency, payment is due in that currency, and any conversion cost or bank fee is borne by the client, so that the amount received equals the invoiced amount. When this line exists, the next international payment answers itself, and you do not have to negotiate with a bank's exchange rate after the money has already moved. Clients who pay internationally will not object, because this is how their other suppliers invoice them. Clients who object are telling you they expect you to absorb their payment costs, which is exactly the arrangement the line prevents. One sentence, agreed once, keeps every international invoice whole.

The pattern underneath

A payment in a different currency feels like a detail and functions like a quiet discount you never agreed to. Name the currency on every quote and invoice, decide who bears the conversion cost in advance, chase a shortfall like any outstanding balance, record the currency pattern, and write the currency term into your next agreement. The goal is not to be difficult about international clients. It is to make sure the amount you quoted is the amount you receive, in the currency you named, on every invoice.
