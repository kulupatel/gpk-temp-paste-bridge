How to Raise Your Rates for Existing Clients Without Losing the Good Ones

Most freelancers do not lose money because they cannot find clients. They lose money because the clients they already have are still paying last year's rate, or the rate from three years ago, while everything else about the work has moved on. Skills improve, turnaround gets faster, the work gets better, and the invoice stays exactly where it started.

Nobody volunteers to pay you more. Clients will happily keep a good freelancer at an old rate for as long as you let them. Your rate is your job to update. Done right, most good clients accept it without drama. Done badly, with a surprise number on the next invoice, even a loyal client feels ambushed.

Do it this way: set the new rate, sort your clients, give proper notice, and fix your payment terms at the same time.

1. Fix your new-client rate first

Before you write to a single existing client, decide what a brand new client pays from today. That number is your anchor. If new clients already pay 40 percent more than your oldest client, you have not got a pricing problem, you have got a loyalty discount you never meant to offer.

Quote the real rate to every new enquiry starting now. You stop the gap from growing, and you get proof: when two or three new clients accept the higher rate without blinking, the fear around telling existing clients shrinks to its real size, which is much smaller than it feels at your desk.

2. Pick a size you can defend in one sentence

For an existing client on an annual review, a 5 to 10 percent increase is the quiet kind. Most clients absorb it as normal business, especially if they have seen their own suppliers do the same. A 10 to 20 percent increase is the usual range when scope has grown, you have added a skill, or you have not raised rates for a couple of years. It needs a short, honest reason, but it does not need a case file.

A market correction is different. If you discover you are charging half the going rate, do not try to close the whole gap in one email. That is how you lose a client you wanted to keep. Raise new clients to the full rate immediately, and phase existing clients toward it over 12 months, in two steps, with the second step dated in the first notice. Clients accept a planned path far more easily than a shock.

Whatever size you pick, defend it in one sentence: "My rate moves from 60 to 70 per hour from January 1, which brings you in line with what new clients have paid since June." Pick one reason, market alignment, expanded scope, or a new skill. One reason, stated plainly, beats five reasons that sound like pleading.

3. Time it like a business decision, not a confession

The best moments are the ones clients already expect a commercial conversation: the start of a new year, a contract renewal, the start of a new quarter, or right after a project that went well. A client who has just seen good results is at peak willingness to keep you. Raising rates right after a win is not opportunistic. It is the moment the value is clearest.

Two moments to avoid. Never put a new rate on an invoice without warning. A changed number on an invoice reads as a billing error at best and a betrayal at worst, and it starts a dispute about money instead of a conversation about value. And never raise rates in the middle of a project. Finish current work at the agreed rate, and apply the new rate to new work after the effective date. Honouring the current project costs you a little in the short term and buys you all of your credibility.

Give 30 to 60 days of notice as the standard, or 90 if the client needs budget approval. Notice gives the client time to plan and to feel like a partner in the change rather than a victim of it.

4. Send one short, confident notice in writing

Always put it in writing, even if you also discuss it on a call. A call alone leaves room for two different memories of what was agreed. A short email leaves a record, and the record protects both sides.

A good notice has five parts and nothing more: appreciation in one line, the new rate, the effective date, what stays the same, and an open door to talk. Here is a version you can adapt:

Subject: Updated rates from January 1

Hi [Name],

I have really enjoyed working together on [project or retainer]. A quick heads-up: my rates are updating from January 1. The new rate is [new rate] per [hour, day, or month]. Current work in progress stays at the current rate, and the new rate applies to new work from January 1.

This brings your rate in line with what new clients have paid since [month]. If you would like to lock in a block of hours at the current rate before then, reply and I will hold them.

Thanks for the partnership. I look forward to what is next.

[Your name]

Notice what is missing: no apology, no long story about your costs, no asking for permission. You are informing a client of a change, not applying for one. Clients pay more because the work is worth more, not because your rent went up.

5. Sort your clients before you send anything

Not every client should get the same treatment, and a rate increase is the perfect moment to sort them honestly into three groups.

Group one: the good payers. They pay on time, respect scope, and reply when you need a decision. These are the clients this article is about protecting. Give them the full notice period, the friendliest version of the email, and, if you want, a small bridge: a block of hours at the old rate, or one final project at the old rate. A bridge is a kindness, not a discount habit. Put an expiry date on it.

Group two: the clients you keep but tighten. They pay late, or scope creeps, or every invoice needs a chase. Raise their rate and tighten their terms in the same notice. Move them from Net 30 to Net 14, require a deposit, or add a work-pause rule. If they accept the new terms, the relationship improves. If they refuse both, you have learned something useful.

Group three: the clients you would quietly be relieved to lose. Set the new rate at the full market rate with no bridge and no negotiation. If they leave, you have freed capacity. If they stay, the work finally pays what it costs you.

6. Have your three pushback replies ready before you send

Most clients accept. A few push back, and pushback goes much better when you have already decided what you will say.

"I cannot afford that." Do not negotiate the rate down. Negotiate the scope instead. Offer a smaller package at the new rate: fewer hours, fewer revisions, a narrower deliverable. This shows flexibility without teaching the client that your rate is a starting bid. If even the smaller package does not fit, the client has a budget problem, not a you problem, and you can part warmly.

"I need to get approval." Give them a date, not an open door. "Of course. Could you let me know by [date, a week before the effective date] so I can plan capacity?" A deadline turns a vague delay into a decision, and it protects you from a client who uses approval to keep the old rate running indefinitely.

"I will have to go elsewhere." Let them, gracefully. "I understand, and I have enjoyed working with you. The new rate stands, and the door is open if things change." Chasing a client who leaves over a fair increase usually means discounting to keep work you will resent. The capacity you free up is worth more than the invoice you kept at the wrong price, provided you use it to find a client who pays the new rate.

One rule covers all three replies: never negotiate against yourself, and do not offer a discount before anyone has asked for one.

7. Use the increase to fix your payment terms too

A rate notice is the one moment a client expects commercial terms to change, so it is the cheapest moment to fix the terms that have been costing you money. Do not waste it on price alone.

Three upgrades fit naturally here. Shorten Net 30 to Net 14 for new work, add or raise the deposit (50 percent upfront on projects, first month prepaid on retainers), and state the late fee and work-pause rule in plain language before anyone is late. Clients accept these far more easily alongside a rate rise than as a separate demand later.

Put the updated terms in writing. A bigger invoice paid 60 days late is still a cash flow problem.

8. What not to do

Do not apologise your way through it, justify the rise with your personal costs, or raise rates only for the clients you think will accept. Clients buy outcomes and reliability, not your expenses, and selective increases punish your best clients. Do not set a new rate and then immediately discount it to keep someone: a discounted rate is your new rate, and the client learns your first number is negotiable. Offer scope, timing, or a bridge with an expiry date, never a lower rate. And do not wait for the perfect moment. If you have not raised rates in a year and your work is better than it was, the moment is the next annual review, renewal, or Monday.

The honest math

Suppose you raise rates 10 percent and lose one client in ten. The nine who stay now pay 10 percent more, which roughly replaces the lost income, and you have gained back that client's hours. Sold to a new client at the new rate, those hours are pure growth.

That is the real prize. Freelancers run out of hours before they run out of clients. A rate rise stops you selling your scarcest resource at an old price.
