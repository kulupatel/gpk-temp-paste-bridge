The Client Who Asks for Net 60 on a Small Invoice: How to Keep Small Invoices on Short Terms

The invoice is small, a few hundred at most, and the client asks for Net 60. It sounds reasonable. Sixty days on a small amount, what difference does it make? Plenty. Small invoices are where bad payment habits are cheapest for the client to form and most expensive for you to carry in aggregate. Ten small clients on Net 60 is a permanent two-month hole in your cash flow, filled with chasing, reconciling, and remembering who owes what from two months ago. The size of the invoice is not a reason to accept a long term. It is a reason to keep the term short, because a small invoice should be the easiest one to pay, not the one that waits the longest.

1. See why small invoices get the longest terms

Clients ask for long terms on small invoices precisely because the amount feels too small to argue about. Nobody wants a confrontation over a few hundred, so the client assumes you will agree, and most freelancers do. But the admin cost of an invoice does not shrink with its size. A small invoice still needs sending, tracking, reconciling, and chasing, and a sixty-day term doubles the window in which it can be forgotten, disputed, or buried under newer work. Worse, a long term on a small invoice teaches the client that your terms bend whenever the amount is small, and the next invoice, which may be much larger, will inherit the same expectation. Terms are set by precedent, not by invoice size. The first small invoice is where the precedent is made.

2. Answer with one standard term, not a negotiation

The most effective answer is a single standard term applied to every invoice, regardless of size. Something like: my standard term is 14 days on all invoices, so this one will be due on that date. Do not explain, do not apologise, and do not offer Net 30 as a compromise, because a compromise on a small invoice still doubles your wait and signals that the term is negotiable. A standard term is easier to hold than a custom one, because it is not personal. You are not refusing this client. You are applying the same rule you apply to everyone. Clients respect a standard term more than a negotiated one, because a standard term signals a business with systems, while a negotiated term signals a freelancer who can be moved. If the client genuinely needs more time, that is a conversation about scope or timing, not about rewriting your terms for one invoice.

3. Make small invoices easy to pay fast

Short terms work best when payment itself is frictionless. Put a payment link or bank details directly on the invoice, state the due date in plain words rather than a code like Net 14, and send the invoice the day the work is complete, not at the end of the month. Small invoices paid quickly are a habit, and habits are built by removing every obstacle between the invoice and the payment. If a client still asks for longer terms, offer a genuine alternative that does not move the due date: payment in advance for small fixed-price work, or a monthly retainer that bundles small jobs into one predictable payment. Both keep your cash flow regular without extending any single invoice. What does not work is accepting Net 60 and then chasing at day 61, because by then the invoice is two months old, the work is forgotten, and your reminder reads like a surprise.

4. Watch for the small-invoice pattern

A single Net 60 request on a small invoice is information, and it is worth recording. Sometimes it is a large company's standard process applied blindly to every supplier, and a short polite push gets you onto a faster track. Sometimes it is the first sign of a client who will treat every term as optional, and the small invoice is just the cheapest place to test you. Note the request, the date, and what you agreed, in the same place you track the invoice. If the same client asks again on the next project, you have a pattern, not a one-off, and the terms for that client should change: payment in advance, or a deposit before work starts. Patterns are only visible if you write them down. A freelancer who forgets last project's Net 60 request will grant this project's request as if it were the first time, and the client learns that long terms are simply how you work.

5. Write one term for every invoice into the next agreement

The lasting fix is a single line in your terms, agreed before work starts: all invoices, regardless of amount, are due within 14 days of the invoice date, and payment terms do not vary by invoice size. It sounds strict, and that is the point. When the term is written down and applied uniformly, the next Net 60 request answers itself, and you do not have to improvise a no over an amount that feels too small to argue about. Clients who run real businesses will not object to this line, because their own small suppliers invoice them the same way. Clients who object are telling you that they expect small suppliers to wait the longest, which is exactly the arrangement the line prevents. One sentence, agreed once, keeps every invoice, large or small, on the same short clock.

The pattern underneath

A long term on a small invoice feels harmless and functions like a permanent hole in your cash flow. See why small invoices attract the longest terms, answer with one standard term, make small invoices easy to pay fast, record the request as a warning sign, and write one term for every invoice into your next agreement. The goal is not to be difficult over small amounts. It is to keep your payment clock short and uniform, so that no invoice, however small, waits two months for money you have already earned.
