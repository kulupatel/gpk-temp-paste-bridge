The Client Who Adds a New Approver at Invoice Time: How to Close the Approval Loop Early

The project had one decision maker all the way through. You briefed with them, revised with them, delivered to them, and they approved the work. Then the invoice arrives, and suddenly there is another name: a finance manager, a partner, a director who "also needs to sign off." The new approver has questions you already answered, changes you already discussed, and a calendar that is full for two weeks. The invoice waits. This is rarely a conspiracy. It is usually a process the client never told you about, surfacing at the worst possible moment. The freelancer who closes the approval loop early never meets the surprise approver at invoice time, because they met them, or ruled them out, before the work started.

1. Ask who approves payment before you start, not after you finish

Add one question to every kickoff, and ask it plainly: "Besides you, who else needs to approve this work or release the payment?" Ask it in writing, in the kickoff email, so the answer is on the record. Most clients will name the finance contact right away, or confirm there is nobody else, and either answer saves you later. The clients who hesitate, or say "we will work that out later," are telling you the approval loop is open, and an open loop at kickoff becomes a stalled invoice at the end. If the answer is a name, add that person to the delivery and approval emails from day one. If the answer is "just me," you have a written record to point at when a new name appears. One question, asked early, prevents the most common invoice delay in freelance work: the approver nobody mentioned.

2. Get approval in stages, not in one big reveal

A single final approval is a single point of failure. Break it up. Approve the direction early, approve the draft midway, approve the final files before delivery. Each stage approval should be short and written: "Approved to proceed," "Approved with these two changes," "Approved for final delivery." When approval is staged, a late-arriving approver cannot reopen the whole project, because the earlier stages are already closed and documented. They can only comment on the final stage, and even that is bounded by what was already agreed. Staged approval also protects you when the original contact leaves or goes quiet: the record shows what was approved and when, and a new contact inherits decisions instead of remaking them. Freelancers who present finished work for one big approval hand the client a veto over everything. Freelancers who approve in stages hand the client a series of small yeses that are hard to undo.

3. Put the approver's deadline in the agreement

An approver without a deadline is a delay without an end. Your agreement should say how long approval takes: for example, approval within five working days of delivery, after which the work is treated as approved and the invoice is due. This is not aggressive. It is the same discipline every supplier uses, and reasonable clients accept it without argument, because it matches how their own suppliers work. The deadline does two jobs. First, it forces the client to bring the hidden approver into the process on time, because the clock is running. Second, it gives you a clean, non-personal reason to follow up: "the approval window closes on Friday," not "why have you not replied." If the client needs longer, they can ask for it, and you can agree to it, but the extension is then a conscious choice, not a silent drift. An approval loop with a deadline closes. One without a deadline just waits.

4. When the new approver appears, welcome them and bound them

Sometimes, despite all of this, a new approver still appears at invoice time. Do not fight them and do not restart the project for them. Welcome them with the record: a short email that lists what was approved, by whom, and when, and asks them for one thing only, the final sign-off, by a named date. "The direction was approved on 4 March, the draft on 11 March, and the final files on 18 March. Could you confirm final sign-off by Friday so finance can release payment?" This email is polite, but it does real work: it shows the new approver that the project is already decided, it limits their role to the last step, and it gives them a deadline. If they raise new changes, price them as new work, because that is what they are. A new approver who wants revisions to an approved project is not reviewing. They are commissioning. Treating their changes as a new scope, with a new fee and a new timeline, keeps the original invoice intact and the conversation honest.

5. Make the invoice itself approver-proof

The invoice is the last document in the chain, so make it carry the whole chain with it. Reference the agreement, the approval dates, and the approver's name on the invoice itself, or in the covering email: "As approved by [name] on [date], per our agreement of [date]." An invoice that tells its own story is much harder to stall, because any new approver who reads it sees that the work was already approved, and stalling it means questioning a decision their own colleague already made. Keep the covering email to three lines: what was delivered, when it was approved, and when payment is due. Attach the approval record if you have it. The invoice should never arrive alone, unexplained, into a finance inbox where nobody knows the project. It should arrive as the final page of a story everyone involved has already read.

The pattern underneath

A surprise approver is not a person problem. It is a process gap, and process gaps close early or not at all. Ask who approves payment before you start, approve the work in stages instead of one big reveal, put an approval deadline in the agreement, welcome any late approver with the record and bound their role to final sign-off, and make the invoice carry the approval story with it. The freelancer who closes the approval loop early is not the one who argues with finance at invoice time. They are the one whose kickoff email already named every approver, whose staged approvals already closed every stage, and whose invoice arrives as a formality, not a negotiation.
