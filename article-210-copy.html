The Client Who Asks You to Split the Invoice Across Two Months: How to Say Yes Without Losing the Deadline

The request sounds reasonable, and often it is: "Can you split this invoice across two months? Our budget sits in two periods." The work is done, the amount is agreed, and the client is not disputing a cent. They just want the same total on two pieces of paper with two dates. The danger is not the split itself. It is that a split quietly turns one clear deadline into two vague ones, and the second half becomes the part that drifts, gets re-approved, or disappears into the next quarter's budget fight.

A split is not a discount and it is not a delay, but it becomes both if you agree to it in a chat message with no terms attached. The client gets the accounting treatment they need, and you get a second invoice with no due date, no late fee, and no link to the first. Saying yes well means keeping the total, the dates, and the consequences identical to the single invoice you would otherwise have sent, just spread across two documents.

1. Agree to the split, not to a new deal

Start by saying yes to the shape and no to any change in substance. The total stays the same, the work covered stays the same, and the payment terms on each part are the same terms that applied to the whole. If your standard terms are payment within 14 days, then the first part is due 14 days after its date and the second part is due 14 days after its date, not 14 days after the client feels ready to approve the second part.

Put that in writing in the same message where you agree, so there is no later version of the conversation in which the split became a favour with no deadline. Something like: "Happy to split it: [amount] dated [date], due [date], and [amount] dated [date], due [date]. Both parts carry the same terms as the original invoice." One sentence now saves a chase later, because the client agreed to the dates when they asked for the split, not when you chased them for it.

2. Date the second part on purpose, not by default

The most common mistake is dating the second part whenever it suits the client, which usually means the end of the next month or the start of the next quarter. That turns a two-week payment into a six-week payment with your permission. Instead, date the second part at the earliest date that still gives the client the accounting split they need, and say why: "I can date the second part [date], which puts it in the next period and keeps its due date at [date]."

If the client needs a specific date for their books, use it, but keep the due date tied to that invoice date, not to their approval cycle. A second part dated the first of next month with a 14-day term is a real deadline. A second part dated "when finance approves it" is not an invoice at all. It is a promise to send an invoice later, and promises do not get paid.

3. Link the two parts so neither can float free

Each invoice should name the other. The first part should say "Part 1 of 2, total project fee [total], part 2 of [amount] dated [date] to follow." The second part should say "Part 2 of 2, total project fee [total], part 1 of [amount] dated [date] already issued." That cross-reference does three jobs: it stops the client from treating either part as the whole bill, it stops their finance team from paying one part and closing the file, and it gives you a single paper trail when you chase.

Keep the invoice numbers linked too, either as consecutive numbers or with a shared project reference on both. When you chase the second part, you chase it as part of a total that is already half paid, which is a much stronger position than chasing a standalone invoice that looks, to a finance team, like a new and optional bill.

4. Chase the second part on its own clock

A split invoice fails most often in the gap between the two parts. The first part gets paid, everyone relaxes, and the second part is forgotten until it is already late. Prevent that by treating the second part as its own invoice from the day it is issued: send it on the agreed date without waiting to be asked, send the same day-1 reminder you would send for any invoice, and escalate on the same ladder if it goes overdue.

Do not let the client reframe the second part as a favour you are doing them, because it is not. It is the second half of a fee for work already delivered, and chasing it on time is not pushy. If the client says the second part needs a fresh approval, answer with the agreement: the split was agreed, the dates were agreed, and the approval for the total happened when the project was signed. A fresh approval for the second half is a new condition, and new conditions do not get added after the work is done.

5. Decide, before the next project, when a split is worth it

Not every split is worth accepting. A split across two months for a long project with a good client is a small accommodation that costs you nothing if the dates are fixed. A split that pushes half the fee into a quarter you cannot see, for a client who has already paid late, is a delay wearing an accounting costume, and the honest answer is to offer a shorter alternative: a split across two weeks, or a deposit now with the balance on a fixed date.

Write your own rule for the next time, so you are not deciding under pressure. For example: splits are fine when both parts are dated within 30 days of each other, both carry the same terms, and the second part is issued on a date you set, not a date the client chooses later. Anything outside that rule gets a different answer: a single invoice on your normal terms, or a payment plan with a signed schedule. Rules like this are not rigidity. They are the reason a reasonable request stays reasonable.

The pattern underneath

A split invoice is an accounting convenience, and it stays one only while the total, the dates, and the terms stay exactly as they were on the single invoice. Agree to the shape, fix both dates in the same message, link the two parts on paper, chase the second part on its own clock, and set a rule for when a split is worth it at all. The client gets the two periods they need, and you get two deadlines that actually mean something.
