The Forgotten Invoice: How to Bill for Work You Delivered Months Ago

You finished the work three months ago. The client was happy, the files are delivered, and then life happened: new projects, deadlines, a holiday. The invoice never went out. Now every time you think about sending it, the awkwardness stops you. Surely it is too late. Surely they will think you are making it up.

They will not. An invoice sent late is still an invoice owed. Clients pay late invoices every day; accounting departments do not keep a calendar of your embarrassment. The only invoice that is guaranteed to go unpaid is the one you never send.

The awkwardness is yours, not theirs

Here is the mental shift: a late invoice is routine admin, not an accusation. Companies receive corrected, delayed, and reissued invoices constantly. Your client is not sitting there thinking "this invoice is three months late, what a nerve." They are thinking about their own backlog. Send the invoice as a normal business document, not as a confession, and it will be received that way.

Send it now, not after one more week

Every additional week of delay makes the invoice harder to send and easier to question. The details fade, the contact may change roles, the budget may close. The best time to send a forgotten invoice was the day after delivery. The second best time is today. Open the template, fill in the numbers, and send it before you close this tab.

How to frame the delay honestly

One honest line is enough. "Sending this through now, apologies for the delay on my end." That is it. No elaborate story about your schedule, no groveling paragraph, no discount offered out of guilt. A brief, factual acknowledgment of the delay, then a completely normal invoice. Over-explaining invites the client to treat the invoice as negotiable. Under-explaining, a single clean line, keeps it business as usual.

Keep your terms standard, even now

Guilt makes freelancers soften their own terms: longer payment windows, dropped late fees, apologetic tone throughout. Do not. The invoice should carry your normal terms, a normal due date (7 to 14 days), and your normal late fee clause. You did the work; the delay was administrative, not a reason to give the client a better deal. Quietly penalizing yourself is the most common mistake with forgotten invoices.

What if they question it

A reasonable client will just pay. If someone pushes back, stay factual: point to the delivery records, the email threads, the files sent. "This covers the [project] delivered on [date]; happy to resend the deliverables for your records." Evidence ends the conversation. If the contact who commissioned the work has left, ask their replacement to check with the team or pull the email history. Paper trails are why you keep them.

Is it too late legally

In most places, you have years, not weeks, to invoice for delivered work, but contracts can set shorter windows and tax years complicate backdated income. This is general information, not legal advice: check your contract's invoicing clause and, if the amount is large or the delay is over a year, confirm the position with an accountant. Do not let legal uncertainty be another excuse for inaction; in the vast majority of cases the invoice is simply due.

Build the never-again system

Forgotten invoices are a system failure, not a character flaw. The fix is small: an invoicing rule of same-day or next-business-day after delivery, no exceptions. Add a Friday sweep to your calendar: ten minutes to check that everything delivered that week has an invoice. And keep a simple delivered-but-uninvoiced list, even a single spreadsheet column, so nothing depends on your memory. The freelancers who never chase old invoices are not more disciplined; they just invoice before the work goes cold.
