The Client Who Wants to Pay in Crypto: Say Yes Without the Headache

A client asks: "Can I pay in crypto?" Your first instinct might be to say no and ask for a wire. But for international clients, crypto can settle in minutes while a wire takes days and eats $40 in fees. Saying yes is fine. Saying yes without rules is how you end up arguing about what the invoice was worth. Here is the clean way to accept crypto payments.

1. Agree on the coin before the invoice

The single biggest crypto payment dispute is volatility. You invoice $2,000, the client pays the equivalent in a coin that drops 8% before you convert it, and now you are short. Fix this before the invoice exists: agree in writing which coin you accept. Stablecoins (USDC, USDT) exist precisely for this; their value tracks the dollar, so the "what was it worth" argument disappears. If the client insists on a volatile coin, the agreement must say who bears the swing between invoice time and conversion time. One sentence in the terms: "Crypto payments are accepted in USDC at a 1:1 rate to the invoiced USD amount." Done. No argument possible.

2. Fix the rate moment in writing

Even with a stablecoin agreed, write down the moment the rate locks: the invoice date, the payment date, or the conversion date. For stablecoins this barely matters, but for any other coin it is everything. The cleanest rule: the amount due is the USD invoice amount, converted at the rate on the day the payment is sent, using a named source (Coinbase, Kraken, or CoinGecko). Name the source in the agreement. Two people checking "the price of ETH today" on two different sites will get two different numbers; naming one source ends that before it starts.

3. Confirm the wallet address twice

Crypto payments are irreversible. A wire sent to the wrong account can be recalled; crypto sent to the wrong address is gone. When a client is ready to pay, send your wallet address, then confirm it through a second channel: if they got it by email, confirm the first and last six characters on a call or chat. Never change a wallet address by email alone, because email compromise is exactly how payment-redirection fraud works. One client, one address, confirmed twice. This takes ninety seconds and prevents the only crypto mistake that cannot be undone.

4. Keep the tax record from day one

In most countries, receiving crypto as business income is a taxable event valued at the fair market price when received, and converting it later can trigger a second event. This is general information, not tax advice; your accountant gives the real rules. Your job is to make their job possible: record every crypto payment with the date, the coin, the amount, and the USD value at receipt, in the same log where you track every other invoice. A screenshot of the transaction plus one spreadsheet row is enough. The freelancers who get hurt by crypto taxes are not the ones who accepted crypto; they are the ones who accepted it and kept no records.

5. The one-line contract clause

Add this to your terms before the first crypto invoice: "Crypto payments accepted in [coin] only, at the [source] rate on the payment date, to the wallet address confirmed in writing. The USD invoice amount is due regardless of price movement after payment is sent." That last sentence is the whole clause. It says: once you send it, the payment is complete, and any swing after that is the sender's business, not a discount on my invoice. Clients who intend to pay fairly will sign it without blinking. The ones who wanted the volatility to work in their favor will suddenly prefer a wire, which tells you everything.

The pattern underneath

Crypto is just another payment rail with two extra risks: price movement and irreversibility. Name the coin, name the rate moment, confirm the address twice, keep the record. Four small rules, and the fastest-settling payment method your international clients have becomes the least dramatic one too.
