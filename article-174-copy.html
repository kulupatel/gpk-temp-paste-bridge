The Client Who Changes the Scope After Delivery: How to Price Post-Delivery Changes

The work is delivered, the files are sent, and then the client comes back with a new idea. Not a fix, not a correction, but a change: a new section, a different format, an extra version they now need. It sounds small, and it arrives in friendly language, as if it were part of the original job. It is not. A change after delivery is new work, and if you treat it as a favour, you will do it for free, and then do the next one for free, until your delivered projects never actually end. The problem is not the client's request. It is the missing boundary between what was delivered and what comes next.

1. Separate a fix from a change, in writing

The first job is to name what the request actually is, before you agree to anything. A fix corrects something that does not match the agreed brief: a wrong name, a broken link, a file in the wrong format. A change adds something that was never in the brief, or revises something the client already approved. Fixes are yours to make, promptly and without argument. Changes are new work, and new work gets a new price and a new date. Say so plainly, in the same message where you acknowledge the request: happy to do this, it sits outside the delivered scope, so here is the price and the turnaround. Clients rarely argue with this distinction when it is stated clearly, because they know the difference themselves. What they argue with is silence, because silence reads as agreement that the change is included.

2. Price the change before you start it

Never start a post-delivery change on the promise of sorting out payment later. Later never arrives, because once the change is delivered, the client's urgency disappears and your leverage with it. Give a short, specific price for the change itself, not an hourly estimate that invites a debate about how long it should take. A fixed price for a defined change is easy to approve and hard to dispute: this addition, this price, delivered by this date, paid on the same terms as the original project. If the client balks at the price, that is useful information. It tells you the change was not worth paying for, which is exactly why it was framed as part of the original job. A change the client will not pay for is a change you should not do.

3. Do not reopen the delivered project

The most expensive version of a post-delivery change is the one that reopens the whole project. The client asks for one extra section, you open the files, and suddenly there are three more tweaks, a revision to something approved weeks ago, and a new deadline for the whole package. Keep the delivered work closed. The change is a separate, small piece of work with its own brief, its own price, and its own delivery. If the change genuinely requires revisiting delivered work, say so, and price the revisit as part of the change. Delivered work that stays open is work that never gets paid for in full, because every invoice for it can be answered with one more request. Close the door on the original project in writing when you deliver it: this is delivered, this is what was included, and anything further is a new piece of work.

4. Record the change, even a small one

Small changes are the ones that cause the largest arguments, because nobody writes them down. A five-minute tweak, done as a favour, becomes the client's memory of how you work, and the next project inherits the expectation that tweaks are free. Record every post-delivery change, however small: the date, what was asked, what you charged, and whether it was paid. If you genuinely choose to do a tiny change for free, record that too, and say so: I will do this one at no charge, and future changes will be priced. A written record turns a favour into a one-off, instead of a precedent. It also protects you when the client later claims a change was included, because you can point to the delivery note and the change note, side by side, with dates.

5. Write a post-delivery clause into the next agreement

The lasting fix is one clause, agreed before work starts: work delivered and approved is closed, corrections to the agreed brief are included for a stated period, and any change after delivery is priced separately before it begins. Fourteen days is a reasonable corrections window, and it gives the client a fair chance to catch real errors without keeping the project open forever. When this clause exists, the next post-delivery request answers itself, and you do not have to improvise a boundary in the middle of a friendly conversation. Clients who run real businesses will not object, because they price their own post-delivery changes the same way. Clients who object are telling you they expect delivered work to stay open indefinitely, which is exactly the arrangement the clause prevents.

The pattern underneath

A change after delivery feels small and functions like an open-ended project that never gets paid for in full. Separate a fix from a change, price the change before you start it, keep the delivered project closed, record every change, and write a post-delivery clause into your next agreement. The goal is not to be rigid about small requests. It is to keep delivered work delivered, so that new work gets a new price, a new date, and the same respect as the work that came before it.
