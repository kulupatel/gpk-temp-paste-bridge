Getting Paid Across Borders Without Losing 5% to Fees

If you invoice international clients, you are probably losing money you never budgeted to lose. A $3,000 invoice can arrive as $2,850 after the payment platform's cut and the currency conversion spread, and most freelancers never notice because the fee is baked into a bad exchange rate rather than itemized on a bill.

Here is how to keep more of what you earned.

Rule 1: Quote in a currency, and own the choice

Decide your billing currency up front and state it in your terms: usually USD, sometimes the client's currency for big accounts. The important part is that it is explicit. Ambiguity lets the client pick the rate that suits them, which will never suit you.

If you quote in the client's currency, check the mid-market rate on the day you quote and add a small buffer for the 30 to 60 days until payment. Currencies move. Your quote should not absorb that risk for free.

Rule 2: Ditch the platform FX tax

PayPal's currency conversion is convenient and expensive: the spread plus the fee can total 3 to 4% against the mid-market rate. For a $5,000 invoice, that is $150 to $200 evaporated. A multi-currency account like Wise receives money in the client's currency and converts at the real rate with a transparent small fee, often under 1%.

The setup takes an hour. The savings are every invoice, forever. This is the single highest-ROI hour in international freelancing.

Rule 3: Say who pays the transfer fee, in writing

Someone always pays the transfer fee. If your terms are silent, it is you, deducted quietly before the money lands. Add one line to your payment terms: "Transfer and conversion fees are borne by the client; the freelancer receives the full invoiced amount." Clients paying cross-border teams are used to this; the surprise only exists when you never said it.

Rule 4: Match payment method to invoice size

Small invoices (under $500): eat the small platform fee and keep it simple; chasing the perfect rail costs more in time than it saves. Mid-size ($500 to $3,000): multi-currency account transfer is the sweet spot. Large ($3,000+): bank wire to your multi-currency account, and never let a large invoice ride on a platform that holds funds for "review."

Also know the review trap: some platforms hold large first-time international payments for days or weeks of "security review." Factor that into your cash flow calendar or route first payments through a cleaner rail.

Rule 5: Invoice timing matters more across borders

A cross-border payment takes 1 to 5 business days longer than a domestic one, every time, even when everything works. Invoice the day work is delivered, not the day you get around to it. Add those days to your late-fee clock honestly: the fee should trigger on when the money was due to arrive, not when you finally clicked send.

International freelancing is profitable exactly to the extent that you keep what you bill. One multi-currency account, one written currency line, one fee clause, and the leak is plugged for good.
