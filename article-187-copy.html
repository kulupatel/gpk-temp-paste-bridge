The Client Who Asks You to Invoice a Different Company Than the One You Worked For: How to Say No Without Losing the Work

It usually arrives as a casual request near the end of a project. The work is done, the invoice is ready, and the client says, almost as an afterthought, that the invoice should go to a different company name than the one you have been dealing with. Sometimes it is a sister company, sometimes a holding company, sometimes a company you have never heard of. Sometimes the reason sounds reasonable: a restructure, a new entity, a finance team preference. Sometimes no reason is offered at all. Either way, the request should stop you, because invoicing a company that did not commission the work changes who legally owes you money, and it can change whether you get paid at all.

The uncomfortable truth is that the company name on the invoice is not admin. It is the identity of your debtor. If you invoice the wrong entity and that entity refuses to pay, or goes bust, or claims it never engaged you, your real client can point at the invoice and say the debt was never theirs. Getting this right is not paperwork pedantry. It is the difference between a debt you can enforce and a favour you cannot collect.

1. Understand what is actually being asked

Before you answer, work out which of three situations you are in, because they call for different responses. The first is a genuine restructure: the business you worked for has changed its legal entity mid-project, and the new entity is willing to take over the contract in writing. The second is a convenience switch: the client wants the invoice routed through another company in their group for tax, accounting, or internal reasons, while the original company stays on the hook. The third, and the one to watch for, is a shell shift: the invoice is being moved to an entity with no assets, no trading history, or no connection to the work, which leaves you chasing a company that cannot pay.

You cannot tell these apart from the request alone, so ask two plain questions before you agree to anything. First, who exactly engaged you, and is that engagement being transferred in writing? Second, will the new entity confirm in writing that it accepts the full debt, including the agreed amount and terms? If the client cannot answer both clearly, you do not have a transfer. You have a risk, and you should say so.

2. Say no to a silent switch, and say it early

The worst version of this request is the one you only discover after the fact, when an invoice you sent to Company A gets queried by Company B, or when a payment arrives from an entity you never contracted with. So respond promptly, in writing, and keep the tone professional rather than suspicious. A message that works: you are happy to invoice the correct entity, but the invoice must go to the party that commissioned the work unless there is a written transfer of the contract, and you need that transfer before you reissue anything.

Do not reissue the invoice to the new name just to keep the peace, and do not backdate or quietly amend the original. A reissued invoice to a different entity, with no paper trail explaining why, is exactly the document a dispute will turn on. If the client pushes, explain the reason in terms of their interest too: a clean paper trail protects them as well, because their own accounts need to show a legitimate expense to a real supplier for real work. Most reasonable clients accept this once they hear it framed as protection rather than obstruction.

3. If the transfer is genuine, document it properly

A genuine entity change can be handled cleanly, but it needs three documents, not one. First, a short written transfer or novation: the original company, the new company, and you all confirm that the contract and the outstanding balance move to the new entity, with the amount and the payment terms stated. Second, a credit note cancelling the original invoice, so there is never a moment when two companies appear to owe the same money. Third, a fresh invoice to the new entity, dated the day it is issued, referencing the transfer.

This sounds formal, and for a small invoice it can be a single short email exchange that covers all three points, confirmed by all sides. The point is not legal theatre. It is that every party has said, in writing, who owes what to whom. If the new entity later refuses to pay, you hold a document signed by that entity accepting the debt, which is a far stronger position than an invoice you sent on a verbal instruction.

4. Watch for the red flags that change the answer

Some versions of this request should be refused outright, not negotiated. Refuse if the new entity is in a different country from the work and the client cannot explain why, because cross-border collection is expensive and slow. Refuse if the client asks you to invoice a company that did not exist when the work was done, unless a proper transfer covers the whole history. Refuse if the request comes with pressure to hurry, to skip the paperwork, or to keep the switch off the record. And refuse if the new entity is the client's personal company being used to absorb a business expense, because that can drag you into their tax problems without any benefit to you.

One flag deserves special attention: a client who wants the invoice reissued to a different entity after you have already chased the original for late payment. That is rarely an admin fix. It is often an attempt to reset the clock, move the debt somewhere harder to collect, or create a dispute about who owes what. In that case, hold the original invoice exactly where it is, keep chasing the original debtor, and only discuss a transfer once the arrears are addressed.

5. Fix it at the start of the next project

The best time to prevent this problem is before the work begins, and the fix is one line in your onboarding. When you take on a new client, confirm the exact legal entity you are contracting with: full registered name, and if you work with larger clients, the company number. Put that name on the proposal, the contract, and the first invoice, and if the client later asks to change it, your own documents already show what was agreed. Add a line to your terms that a change of invoicing entity requires written agreement from all parties, so the request itself triggers the process rather than a negotiation about whether a process exists.

This also protects you in a subtler way. Clients who intend to pay properly rarely object to confirming their legal entity. Clients who object, delay, or get vague when you ask are telling you something useful about how the payment conversation will go later, while you still have the chance to ask for a deposit or a shorter payment term.

The pattern underneath

The company name on an invoice is the identity of the person who owes you, and changing it without a written transfer moves your debt to a stranger. So ask who engaged you and whether the transfer is in writing, refuse a silent switch politely and promptly, document a genuine transfer with a written agreement, a credit note, and a fresh invoice, refuse outright when the flags point to a shell or a clock reset, and lock the legal entity down at the start of every project. You are not being difficult when you insist on this. You are being the kind of supplier whose invoices get paid, because there is never any doubt about who owes them.
