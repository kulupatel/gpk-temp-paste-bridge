The Client Who Wants Unlimited Revisions: How to Scope Revisions Before They Eat Your Fee

Every freelancer has met this client. The brief was clear, the work was approved, and then the revisions began. One round becomes three, three become six, and each round is presented as a small tweak: a different headline, a different colour, a different layout, a different direction entirely. The fee was for a project. The revisions have turned it into an open-ended relationship where you work for free and the client decides when, or whether, it ever ends. Unlimited revisions are not generosity. They are a scope failure, and the freelancer who scopes revisions before the work starts is the one who gets paid for every round after the agreed number.

1. Define a revision before the first one arrives

Most revision disputes are not about the number of rounds. They are about what counts as a round. To one side, a revision is a batch of changes on the delivered draft. To the other, it is every single comment, sent whenever, for as long as the project is open. Define it in writing before work starts: one revision round is one consolidated list of changes, delivered by an agreed date, on the current draft. Changes after that list are the next round. Changes to the brief itself, a new direction, or a new audience are not revisions at all. They are new scope, priced separately. This definition does two jobs. It stops the drip feed of one-comment-a-day revisions that never close a round, and it gives you a clean, non-personal way to say that a request falls outside the agreed rounds: it is not a refusal, it is the definition both sides accepted. Clients who hear this definition at kickoff almost always accept it, because it is reasonable, and because it protects them too: it guarantees their rounds are real, considered feedback, not a race against the clock.

2. Put a number on it, and a price on what comes after

A revision policy without a number is a wish. Two rounds included in the fee is the standard that works for most project work: enough for real refinement, few enough that the client takes each round seriously. State it plainly in the agreement, and state the price of additional rounds in the same sentence: two revision rounds included, further rounds at your hourly or per-round rate, quoted before they begin. The price matters as much as the number. Without a price, the third round is an awkward conversation. With a price, it is a simple choice the client makes, and clients who have to pay for a fourth round suddenly become very good at consolidating their feedback. Never leave the extra-round rate blank, and never quote it for the first time in the middle of a dispute, when it sounds like a penalty. Quoted upfront, it is a term. Quoted mid-argument, it is a threat. The number and the price belong together, in the agreement, before the first draft exists.

3. Make each round expire, or it never ends

A revision round with no deadline is a round that stays open forever, and an open round is how a project drifts for months. Give each round a window: feedback within five working days of delivery, for example, after which the draft is treated as approved and the project moves on. This is not about rushing the client. It is about keeping the project in the present, while the work is still fresh in everyone's mind and the feedback is still about the project, not about a new idea the client had six weeks later. When a round expires, the client can still request changes, but they are a new round, at the agreed rate, and that is fair to both sides. The expiry also protects your schedule: you are not holding a slot open indefinitely for a client who may return in two months with a full new direction. Projects with expiring rounds finish. Projects without them linger, and lingering projects are where fees quietly evaporate.

4. When the revisions change the brief, name it and reprice it

The hardest revision conversation is the one where the client is not really revising. They are changing the project: a new audience, a new message, a new deliverable dressed up as feedback. Calling this out is not confrontational if you do it with the record. Point at the original brief, point at the new request, and name the gap in one sentence: this changes the audience we agreed on, so it is a new scope, and here is the quote for it. Most clients, faced with that sentence, either accept the quote or withdraw the request, and both outcomes are fine. The ones who push back are usually testing whether the boundary is real, and a calm, written answer shows them it is. The mistake is to absorb a brief change silently, hoping the client notices your generosity. They will not notice, and the next change will be bigger. Every absorbed scope change trains the client to ask for the next one, and the fee you quoted for the original project ends up paying for two projects. Name the change, price it, and let the client choose.

5. Close the project in writing, even when the client goes quiet

Revision scope does not end when the work ends. It ends when someone says it ends, in writing. When the final round is delivered and the feedback window passes, send a short close-out note: the work is delivered, the revision rounds are complete, the final files are attached, and the final invoice is due on the stated date. If the client returns weeks later with more changes, those changes are new work, quoted fresh, and the close-out note is what makes that obvious instead of arguable. If the client never replies to the close-out, the note still did its job: it put a date on the end of the project, and it started the clock on the final payment. Freelancers who never close a project in writing end up with a portfolio of half-open engagements, each one capable of producing one more free round at any moment. A project that is closed in writing stays closed, and the next request arrives as what it is: a new engagement, with a new fee.

The pattern underneath

Unlimited revisions are never really about revisions. They are about a project with no edges: no definition of a round, no number, no price for more, no deadline, and no written close. Define a round before the first one arrives, put a number on it and a price on what comes after, let each round expire, name a brief change for what it is and reprice it, and close the project in writing. The freelancer who scopes revisions well is not the one who argues about feedback. They are the one whose agreement already answered every revision question before the client ever asked it, so the conversation stays about the work, and the fee stays attached to the work actually done.
