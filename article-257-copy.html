The Client Who Blames the Bank for Every Late Payment: How to Answer the Payment-Is-Stuck Story

There is a stall line that sounds more convincing than the others, because it blames a third party, and a third party cannot argue back: "The bank is holding the payment." Or: "The transfer is stuck." Or: "It is in transit, it should clear any day now." Or: "Our bank rejected it, there must be something wrong with your details." The message sounds technical, and patient, and reasonable, and it buys the client three to five days, every time, because the freelancer cannot see into the client's bank account, and cannot prove that the payment is not, in fact, stuck. But the bank story has a weakness, and it is the weakness of every story that involves a real system: real systems leave traces. Every transfer has a reference number, a value date, a sending account, a receiving account, and an amount, and a client who has genuinely sent a payment can produce those traces in a minute, and a client who has not sent a payment cannot produce them at all. The way through the bank story is to ask for the traces, politely, specifically, and once, and then to hold the client to the answer, because the proof either arrives, and the matter is settled, or it does not arrive, and the stall is exposed, and the chase resumes on the normal schedule.

The bank story works as a stall for four reasons, and it is worth naming them, because naming them is how you stop being fooled by them. The first reason is that it is unverifiable to the freelancer. You cannot log into the client's bank, and you cannot see whether a transfer was sent, so you have to take the client's word for it, and the client knows that. The second reason is that it sounds technical, and technical problems sound like real problems, and they sound like problems that are not the client's fault, which makes the freelancer feel unreasonable for chasing. The third reason is that it blames a third party, the bank, and a third party cannot be chased, and cannot be blamed, and cannot be hurried, which makes the delay feel like nobody's fault, and therefore like nobody's responsibility. And the fourth reason is that it buys time in a way that is hard to argue with, because "the bank is holding it" sounds like a fact, and arguing with a fact feels rude, and the freelancer who argues with it feels like they are accusing the client of lying, which is the feeling the stall is designed to produce. The answer to all four reasons is the same: ask for the proof, because proof is not an accusation, it is a request, and a client who has sent the money will be happy to provide it, and a client who has not sent the money will change the subject, and the change of subject is the answer.

1. The reply that ends it: ask for the traces, specifically

The reply to a bank story is not an argument. It is a request, and it is specific, and it names exactly what you need, so the client cannot answer with a vague reassurance, and so the freelancer can check the answer against their own records. The reply that does this sounds like this:

"Thank you for letting me know. So I can check on my side, could you please send me the transfer confirmation, or the reference number, the date the payment was sent, the amount, and the account it was sent from? My bank can trace a payment with the reference number, and once I have it I can confirm whether it is on its way, and when it should arrive."

Notice what that message does. It thanks the client, which keeps the tone professional, and it asks for five specific things: the confirmation, the reference number, the date, the amount, and the sending account. Each of those is a fact, and each of them is something a client who has sent a payment can provide in a minute, by forwarding the confirmation email, or copying the reference from their banking app, or taking a screenshot of the transaction. And the message explains why you are asking, which is that your bank can trace a payment with the reference number, which is true, and which makes the request sound like a practical step, not an accusation. And it promises a next step, which is that you will check, and confirm, and tell the client when the money should arrive, which shows that you are taking the matter seriously, and that you are not just chasing.

Notice what it does not do. It does not accuse the client of lying, because an accusation escalates the matter, and makes the client defensive, and gives them a reason to stop replying. It does not accept the story, because accepting it would restart the clock, and give the client another three to five days, and then another, and another. And it does not offer a discount, or a new deadline, or a concession, because the bank story is not a reason to change the terms, it is a claim about a payment, and the claim is either true, in which case the proof will show it, or it is not true, in which case the chase resumes.

The client's reply to that message tells you everything you need to know, and it tells you quickly. A client who sends the reference number, and the confirmation, and the date, has sent the money, or has tried to, and the matter is now a traceable payment, and the next section is the one to read. A client who replies with another vague reassurance, "it is definitely on its way, just give it a few more days," has not sent the money, or has not sent it yet, and the stall is exposed, and the chase resumes on the normal schedule, with a short deadline, and a clear statement that the invoice remains due. And a client who changes the subject, or who gets angry, or who stops replying, has also answered the question, by not answering it, and the freelancer should note that, in the thread, and move on.

2. What the proof tells you, and how to check it

A real bank transfer leaves a trail, and the trail has a shape, and knowing the shape is how you tell a real payment from a story about one. The confirmation email from the bank states the amount, the date, the reference number, and the receiving account, or at least the last few digits of it. The reference number, sometimes called a UTR, or a transaction ID, or a payment reference, is a unique string, and it is the key to tracing the payment, because your bank can look it up, and tell you whether a payment with that reference has arrived, or is on its way, or does not exist. The value date is the date the money left the client's account, and it tells you when the payment should arrive, because a domestic transfer usually clears the same day, or the next working day, and an international transfer usually takes two to five working days, and a payment whose value date was a week ago, and which has still not arrived, is a payment that has a problem, or that was never sent.

The first check is your own account. Before you reply to the client, check your bank, and your payment app, and your invoicing software, for the amount, and for the reference, and for the client's name, because payments do arrive under unfamiliar names, and through new payment methods, and against the wrong reference, and accusing a client of inventing a payment that actually cleared is a mistake you cannot easily undo. If the payment is there, match it, and say so, and close the matter. If it is not there, you now reply from certainty, not suspicion, and the certainty is the thing that makes the reply strong.

The second check is the reference itself. A real reference number has a format, a length, and a structure, and it is consistent with the bank and the payment method, and a client who provides a reference that is too short, or that does not match the bank's format, or that changes every time you ask, is a client who is inventing one. Ask your bank whether they can trace the reference, and tell the client that you have done so, in writing, because the fact that you checked is itself a message, and it tells the client that the story will be verified, and that vague answers will not be enough.

The third check is the screenshot, and it is the check that needs the most care, because a screenshot can be faked, and a faked screenshot is easy to make, and hard to spot. A genuine confirmation screenshot shows the bank's name, the transaction, the date, the amount, and the reference, and it is consistent with the rest of the story. A screenshot that is cropped so tightly that the bank's name is missing, or that shows a different amount, or a different date, or a reference that does not match the one the client gave you in writing, is a screenshot that should be questioned, politely, in writing: "Thank you for the screenshot. The reference here shows a different number from the one in your last message. Could you please confirm which one is correct, so my bank can trace it?" That question is not an accusation. It is a request for clarity, and a client with a real payment will clarify it, and a client with a fake one will change the subject.

And there is one more check, which is the simplest, and the most powerful: time. A domestic bank transfer that was sent a week ago has either arrived, or it has failed, and a failed transfer is returned to the sender, and the sender is notified, and the sender can tell you, in writing, that it failed, and why. An international transfer that was sent two weeks ago has either arrived, or it has been held for compliance, and a held transfer generates a message from the bank, and the client can forward that message. A payment that is "still in transit" after two weeks, with no reference, no confirmation, and no message from the bank, is not in transit. It was never sent, and the freelancer who waits for it is waiting for a payment that does not exist, and the time to stop waiting is now.

3. Give the bank story one short window, and then resume the chase

The bank story deserves one short window, and only one, because a genuine bank delay is real, and it is short, and a stall is indefinite, and the difference between them is the deadline. When the client says the payment is stuck, and provides a reference, or a confirmation, give them 48 hours, and say so, in writing, with a date, and a clear statement of what happens next. The message sounds like this:

"Thank you for the reference number. I have checked with my bank, and they cannot see the payment yet. I will check again on 18 March, and if it has not arrived by then, I will need to treat the invoice as unpaid, and resume the normal follow-up. If the payment arrives before then, please let me know, and I will mark the invoice as paid straight away."

Notice what that message does. It thanks the client for the proof, which shows that the proof mattered, and it states what you did, which is that you checked with your bank, and what you found, which is that the payment is not there. It gives a specific date, 48 hours away, for the next check, which is a short window, and it states what happens if the payment has not arrived, which is that the invoice is treated as unpaid, and the follow-up resumes, and it offers the client a way out, which is to let you know if the payment arrives, so the invoice can be marked as paid. And it does all of that without arguing, and without accusing, and without giving the client another week.

The mistake is to give the bank story an open-ended window, "let me know when it clears," or "take your time," or "no rush," because an open-ended window is a window that never closes, and a client who has discovered that "the bank is holding it" buys them a week will use it again, and again, and the invoice will drift, and the freelancer will have given away the one thing that gets invoices paid, which is a deadline. A deadline is not rude. It is a fact, and it is the fact that turns a story into a payment, because a client who knows the chase resumes on 18 March will either produce the proof, or pay, or admit the delay, and all three are better than another week of waiting.

And if the client comes back, after the window, with a new bank story, "the bank sent it back, I am resending it," or "it is stuck again, in a different way," treat it as a new claim, and ask for the new proof, and give it the same short window, once, and then stop accepting bank stories, and say so, in writing: "I have now given this two windows, and the payment has not arrived. From here, I need the payment itself, or a confirmation from your bank that it has been sent, with a reference number my bank can trace. Until then, the invoice remains overdue." That message is the line, and it is a fair line, because two windows is generous, and a third is not a window, it is a habit.

4. When the bank story is actually true

Bank delays are real, and a freelancer who treats every bank story as a lie will damage good clients, and lose the trust that matters, so it is worth knowing what a genuine delay looks like, and how it differs from a stall. A genuine delay has proof, and the proof arrives promptly, and it is consistent, and the dates line up, and the money arrives, and the client is as frustrated as you are, and tells you so, and keeps you updated, without being chased.

The genuine delays that actually happen are usually one of five. The first is the weekend and holiday cutoff, a payment sent on a Friday afternoon, or before a public holiday, that does not clear until the next working day, or the day after, and the client who tells you this is usually right, and the money arrives on Monday, or Tuesday, and the matter is closed. The second is the first-transfer compliance hold, a payment to a new payee, or a first payment to a new country, that the bank holds for a day or two while it checks the details, and the client is usually notified, and can forward the notification. The third is the wrong-details rejection, a payment sent to an old account, or with a transposed digit, that the bank rejects and returns, and the client is notified, and can tell you, and can resend it, correctly, once you confirm the details in writing. The fourth is the international correspondent delay, a payment that travels through one or two intermediary banks, each of which takes a day, and the client can provide the reference, and the payment arrives, late, but it arrives. And the fifth is the client's own bank's daily limit, a payment that exceeds the daily transfer limit, and is split, or delayed, and the client can tell you, and can send it in two parts, or raise the limit.

The way to tell a genuine delay from a stall is the proof, and the attitude, and the outcome. A genuine delay comes with a reference, and a confirmation, and a client who provides them without being asked twice, and who updates you, and who is frustrated, and who wants the payment to arrive as much as you do. A stall comes with a vague reassurance, and no reference, and a client who changes the subject when you ask, and who is never frustrated, only patient, and who wants you to wait. The freelancer who can tell the difference is the freelancer who keeps the good clients, and gets paid by the slow ones, and the difference is always the same: the proof, and the deadline, and the willingness to be checked.

5. The wrong-details variant: when the bank story blames you

There is a version of the bank story that blames the freelancer, and it is worth naming separately, because it is designed to make the freelancer feel responsible for the delay, and to make them do the work of fixing it. "The bank rejected it, there must be something wrong with your details." "Your account number is not working." "The transfer bounced, can you send your details again?" The message sounds like a technical problem, and it sounds like the freelancer's fault, and it is designed to make the freelancer apologise, and resend their details, and wait, and the cycle repeats.

The reply is simple, and it is the same discipline as the rest of this article: check, in writing, and ask for the proof. First, check your own details, and confirm, in writing, that they are correct, by copying them from the invoice, and from your bank, and stating them plainly, with the account name, the account number, and the sort code or IBAN, and the bank's name. Second, ask the client for the rejection notice, or the return message, from their bank, because a rejected payment generates a message, and the message states the reason, and the reason is either a wrong detail, in which case the message will say so, or something else, in which case the message will say that instead. And third, give the client one short window to resend the payment, with the confirmed details, and say so, in writing, with a date.

The message sounds like this:

"Thank you for letting me know. I have checked my details, and they are correct as shown on the invoice: [account name], [account number], [sort code or IBAN], [bank name]. Could you please forward the rejection notice from your bank, so I can see the reason given? Once I have it, I can confirm whether the details need changing. In the meantime, please resend the payment using the details above, and let me know once it is sent, with the reference number, so I can check on my side."

Notice what that message does. It states the details, plainly, so there is no ambiguity, and it asks for the rejection notice, which is the proof, and it gives a clear next step, which is to resend the payment, with the confirmed details, and to provide the reference. And it does not apologise for the details, because the details are correct, and it does not accept the blame, because the blame has not been proved, and it does not offer to change the details, because changing the details without proof is how a freelancer's payment gets sent to the wrong account.

If the client provides a rejection notice, and the notice shows a genuine error in your details, fix it, and say so, and thank the client, because a real error deserves a real fix. If the client cannot provide a notice, or the notice shows a different reason, or the client simply resends the payment, and it arrives, then the wrong-details story was a stall, and the freelancer should note that, and move on, and not let the same story work twice.

6. The traps that arrive with a bank story

A bank story rarely arrives alone. It usually arrives with a second request, bundled into the same conversation, and the second request is often the real one. There are three traps to watch for, and each one needs its own refusal.

The first trap is the different-account payment. "The bank is holding it, so I will pay you from a different account, or a different person will pay you." A payment from a different account, or a different person, is a payment that cannot be matched to the invoice, and cannot be traced, and creates a record problem, and the freelancer who accepts it will spend weeks explaining where the money came from. The answer is to refuse, politely, and to say that the payment must come from the client's account, to the account on the invoice, with the invoice number as the reference, and that a payment from anywhere else will be returned, or held, until it can be verified.

The second trap is the new-details request. "Can you send me your bank details again, by a different method, or to a different email address?" Bank details sent to a new address, or by a new channel, are bank details that can be intercepted, and the freelancer who sends them is taking a risk for no reason. The answer is to refuse, and to say that the details on the invoice are the correct ones, and that they will not be sent again, by any other method, and that the client should use the invoice.

The third trap is the fee shift. "The bank is charging a fee to release the payment, can you cover it, or split it?" A bank does not charge the recipient to release a payment, and a client who asks the freelancer to pay a fee to receive their own money is running a scam, or a mistake, and the answer is to refuse, plainly, and to say that the freelancer does not pay fees to receive payment, and that the client should ask their bank for the fee in writing, and forward it, and that the freelancer will look at it when it arrives. A fee that is real will have paperwork. A fee that is invented will not, and the freelancer who asks for the paperwork will find out which one it is.

7. Prevention, and the payment setup that stops the bank story before it starts

The best answer to a bank story is to never receive one, and the prevention is the payment setup, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the part that freelancers skip most often, and it is the part that would have stopped most bank stories before they were told.

The first prevention is a payment link on every invoice, a link the client can click, and pay, in a minute, without a bank transfer, and without a reference number, and without a story. A client who can pay by card, or by a payment link, has no reason to tell a bank story, because the payment is instant, and the confirmation is immediate, and the freelancer can see it, and mark the invoice as paid, in the same hour. The invoice that names only a bank transfer is an invoice that invites a bank story, because a bank transfer is slow, and opaque, and unverifiable, and the freelancer who offers only that method is offering the client a stall.

The second prevention is two methods, not one, so the client who genuinely cannot use the first method has a second one, and no excuse to wait. A payment link, and a bank transfer, with the details on the invoice, and a request to use the link first, and to tell you when they have paid, so you can confirm it. The third prevention is the same-day confirmation, a message in the thread, the same day a payment arrives, stating the amount, and the date, and the method, and the invoice it covers, so the client knows the payment arrived, and will not pay again, and will not tell a story about a payment that has already been received. And the fourth prevention is the check before every chase, a two-minute check of the bank account, and the payment app, and the invoicing software, before you send a reminder, because a payment that arrived yesterday, and has not been matched yet, is a payment that will be chased, and a chased payment is a payment that gets a bank story in reply.

And the last prevention is the simplest, and the most important: put the payment terms on the invoice, the due date, the payment methods, and the statement that the invoice is accepted as issued after 7 days, and ask the client to confirm receipt of the invoice, in the thread, when it is sent, so there is a record that the invoice was received, and the terms were seen, and the payment methods were known, and the bank story has nowhere to start.

The pattern underneath

A bank story is a stall that blames a third party, and its power comes from the fact that the freelancer cannot see into the client's bank account, and cannot prove that the payment is not stuck. The way through it is to ask for the traces, politely, specifically, and once, because every real transfer has a reference number, a value date, a sending account, and an amount, and a client who has genuinely sent a payment can produce those traces in a minute, and a client who has not sent a payment cannot produce them at all. Give the story one short window, 48 hours, with a date, and then resume the chase, because a genuine delay is short, and a stall is indefinite, and the difference between them is the deadline. When the story is actually true, it comes with proof, and a frustrated client, and money that arrives. When it is a stall, it comes with a vague reassurance, and no reference, and a client who changes the subject when you ask. Do not refund to a different account, do not resend your bank details by a new channel, and do not pay a fee to receive your own money, because none of those are real, and all of them are traps. And the prevention is a payment link on every invoice, two methods, a same-day confirmation, a check before every chase, and the terms on the invoice, because a freelancer who makes payment easy, and visible, and traceable, rarely hears a bank story, and a freelancer who hears one anyway has the proof request to answer it.
