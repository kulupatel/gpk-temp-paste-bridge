When Your Contract Has No Payment Terms: What to Do After the Fact

You shook on the work, started building, and now the invoice is due. Then you look at the contract and realize it says nothing about when you get paid, how you get paid, or what happens when payment is late. This is one of the most common freelancer traps, and it does not mean the money is gone. You can fix it after the fact. Here is the playbook.

Step 1: Invoice with terms attached, not terms buried

The moment work is delivered, send the invoice with the payment terms printed on the invoice itself: the exact due date (not "Net 30", a real calendar date), the payment methods you accept, and your late fee policy. An invoice that carries its own terms is a one-time repair for a contract that has none. Most clients will not even notice this is new; they will read it as the invoice being thorough.

Subject: Invoice #24 for [project], due [date]

>

Hi [name], attached is invoice #24 for [deliverables]. Payment is due by [date, e.g. Friday, 21 November]. I accept bank transfer and [payment link]. Late payments carry a [1.5% per month / flat $50] fee after 14 days past due. Let me know if anything on the invoice needs a second look.

Send the invoice and the terms in the same message. Terms that arrive separately look like an ambush. Terms printed on the invoice look like standard practice.

Step 2: Get one written line back

Silence on an invoice is not a fight, but it is not an agreement either. You want exactly one line of written confirmation that the client saw the terms and is fine with them. Reply to your own invoice thread the next day with a single sentence:

Just flagging that payment terms are on the invoice, due [date]. Reply "looks good" and I will mark it confirmed on my end.

Most clients reply "looks good" within hours. Save that reply. A short written confirmation of terms, combined with the work delivered and accepted, is the backbone of your paper trail if a dispute ever goes further. One line now prevents a three-week argument later.

Step 3: Send a one-page addendum for the rest of the engagement

If there is ongoing or upcoming work, do not let the original gap ride forward. Send a one-page contract addendum that covers the whole relationship from today onward: payment due dates, accepted methods, the late fee, what happens to deliverables if payment is late (work pauses), and kill-fee language for cancelled projects. Keep it to one page. Clients sign short documents; they stall on long ones.

Frame it as housekeeping, not punishment:

I am standardizing my payment terms across all clients this quarter, so here is a one-page addendum that covers the rest of our project. Same work, same rates, just the paperwork caught up.

Clients accept "I am doing this with everyone" far more easily than "I am doing this because of you."

What to do if they push back on the terms now

Some clients will say "we never agreed to late fees" or "our policy is Net 60." Here is how to respond without torching the relationship:

  • Concede the past, own the future. "Fair enough, I should have put terms in the original agreement. Going forward, these are the terms. This invoice is due [date]."
  • Trade, do not fold. If they want Net 60, offer Net 30 with a small deposit on the next phase. Never accept worse terms in exchange for nothing.
  • Put the limit in writing. If they insist on terms you cannot accept, email: "I understand your policy is Net 60. My terms are [X]. I will pause the next milestone until we agree on terms in writing." The pause is the point. Unpaid work continuing on disputed terms is how a late invoice becomes a lost one.

The prevention line for next time

The fix for this gap costs you thirty minutes on every future project: a short terms sheet sent with every quote, before any work begins. Due date on a real calendar date. Accepted payment methods. Late fee stated plainly. Work-pause clause. Five lines, attached to the quote, confirmed with a "looks good" before the kickoff call.

The work you already delivered still deserves payment. Send the invoice with terms today, get the one-line confirmation tomorrow, and the addendum before the next milestone. A missing contract clause is a gap in paperwork, not a gap in your right to be paid.
