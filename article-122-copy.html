The Credit Note Mistake: When and How to Reissue an Invoice Without Losing Your Place in Line

Sooner or later you will send an invoice with a wrong number on it: a typo in the amount, the wrong PO reference, a line item that should not be there. The instinct is to just fix it and resend. That instinct costs freelancers their place in the payment queue, because to the client's accounts payable department, your corrected invoice is a brand-new invoice, and brand-new invoices start their payment terms over. Here is how to reissue without losing your place.

The mistake almost everyone makes

You spot the error, edit the original PDF, and send it again with the same invoice number and a note: "Updated." Here is what happens on the client's side: their system either rejects it as a duplicate (same number already logged) or processes it as a new submission dated today. If it is treated as new, your Net 30 clock quietly restarts. An invoice that was due in six days is now due in thirty. You did the correcting; you also did the waiting.

The fix has two parts, and both matter: how you mark the correction, and how you protect the date.

Step 1: issue a credit note, then a new invoice

The correct sequence is: (1) issue a credit note against the original invoice number, which formally cancels it in both your books and theirs; (2) issue a corrected invoice with a NEW number. This is not bureaucracy. The credit note gives the client's AP team a document they can file against the original, which is how they close it out. The new number keeps your records audit-clean: one cancelled document, one live document, no duplicates, no gaps.

What the credit note must contain: your business name, the word "credit note," its own unique number, the date, the client name, the number of the invoice it cancels, and the amount credited. Keep the same numbering sequence as your invoices if you like, or run a separate CN sequence; either is fine as long as it is unique.

Step 2: protect your payment terms in writing

When you send the corrected invoice, include one sentence that preserves your original due date: "This corrected invoice replaces 2026-014; the original payment terms and due date of October 16 apply." This is the line that keeps your place in the payment queue. Without it, the corrected invoice is processed on its own submission date and you silently accept a longer wait.

Most clients will accept this without argument, because their AP team cares about matching documents, not about extending your wait. The clients who push back on the original due date are telling you something useful: they were looking for an excuse to reset the clock. Now you know, and you handle them like every other slow payer: firm, dated, documented.

When you do NOT need a credit note

Not every correction needs the full sequence. Small rule of thumb: if the client's AP department has not logged the invoice yet (you caught the error within hours, before anyone confirmed receipt), you can usually cancel-and-replace informally with an email: "Please delete 2026-014 and use 2026-015 instead; same terms, due date October 16." The credit note matters when the wrong document is already in their system. When in doubt, do the formal version; it costs you five minutes and it always works.

The one thing to never do

Never edit the amount on an invoice that is already in dispute. If a client is challenging what they owe, the invoice is now evidence. Changing it mid-dispute undermines your position, even when you are correcting an honest error. In a dispute, issue the credit note and the corrected invoice as separate, dated documents, and keep every version. The paper trail is your leverage; keep it intact.

A numbering system and a clean reissue process sound like accounting drudgery until the month they save you a payment cycle. Build both now, while nothing is on fire.
