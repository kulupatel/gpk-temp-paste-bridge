The Client Who Will Not Pay Until the Project Launches: How to Get Paid for Work That Is Already Done

The work is done, approved, and invoiced. Then the client tells you they will pay when the project launches. The website is built, the copy is written, the designs are approved, the code is delivered, and the launch is next month, or next quarter, or when marketing is ready, or when the product ships, and the payment waits with it, because the client has decided that the launch, not the delivery, is the moment the money is owed, and a freelancer who accepts that decision has handed the client the calendar, and the calendar is the one thing the client controls completely, because a launch can be delayed by a week, or a month, or a quarter, or forever, and every delay is somebody else's fault, and the freelancer is the only person in the arrangement who is not being paid while the launch waits.

The first thing to understand is that the launch is not a payment event, and a freelancer who treats it as one has confused two different things. The launch is the client's business decision, about their timing, their market, their readiness, and their risk, and it belongs to the client, and the client can move it, delay it, shrink it, or cancel it, and none of that changes what the freelancer did, because the freelancer did the work, delivered the work, and had the work approved, and the invoice is for the work, not for the launch, and the work is finished whether the launch happens on Tuesday, or in March, or never, and a client who will not pay until launch is a client who is asking the freelancer to finance the client's launch, interest free, for an unknown length of time, and the freelancer who agrees is a freelancer who has become the client's bank, and the bank does not get to choose when the loan is repaid, because the client does.

The second thing to understand is why the launch works so well as a stall, because it works for four reasons at once, and each one is worth naming, because naming them is how a freelancer stops being fooled by them. The first reason is that the launch sounds reasonable, and it sounds reasonable because it sounds like a milestone, and milestones sound fair, and a freelancer who hears "we pay on launch" hears something that sounds like a normal business arrangement, and it is, in the narrow sense that a milestone payment is a real thing, and the mistake is in thinking that the launch is a milestone in the freelancer's work, when it is a milestone in the client's business, and the freelancer's work ended at delivery, and the launch is not the freelancer's milestone at all. The second reason is that the launch is unverifiable from the freelancer's side, because the freelancer cannot see the client's launch plan, cannot check whether marketing is ready, cannot know whether the delay is real or invented, and cannot prove that a launch that was meant to happen in March is still meant to happen in March, and a stall that cannot be checked is a stall that cannot be argued with, and that is why clients choose it. The third reason is that the launch is movable without a conversation, because a launch date is a plan, and plans change, and a client who moves a launch by three weeks does not feel they have done anything to the freelancer, because they have not changed the deal, they have only changed their own plan, and the freelancer is expected to wait, because the plan changed, and the freelancer was never consulted, and the freelancer is not owed a consultation, because the launch is the client's business, and that is the point, and that is also the problem. And the fourth reason is that the launch can be redefined, because a launch can be a soft launch, or a phased launch, or a beta, or a quiet release, or a launch to one customer, and each redefinition moves the payment again, and the freelancer who accepted "we pay on launch" has accepted a trigger that the client can redefine at will, and a trigger that can be redefined at will is not a trigger at all, it is a permission to wait.

1. The reply: the work is done, the invoice is due

The reply to "we pay on launch" is short, factual, and unmoved, because the reply is not an argument about the launch, and it is not a negotiation about the date, and it is not a discussion of the client's business, and it is a statement of the facts, and the facts are the work, the approval, the invoice, and the date, and the reply says them, in that order, and then stops, because every extra sentence is a new thing for the client to argue with, and the freelancer who argues about the launch has already lost, because the launch is the client's ground, and the work is the freelancer's ground, and the reply should be fought on the freelancer's ground, and the freelancer's ground is the work.

The reply looks like this. "The work was delivered on 12 February and approved on 14 February. Invoice 47 is for 1,800, due 28 February. The payment date does not move with the launch, because the invoice is for the work, which is complete. Please confirm the payment will be made by 28 February." That is the whole reply, and it does four things, and each one matters. It states the delivery date and the approval date, which are facts the client cannot dispute, because the client approved the work, in writing, on a date, and the approval is the proof that the work is done. It states the invoice number, the amount, and the due date, which are facts that come from the invoice itself, and the invoice is the document, and the document says what it says, and the client has it. It states the rule, plainly, that the payment date does not move with the launch, because the invoice is for the work, which is complete, and the rule is stated as a fact, not as a request, and not as a negotiation, and a rule stated as a fact is harder to argue with than a request, because a request can be declined, and a fact can only be disputed, and the client cannot dispute this fact, because the work is complete. And it asks for a confirmation, with a date, which gives the client one clear action, and one clear deadline, and a client who is given one clear action and one clear deadline has no room to answer with vagueness, because vagueness does not confirm anything, and the freelancer who receives a vague answer knows the answer is not a confirmation, and can say so, and can repeat the request, and can keep repeating it, because the request is reasonable, and the date is the date on the invoice, and the invoice is the document, and the document says what it says.

The reply must not do four things, and each one is a trap, and the traps are worth naming before the reply is sent, because the traps are tempting, and a freelancer who falls into one of them has given the client exactly what the client wanted. The reply must not ask when the launch is, because asking when the launch is accepts that the launch matters, and the launch does not matter, and a freelancer who asks about the launch has agreed, without meaning to, that the launch is the trigger, and the client will answer with a date, and the date will move, and the freelancer will ask again, and the cycle will repeat, and the freelancer will have become the person who waits for the launch, and that is the position the client wanted them in. The reply must not offer to help with the launch, because offering to help with the launch accepts that the launch is the freelancer's problem, and it is not, and a freelancer who offers to help with the launch has volunteered to work, unpaid, on a project that is already delivered, and the client will accept the help, and the launch will still move, and the freelancer will have worked for free, and still not been paid. The reply must not apologise for chasing, because there is nothing to apologise for, and the invoice is due, and the work is done, and the client is the one who moved the payment, and the freelancer who apologises has accepted blame for a problem they did not create, and a client who receives an apology hears a concession, and a concession is a step towards accepting the new date. And the reply must not offer a discount to speed the launch, because a discount to speed the launch is a payment for the client's own delay, and the client who is offered a discount for paying on time learns that delaying payment earns a discount, and the lesson will be applied to every future invoice, and the freelancer who teaches it will pay for it, again and again.

2. Why the launch is the perfect stall

The first property is that the launch is a future event, and a future event cannot be disproved today, because today the launch has not happened, and the client can say it will happen next month, and the freelancer cannot prove it will not, and the freelancer cannot prove it will, and the client is asking the freelancer to wait for something that has not happened, and the waiting has no end, because the future keeps arriving, and the launch keeps not happening, and the freelancer who waits for a future event is a freelancer who has no way to know when the waiting ends, and no way to prove that it should have ended already, and that is why the launch is chosen, because it cannot be checked, and it cannot be argued with, and it cannot be disproved, and the only answer to it is to refuse it, and to refuse it on the grounds that the payment is not tied to it, because the payment is tied to the work, and the work is done.

The second property is that the launch belongs to the client, and what belongs to the client is controlled by the client, and what is controlled by the client can be moved by the client, without notice, without consultation, and without consequence, and the freelancer who ties payment to the launch has tied payment to something they cannot see, cannot check, and cannot influence, and that is the worst possible trigger for a payment, because a payment trigger should be something the freelancer can verify, and the freelancer can verify delivery, and can verify approval, and can verify the date on the invoice, and cannot verify a launch, because a launch is a plan, and a plan is private, and the freelancer is not in the room where the plan is made, and the freelancer who accepts a private plan as a payment trigger has accepted a trigger they cannot see, and a trigger they cannot see is a trigger that can be moved in the dark, and the freelancer will never know, until the payment does not arrive, and by then the launch has moved again, and the reason is a new one, and the cycle continues.

The third property is that the launch is sympathetic, and a sympathetic stall is harder to answer than a hostile one, because a hostile stall can be answered firmly, and a sympathetic stall invites understanding, and the client who says "we pay on launch" is not shouting, and is not threatening, and is not lying, and is, in many cases, telling the truth about their own plan, and the plan really is to launch in March, and the client really does intend to pay then, and the freelancer who hears the truth in the client's voice feels unreasonable for insisting on the invoice date, and that feeling is the trap, because the feeling is real, and the invoice is also real, and the two do not cancel each other out, and a freelancer can understand the client's plan, and still insist on the invoice date, because understanding a plan is not the same as accepting it as a payment trigger, and the client who is understood, and still held to the date, is a client who learns that sympathy does not move due dates, and the freelancer who learns to be sympathetic and firm at the same time is a freelancer who gets paid, because the two are not opposites, and the freelancer who thinks they are will choose sympathy, and will wait, and will not be paid.

And the fourth property is that the launch is infinitely divisible, because a launch can be split into phases, and each phase can be a launch, and the soft launch can be followed by the full launch, and the full launch can be followed by the public launch, and the public launch can be followed by the relaunch, and each one can be the launch that the payment waits for, and the client who splits the launch has split the trigger, and the freelancer who accepted the first split will be asked to accept the second, and the second will be followed by the third, and the payment will wait for a launch that keeps arriving in pieces, and never arrives whole, and the freelancer who notices the splitting, early, and names it, and refuses it, is the freelancer who stops the cycle, because the cycle only works while it is unnamed, and a cycle that is named, plainly, in writing, in the thread, is a cycle the client cannot continue without admitting what it is, and most clients will not admit it, and will pay, rather than admit it, and that is the point of naming it.

3. The three versions of the message, and the reply to each

The message arrives in three versions, and each one needs its own reply, because each one is a different claim, and a freelancer who answers all three with the same reply will miss the claim, and the client will feel unheard, and the conversation will stall, and the payment will stall with it, and the reply that answers the claim, specifically, is the reply that moves the payment, because it shows the client that the freelancer heard them, and is not moved by them, and the two together, heard and unmoved, are the combination that gets paid.

The first version is the delayed launch. "The launch has moved to next quarter, so the payment will move with it." The reply states the facts, and holds the date, and does not ask about the new launch date, because the new launch date does not matter, and the reply says so, without saying it rudely, and the reply looks like this. "The launch moving does not move the invoice. The work was delivered and approved in February, and invoice 47 is due 28 February. Please confirm payment by that date." The reply is short, because the claim is simple, and the answer is simple, and the freelancer who writes a long reply to a simple claim has given the client more to argue with, and the client will argue with the extra sentences, and the extra sentences will become the new conversation, and the invoice date will be forgotten, and the freelancer who keeps the reply short keeps the invoice date in the centre, and the invoice date is the only thing that matters.

The second version is the launch that happened, but not really. "We did a soft launch, but the real launch is next month, so the payment waits for the real one." 4. The traps: accepting the trigger, releasing everything, waiting silently, discounting to hurry

There are four traps in a launch-tied payment, and each one is tempting, and each one costs the freelancer money, and each one is worth naming, because a trap that is named is a trap that is easier to avoid, and a freelancer who avoids all four has kept the payment on the work, where it belongs.

The first trap is accepting the trigger, even once, even politely, even in a single sentence, because accepting the trigger once makes it the deal, and the deal, once accepted, is hard to unaccept, and the client who hears "okay, we can wait for the launch" has heard an agreement, and will remember it, and will rely on it, and will move the launch, and will expect the freelancer to wait, and the freelancer who accepted it once will find it very hard to unaccept it later, because the client will say, correctly, that the freelancer agreed, and the freelancer did agree, and the agreement is the problem, and the way to avoid it is to never agree, not once, not politely, not in a single sentence, and to answer the first mention of the launch with the facts, and the date, and nothing else, because the facts and the date are the answer, and the answer does not need a concession.

The second trap is releasing everything for the launch, because the client who is waiting for the launch will often need something for the launch, and the something will be the finished files, or the admin access, or the source code, or the final assets, and the client will ask for them, with a reasonable-sounding reason, and the reason will be that the launch cannot wait, and the launch really cannot wait, and the freelancer who releases everything for a launch that has not been paid for has given away the only leverage they have, because once the client holds the finished work, the invoice is no longer the price of receiving the work, because the work has already been received, and chasing payment after delivery is a completely different negotiation from collecting payment before release, and the freelancer who holds the final files, the admin access, and the handover until the invoice clears is the freelancer who keeps the leverage, and the leverage is the work, and the work is the payment, and the two move together, and the freelancer who separates them has given one away for free.

The third trap is waiting silently, because the freelancer who accepts the launch trigger, and then waits, silently, for the launch, has given the client the gift of silence, and silence is interpreted as agreement, and the client who hears nothing for six weeks assumes the freelancer has accepted the wait, and the launch moves, and the freelancer hears nothing, and waits longer, and the silence grows, and the payment recedes, and the way to avoid it is to chase on the invoice schedule, not on the launch schedule, because the invoice schedule is the schedule that matters, and the reminders go out on day one, day seven, day fourteen, and day thirty, exactly as they would for any late invoice, because the invoice is late, and the launch is not the reason, and the reminders are the proof that the freelancer has not accepted the wait, and the reminders are the record that the payment was chased, on time, on the invoice's own dates, and the client who receives the reminders knows the freelancer is not waiting, and the freelancer who sends them knows they are not waiting either, and the two together, the reminders and the record, are what keep the payment alive.

And the fourth trap is discounting to hurry the launch, because the client who is offered a discount for paying before the launch learns that delaying payment earns a discount, and the lesson is applied to every future invoice, and the freelancer who offers it has paid the client to pay, and the payment that arrives is smaller than the invoice, and the invoice was for work that is already done, and the work is worth what it was quoted at, and the discount is a gift to a client who has not paid, and a gift to a client who has not paid is a gift that teaches the client to wait, and the freelancer who wants to keep the relationship warm should offer something that costs little and does not touch the price, a short check-in after launch, a note on how to use what was delivered, priority booking for the next piece of work, and these gestures show goodwill without moving the number, and a price cut is never free, because it is remembered at the next quote, and the next quote starts from the discounted number, not the original one.

5. When it is genuinely true, and how to tell

Sometimes the launch really is the payment trigger, and a freelancer who treats every launch mention as a stall will damage a genuine arrangement, and the difference matters, and the difference is in the agreement, and the agreement is in writing, before the work starts, and the freelancer who checks the agreement, before answering, is the freelancer who answers correctly, because the answer depends on what was agreed, and what was agreed is the only thing that matters.

The genuine version looks like this. The test has three parts, and all three must pass, and if any one fails, the launch is a stall, and the freelancer should answer it as a stall. The second part is the definition: is the launch defined, with a date, or a clear event, such as the website going live on a named domain, or the product shipping to customers, and if the launch is undefined, or is defined as "when we are ready", or "when marketing says so", or "when the time is right", then the trigger is not a trigger, it is a permission to wait, and the freelancer should say so, by stating the facts, and the date, and nothing else. And the third part is the proportion: is the launch-tied part a defined final part of the fee, such as the last twenty percent, with the rest already paid on delivery, or is the whole invoice, or the whole fee, waiting for the launch, and if the whole fee waits, then the freelancer has financed the entire project, and the financing was never agreed, and it is a stall, and the freelancer should say so, by stating the facts, and the date, and nothing else.

A client who passes all three tests, who has the trigger in writing, with a defined launch, for a defined final part of the fee, is a client with a genuine arrangement, and the freelancer should honour it, and should ask, politely, for the launch date, and should diary it, and should chase it, on that date, and should also, for next time, price the wait into the quote, because waiting is a cost, and a cost that is not priced is a cost the freelancer pays, and the freelancer who prices it, openly, in the quote, is a freelancer who can afford to wait, because the wait was agreed, and was paid for, and the payment will arrive, on the launch, because the launch is defined, and the date is known, and the client is genuine, and the arrangement is real, and the freelancer who can tell the difference, between a term and a stall, is a freelancer who honours the terms, and refuses the stalls, and gets paid, in both cases, on time.

6. Prevention, and the invoice that cannot be tied to a launch

The best answer to "we pay on launch" is to never receive it, and the prevention is in the agreement, before the work starts, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the part that freelancers skip most often, and it is the part that would have stopped most versions of this message before they were sent.
