The Client Who Never Signed the Contract, Then Disputes the Terms: How to Hold the Line Without a Signature

It starts the way most freelance projects start: quickly. A client messages on a Tuesday, the work is urgent, the deadline is Friday, and the contract is sent with a note that says "have a look and sign when you get a chance". The freelancer, who wants the work and wants to be helpful, starts on Tuesday, delivers on Friday, and sends the invoice. And then the client replies, not with a payment, but with a dispute: "I never agreed to a late fee." Or "I never agreed to those payment terms." Or "I never agreed to that price." And the freelancer looks at the contract, and the signature line is blank, and the client is right, in the narrowest sense: the contract was never signed.

The unsigned contract is one of the most common problems in freelance work, and one of the most misunderstood. Most freelancers believe that a contract without a signature is worth nothing, and most clients who dispute terms believe the same thing, and both of them are wrong in the same way. A signature is one form of agreement. It is not the only form. An agreement can also be made in an email, in a message, in a voice call that is followed up in writing, in the acceptance of an invoice, in the payment of a deposit, or in the act of accepting the work itself. The law, in most places freelancers work, recognises all of these as agreement, provided the terms were clear, and provided the client had a chance to see them, and provided the client's behaviour shows they accepted them. A signature is the clearest proof of agreement. It is not the only proof, and it is not the only agreement.

That does not mean the freelancer is safe. A freelancer with an unsigned contract is in a weaker position than a freelancer with a signed one, because the argument is harder, and the evidence is messier, and the client has a plausible story to tell. But weaker is not defenceless, and the freelancer who knows what counts as agreement, and who has kept the record, can hold the line on the terms, calmly, and usually successfully. The key is to stop arguing about the signature, and start pointing to the record.

1. Why the contract never got signed, and why that matters less than you think

Contracts go unsigned for the same reasons in almost every case, and none of them are mysterious. The work was urgent, and the client needed it started before the paperwork could be finished. The client is a busy person, and signing a document is one more task on a list that never gets shorter. The freelancer sent the contract and never followed up, because following up felt awkward, and the work was already underway, and it seemed rude to stop and ask for a signature. The client said "looks fine, let us get going", and the freelancer took that as agreement, which, in most cases, it was. Or the client deliberately avoided signing, because an unsigned contract is a contract they can dispute later, and they knew it.

The reason matters, because it decides how to handle the dispute. A client who never signed because they were busy, and who has paid every invoice on time, and who is now disputing one term, is a different problem from a client who never signed because they planned to dispute the terms all along. The first client needs a calm reminder of what was agreed, and the second needs a firm statement of the record, and a pause on further work, and the freelancer who treats them the same way will either damage a good relationship, or give a bad client another month of free work.

But whatever the reason, the freelancer's position is the same in one important respect: the absence of a signature does not erase the agreement. It makes the agreement harder to prove, and it makes the conversation longer, and it makes the freelancer's job more difficult. It does not make the terms disappear. The terms were sent, and the client saw them, or had the chance to see them, and the client went ahead with the work, and accepted the deliverables, and, in most cases, paid at least one invoice under those terms. That is an agreement, even without a signature, and the freelancer who understands that can answer the dispute with confidence, instead of apologising for a missing signature.

There is a useful question to ask yourself, before you reply to the dispute, and it is this: if the client had signed, would the terms be any different? The answer is almost always no. The terms are the same terms, in the same document, with the same words. The signature would have made the agreement easier to prove. It would not have changed what was agreed. And that is the point to hold on to, because the client who says "I never signed" is trying to make the conversation about the signature, and the freelancer's job is to move it back to the terms, and to the record that shows the client accepted them.

2. What counts as agreement without a signature

The record is the whole case, and it is worth knowing what counts, before you need it.

The strongest piece of evidence, after a signature, is a written confirmation. An email from the client that says "looks good, let us go ahead", or "agreed", or "proceed", in reply to a message that included the terms, or linked to them, or attached them. A message that says "I accept the terms" is an agreement. A message that says "let us get going" after the terms were sent is an agreement. A message that says "sounds good" in reply to a message that set out the price, the payment terms, and the late fee, is an agreement to all three. The words do not have to be formal. They have to show that the client saw the terms, and went ahead.

The second strongest piece of evidence is conduct. A client who pays a deposit, under terms that say a deposit is non-refundable, has accepted the deposit term. A client who pays the first invoice, under terms that say payment is due within 14 days, has accepted the 14-day term. A client who accepts the deliverables, and uses them, and asks for revisions, under terms that say the work is owned on payment, has accepted the ownership term. Conduct is agreement, because a client who did not agree to the terms would not have paid under them, or accepted work under them, or carried on under them. The client who says "I never agreed to a late fee" after paying two invoices that were each sent with a late-fee term, and after the late fee was mentioned in the first chase, has a difficult argument to make, because their own behaviour says otherwise.

The third piece of evidence is the invoice itself. An invoice that states the payment terms, the due date, and the late fee, and that is sent to the client, and that the client does not dispute, is a record. A client who receives an invoice that says "payment within 14 days, late fee of 1.5% per month applies after the due date", and who says nothing, and who then pays late, and is then charged the fee, cannot honestly say they never knew about the fee. They knew, because it was on the invoice, and they had the chance to dispute it, and they did not. Silence is not the strongest form of agreement, and it is not enough on its own, but combined with a contract that was sent, and work that was accepted, and payments that were made, it is part of a record that is hard to argue with.

The fourth piece of evidence is the message thread. A freelancer who discussed the price in messages, and the payment terms in messages, and the late fee in messages, and who has the client's replies in the same thread, has a record that is often clearer than a contract, because it shows the conversation, in the client's own words, in order. The thread is the agreement, in the client's own voice, and it is the piece of evidence that most freelancers already have, without realising it, because they have been keeping it all along.

None of this is legal advice, and a freelancer with a large sum at stake should get proper legal advice, in their own jurisdiction, before relying on any of it. But for the ordinary dispute, over an ordinary invoice, the record is usually enough, and the freelancer who knows what counts can answer with confidence, instead of folding because a signature line is blank.

3. The reply that holds the line

The reply to "I never signed the contract" should not argue about the signature. It should not say "you should have signed", because that is a lecture, and lectures make people defensive. It should not apologise for the missing signature, because an apology accepts the client's framing, and the framing is that the terms do not apply. It should point, calmly, to the record, and state the terms, and ask for the payment.

The message sounds like this:

"I understand the contract was not signed, and I am happy to get a signed copy in place for the next project. For this project, the terms were sent on 3 March, before the work started, and you replied on 4 March saying 'looks good, let us get going', and the invoice sent on 10 March stated payment within 14 days with a late fee after the due date. The invoice is now 21 days overdue. Could we settle it this week, and I will send a fresh contract for signature before we start the next piece of work?"

Notice what that message does. It acknowledges the missing signature, without conceding that it matters, which keeps the tone friendly. It states the facts, with dates, which shows that you have a record, without turning the message into an argument. It quotes the client's own words, "looks good, let us get going", which is the strongest move in the whole reply, because the client cannot dispute their own words, and the words show acceptance. It states the terms, and the current position, and it asks for the payment, and it offers a way forward, a signed contract for the next project, which gives the client a face-saving route to agreement, without giving away anything on this invoice.

If the client replies that they did not read the terms, or did not see them, or did not understand them, the answer is the same shape: acknowledge, state the fact, offer the record, move forward. "The terms were sent on 3 March, in the same message as the quote, and the invoice repeated the payment terms and the late fee. I am happy to send you copies of both. Could we settle this invoice now, and then agree the terms for the next project in writing, before it starts?" The not-read claim is worth checking, once, because sometimes it is true, and a freelancer who buried the terms in a long document has a real problem to fix. But a not-read claim that arrives after the work is delivered, and after the invoice is overdue, and after the client has already used two other explanations, is not a problem to fix. It is a reason to keep the conversation on the payment.

There is one more thing the reply should do, and it is the thing that most freelancers forget: it should stop the work. A client who is disputing the terms of the current project should not be given a new project, or a new phase, or a new deliverable, until the dispute is settled, because every new piece of work is a new piece of work under disputed terms, and the freelancer who keeps working is the freelancer who keeps adding to the pile. The pause is not a punishment. It is the same pause you would apply to any unpaid invoice, and it is the protection that stops a disputed invoice from becoming two disputed invoices. Say it plainly, in the same message, or in the next one: "I will pause the next phase until this invoice is settled, so we can start the next piece with agreed terms in place."

4. When the client genuinely never saw the terms

There will be times when the client is right, and they genuinely never saw the terms, or never received the contract, or received it in a form they could not open, or received it after the work had already started, and the honest thing to do is to say so, and to fix it, without giving away the invoice.

The way to tell the difference is the record. If the contract was sent to the wrong address, or to a dead inbox, or as an attachment that would not open, or after the work had started, then the client has a real point, and the freelancer should own it. "You are right, I sent the contract on 5 March, after the work had already started on 3 March, and that is my mistake. The terms were on the invoice, which you received on 10 March, and the invoice is now overdue. Could we settle this invoice, and I will send the terms for signature before the next project starts?" That message owns the specific mistake, and it does not own the invoice. The invoice was sent, with the terms on it, and the client received it, and the payment is due, and the mistake about the contract does not change any of that.

The mistake to avoid is the blanket concession, the one that says "you are right, there was no contract, so the terms do not apply, and I will waive the late fee, and we can agree new terms." That concession gives the client everything they asked for, and it gives it away for free, and it will be quoted back to you on the next invoice, when the client says "there was no contract last time either", and points to the time you agreed. Own the specific thing that went wrong, and no more. The payment is still due, and the due date is still the due date, and the missing signature does not change either of those facts.

There is also a practical fix worth making, once, properly, after the payment is settled. Send the terms again, in a form the client can open, and get a written acceptance, in the same message, before the next piece of work starts. The acceptance does not have to be a signature. It can be an email reply that says "agreed", or a message that says "I accept the terms", or a deposit paid under the terms. But it has to be in writing, and it has to be before the work starts, and it has to be in the record, because the record is the protection, and the protection only works if it exists before the dispute, not after it.

5. What not to do when the terms are disputed

There are four mistakes that freelancers make in this conversation, and all four of them cost money.

The first is to renegotiate the terms in the middle of the dispute. A client who says "I never agreed to a late fee" is asking you to agree new terms, now, for work that is already done, and the answer is no. The terms for work that is already done are the terms that were in place when the work was done, and the terms for the next project are a separate conversation, for after this invoice is paid. Renegotiating mid-dispute teaches the client that disputing the terms is a way to change them, which is a lesson you will pay for on every invoice after this one.

The second is to keep working without agreed terms. A client who has disputed the terms of the current project, and who has not agreed new terms, is a client whose next project has no terms at all, and the freelancer who starts it is the freelancer who will have the same conversation again, in a month, about a larger invoice. Do not start the next piece until the terms are agreed, in writing, and the current invoice is settled. The pause is the protection, and the protection is worth more than the next piece of work.

The third is to send a new contract, with new terms, and ask the client to sign it, as if the new contract settles the old invoice. A new contract, signed now, applies to the work that comes after it, not to the work that is already done, and the client who signs it will say so, when the old invoice is chased. The old invoice is governed by the terms that were in place when the work was done, and the record that shows those terms, and the new contract is for the future, not the past. Do not confuse the two, and do not let the client confuse them either.

The fourth is to get drawn into an argument about the law. The conversation is not about the law. It is about whether the client saw the terms, and went ahead, and accepted the work, and the answer to that question is in the messages, and the emails, and the invoices, and the payments. A freelancer who argues about statutes, or jurisdiction, or enforceability, has left the facts, and the facts are the only ground that is solid. Stay on the facts, quote the client's own words, state what was sent and when, and let the record do the work. And if the sum is large enough that the law genuinely matters, get proper legal advice, in your own jurisdiction, before you say anything that could weaken your position.

6. Prevention, and the system that makes the dispute impossible

The prevention for this particular problem is simple, and it is the part that freelancers skip most often, because it feels awkward, and because it slows down the start of a project by a day.

The first prevention habit is the simplest one there is: no work before written acceptance. Not a signature, necessarily, although a signature is best. A written acceptance, in an email, or a message, that says the client has seen the terms, and agrees to them, and the work starts after that message, not before it. The habit is to send the terms, wait for the reply, and then start. The wait is usually hours, not days, and the client who will not reply is a client who is telling you something, and it is better to hear it before the work starts than after it is delivered.

The second prevention habit is to put the key terms on the invoice itself, every time, in plain language, with a number of days, not a phrase. "Payment within 14 days of the invoice date" is a term. "Payment promptly" is not a term, and "payment on receipt" is not a term either, because both of them can be argued about, and both of them will be, by the client who wants to argue. A number of days, a due date on the invoice, and a stated consequence for late payment, in writing, on every invoice, is the prevention. The client who has received ten invoices, each with the same terms printed on them, cannot say they never knew about the terms, and the freelancer who has the invoices does not have to argue about it.

The third habit is the deposit, because a deposit is an acceptance. A client who pays a deposit, under terms that say the deposit is non-refundable, and that the balance is due within 14 days, has accepted those terms, in the clearest way there is, by paying money under them. A deposit is not just cash flow. It is evidence, and it is the evidence that is hardest to dispute, because the client chose to pay it, and the payment is in the record.

The fourth habit is the single-thread rule, which is the rule that all agreement communication for one project stays in one thread, in one channel, from the quote to the final invoice. A client who says "I never saw the terms" is often a client who has moved the conversation across email, messages, calls, and meetings, until nobody can say what was sent where. A single thread ends that. The thread is the record, and the record is complete, and the client who wants to dispute the terms has to dispute them in the thread, where the terms, and their own reply, are both visible.

The pattern underneath

"I never signed the contract" is a dispute about a signature, and the way to answer it is to move the conversation from the signature to the record, and to let the record speak. A signature is one form of agreement, and it is not the only one. An email that says "looks good, let us get going", a deposit paid under the terms, an invoice accepted without dispute, work accepted and used, all of these are agreement, and the freelancer who has kept the record can hold the line on the terms, calmly, and usually successfully. Answer with the facts, with dates, quote the client's own words, and separate the payment from the process, settling this invoice now and getting a signed contract for the next project. When the client genuinely never saw the terms, own that specific mistake, and no more, and never let a missing signature become a waiver of the whole invoice. The prevention is no work before written acceptance, the key terms on every invoice, the deposit as acceptance, and the single thread that keeps the record complete. The freelancer who keeps that record never has to argue about a signature, because the record answers the question, and the freelancer who answers the question, once, calmly, and then gets back to work, is the freelancer who gets paid.
