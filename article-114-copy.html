The "Check Is in the Mail" Excuse: What Slow Payers Really Mean

Every freelancer learns the catalog of payment excuses. "The check is in the mail." "Accounting is backed up." "Can we do Net 60 instead?" They sound reasonable, and sometimes they are. But experienced freelancers know that each excuse carries a real meaning underneath, and your response should match the meaning, not the words. Here is a translation guide for the most common ones.

"The check is in the mail"

Translation: nobody has processed your invoice yet. In the digital-payment era, a mailed check is usually a sign that your invoice is sitting in a pile, not that a payment is traveling to you. The response is friendly and specific: "Great, can you share a tracking number or a screenshot of the processed payment? That helps me update my records." You are not calling them a liar. You are asking for the one piece of evidence that distinguishes a sent payment from a stalled one. If it never arrives, follow up in 5 business days.

"Accounting is backed up"

Translation: your invoice is not a priority. Backlogs clear from the top, and you are not at the top. This is actually useful information: it tells you the problem is process, not malice. Your move is to make your invoice easier to process than everyone else's. Send one clean email with the invoice number, amount, due date, and your payment details in the body, not just the attachment. Ask: "Is there anything missing that accounting needs to release this?" Putting your payment details in the email body, not just the attachment, removes the most common reason an invoice sits untouched in a queue.

"Can we do Net 60 instead?"

Translation: we want a two-month interest-free loan from you. Net 60 is not a payment term; it is financing. The polite refusal: "My standard terms are Net 14 for all clients, which keeps me fair and consistent. I can split this invoice into two Net 14 payments if cash flow is tight right now." You have said no to the bad term, offered a real alternative, and framed your terms as policy rather than personal. Clients respect policy; they negotiate with people.

"We never received the invoice"

Translation: either it genuinely got lost, or someone is stalling. Treat it as genuine the first time, every time. Resend immediately, confirm the right recipient ("Just to confirm, is invoices@client.com the right address, or should I send to someone in accounts payable?"), and get a named contact. The second time it happens with the same client, the translation changes: it is a pattern. Patterns get the escalation treatment: firmer language, a pay-by date, and a stop-work clause for future projects.

"The person who approves payments is on vacation"

Translation: plausible, and usually true for one to two weeks. Beyond that, it is a stall. Every real company has a backup approver. Your response: "No problem at all. Who is covering approvals while they are out? I will send the invoice to them so it is ready when they return." This is perfectly reasonable and completely eliminates the excuse. If there is genuinely no backup, note the return date, add 3 days, and set your own follow-up for that date. Do not let a two-week vacation become a six-week gap.

"We are a startup, cash is tight"

Translation: honest, sometimes, but their cash problem is not your cash problem. You can be sympathetic without financing their runway. Options that work: smaller milestone invoices (less per payment, easier to approve), a deposit before continuing, or a payment plan with specific dates. What does not work: doing more work while the old invoice sits. Every week of unpaid work increases your exposure to a company that may not exist next quarter. Be kind, but be structured.

When excuses become a pattern

One excuse is a situation. Two excuses are a delay. Three excuses are a policy, and the policy is "we pay you when it suits us." The shift in your approach is simple: stop responding to the excuse and start responding to the pattern. The pattern gets a formal email with invoice numbers, dates, amounts, and a clear pay-by date. The pattern gets a late fee (stated in your contract). The pattern gets a work pause. You do not have to be angry. You just have to stop treating the third excuse like it is the first.

The one line that ends excuse loops

When excuses keep coming, this sentence moves things forward without burning the relationship: "I want to keep working with you, and I need the account current to do that. What date can you commit to for clearing the outstanding invoices?" It does three things: affirms the relationship, states the condition plainly, and asks for a commitment with a date. A date is the one thing excuses never contain. Get the date in writing, then hold them to it.
