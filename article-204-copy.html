The Client Who Keeps Moving the Goalposts: How to Price Scope Changes Before They Eat the Project

The project started with a clear brief: a landing page, five sections, one revision round. Then the client adds a section. Then a second landing page "while you are in there." Then a whole new audience segment, a video embed, and a rewrite of the headline strategy. Each change sounds small on its own. Together, they have doubled the project, and the price has not moved at all.

This is scope creep, and it is the quietest way freelancers lose money, because no single change feels worth a fight. The client is not being malicious. They are thinking out loud, and each new idea genuinely seems like part of the same project to them. Your job is not to refuse good ideas. It is to price them the moment they appear, so the project grows in price at the same speed it grows in work.

1. Define the scope in writing before the first change arrives

You cannot police a boundary that was never drawn. If the original agreement says "a landing page" with no detail, then every addition is arguably part of the project, and your objection sounds like reluctance rather than a fact. Scope control starts at the proposal, not at the first extra request.

Write the scope as a list, not a paragraph: the deliverables, the number of pages or sections, the revision rounds included, and what is explicitly not included. "Included: one landing page, five sections, copy for each section, one revision round. Not included: additional pages, video production, ad creative." That "not included" line is the most valuable sentence in the proposal, because it turns every later addition from an argument into a lookup.

If you are already mid-project with a vague scope, draw the line now, before the next request: "Just to keep us aligned, here is what the current price covers: [list]. Anything beyond this I will quote separately before starting." Clients rarely object to a clear list, because it protects them too: they finally know what they bought.

2. Answer every extra request with a price, not a sigh

The moment a client asks for something outside the scope, your reply sets the precedent for the rest of the project. A sigh and a yes teaches the client that extras are free. A price teaches them that extras are available, which is a much better lesson, because most clients are happy to pay for things they actually want.

Use a short, fixed reply shape every time: acknowledge the idea, name it as an addition, give a price and a time effect, and ask for confirmation before starting. "Good idea, and that sits outside the current scope, so I can add it for [amount], which adds [time] to the timeline. Shall I go ahead on that basis?" No apology, no lecture about scope, no long explanation. The client either says yes and the work is paid, or says no and the project stays as agreed. Both outcomes are wins.

The hardest part is speed. Price the extra the same day it is requested, before you start it. Once you have done the work, your negotiating position is gone, and asking for payment afterwards feels like a surprise bill. A price given before the work is a choice the client made. A price given after the work is a complaint.

3. Batch small changes instead of absorbing them one by one

Not every change deserves a separate quote. A tweaked headline, a swapped image, a small wording fix: these are normal project friction, and quoting each one would make you look petty. The danger is that ten small changes, each too small to quote, add up to a day of unpaid work.

Handle this with a batching rule, stated early: "Small tweaks inside the agreed scope are included. When extras add up, I will bundle them into one quote before the total passes [amount or hours]." Then keep a simple running list of extras as they arrive, and send the bundle quote when it reaches your threshold. The client sees one reasonable number instead of ten tiny invoices, and you never carry a silent pile of unpaid hours.

Set the threshold low enough to matter and high enough to be fair: roughly an hour of work, or about 10 percent of the project fee, whichever you reach first. And tell the client the rule exists before you use it, so the bundle quote arrives as an expected step, not a sudden charge.

4. Never let a change reset the payment clock

Scope changes do more damage than extra hours: they quietly push the delivery date, which pushes the invoice date, which pushes payment. A project that was due to finish this month drifts into next month, and your cash flow drifts with it, while the client believes everything is on track because work is still happening.

Protect the clock in the same message as the price. Every change quote should carry three numbers: the extra price, the extra time, and the new delivery and invoice dates. "Adding the second page is [amount], adds three working days, and moves delivery to [date], with the final invoice following on delivery." If the client wants the original date kept, that is a rush fee conversation, not a free acceleration.

Also watch for the change that reopens finished work. A revision to a section that was already approved is not a tweak, it is new work on an old deliverable, and it should be priced as such. "That section was signed off on [date], so reopening it is a new round at [amount]." Approved work that can be reopened for free will be reopened forever.

5. Price the pattern when the same client moves the goalposts twice

One scope change is normal. Two is a pattern, and patterns need a structural fix, not another one-off quote. A client who expands every project is telling you that your scoping process, or your price, does not match how they actually buy.

For a repeat mover, change the structure of the next engagement. Options that work: a higher base price that includes a defined change allowance, a phased agreement where each phase is quoted and approved before it starts, or an hourly rate for anything beyond the agreed scope, agreed in advance. "On the next project, anything beyond the agreed scope bills at [rate], quoted before I start it, so you always know where you stand." Clients who genuinely value the flexibility accept this easily, because it gives them what they wanted: the freedom to change their mind, at a known price.

If the client refuses every structural fix and keeps expecting free expansion, that is a decision about the client, not about the project. Price the next project for the work it will actually become, not the work described in the brief, or decline it. A project that doubles in size at a fixed price is not a project. It is a discount you never agreed to.

The pattern underneath

Scope creep is not a client character flaw. It is what happens when a project has no written boundary, no price for extras, and no clock protection, so every good idea flows straight into unpaid work. Draw the scope as a list, answer each extra with a price before starting it, batch the small ones to a threshold, protect the delivery and invoice dates with every change, and fix repeat patterns structurally in the next agreement. Done this way, a moving goalpost stops being a loss and becomes what it should have been all along: more work, at a fair price, agreed before it starts.
