The Client Who Asks for a Discount After the Work Is Done: How to Hold the Agreed Price

The work is delivered, approved, and in use. Then, with the invoice already issued, the client asks: "Can you do 20 percent off?" "Our budget changed, can we meet in the middle?" "We were expecting a lower number." The price was agreed before the work started, the work is finished, and suddenly the price is negotiable again, but only downward, and only after you have already spent the time.

This is not a negotiation. A negotiation happens before work starts, when both sides can still walk away. A discount request after delivery is pressure applied at the one moment you have the least leverage: the work is done, the client has the deliverable, and refusing feels like risking the relationship and the payment itself. That is exactly why it works, and why it keeps happening to freelancers who give in once.

The answer is not to argue about the value of the work. It is to hold the structure that was agreed, calmly, and to make the next discount request harder than this one.

1. Point to the agreement, not to your effort

The weakest response to a post-delivery discount request is to justify the price: the hours you spent, the quality of the work, the revisions you included. That invites a debate about value, which the client will win by simply valuing the work less than you do. The strongest response points to the agreement, which is not a matter of opinion.

"The price of [amount] was agreed in the proposal dated [date], and the work was delivered to that scope." One sentence, factual, unarguable. You are not saying the work is worth the money. You are saying the money was agreed, and the work was done. Those are two facts, and neither depends on how the client feels about the result now.

If there was no written agreement, say so honestly and fix that gap before the next project, but still hold the price: "The quoted price was [amount], confirmed in our messages on [date], and the invoice matches that quote." A quoted price confirmed in writing is still an agreement, even without a formal contract, and most clients know it.

2. Never discount for nothing

If you ever do reduce a price after delivery, and there are rare cases where it makes sense, never give the reduction away free. A discount given for nothing teaches the client that the price was flexible all along, and the next invoice will get the same request, larger.

Trade, do not concede. A reduced price buys something concrete: payment today instead of Net 30, a shorter payment term on all future work, a deposit on the next project paid now, or a scope reduction that matches the lower price. "I can reduce this invoice to [amount] if payment is made today by bank transfer" is a trade. "Okay, 20 percent off" is a surrender, and the client will remember which one you gave.

In practice, most post-delivery discount requests evaporate the moment a trade is required, because the client never wanted a trade. They wanted a lower price for the same work, paid on the same slow terms. When the discount costs them something, they usually decide the original price was fine.

3. Separate the discount from the payment

The most dangerous version of this request is the one tied to payment: "We will pay the lower amount now, or you can wait for the full amount." That is not a discount request, it is a hostage offer, and accepting it sets a price for every future invoice: whatever the client decides to pay, whenever they decide to pay it.

Refuse the linkage plainly: "The invoice amount is [amount], due [date]. I am happy to discuss pricing for future work, but this invoice stands as agreed." Then continue your normal chase schedule for the full amount. Do not pause the chase while a discount is "under discussion," because a discussion with no deadline is just another form of late payment, and the client knows it.

If the client pays the reduced amount anyway, do not treat the balance as written off by default. Acknowledge the partial payment, state the remaining balance in writing, and keep chasing it on the normal schedule. Silence after a short payment reads as acceptance, and acceptance is very hard to undo later.

4. Find out what the request is really about

A post-delivery discount request sometimes hides a real problem: the client is short of cash, the budget holder changed, or the work genuinely missed something. It is worth one calm question before you hold the line: "Is there an issue with the work itself, or is this about budget timing?" The answer changes your response.

If it is a genuine quality issue, fix the issue, and fix only the issue. A real defect deserves a real remedy, and fixing it promptly protects the relationship and the payment. But a defect in one deliverable does not justify a discount on the whole invoice, and a vague "we expected more" is not a defect. Ask for specifics: "Please tell me exactly what does not match the agreed scope, and I will address that item."

If it is cash flow, a payment plan beats a discount. "I can split this invoice into two payments, [amount] by [date] and [amount] by [date], with the full amount unchanged" solves the client's real problem without repricing your work. A client who accepts a payment plan needed time. A client who refuses both the plan and the full price wanted a discount, and now you know it.

5. Price the pattern into the next project

A client who asks for a post-delivery discount once will ask again, unless something changes. That something is your terms for the next project, and you set it before the next project starts, not during the next discount conversation.

Three changes, chosen to fit the client: a deposit before work starts, so a later discount request can only touch the balance, not the whole fee; a written clause stating that the agreed price is fixed once work begins and changes require a written change order; and shorter payment terms, so the window for pressure shrinks. Tell the client plainly: "For the next project, my terms are a 50 percent deposit and the balance on delivery, with the price fixed in the proposal." Clients who intend to pay accept this. Clients who wanted another discount round will object, and that objection arrives before you have done any work, which is exactly when you want it.

If the client will not accept tighter terms and has already discounted you once, that is a complete picture of the relationship: the price will keep falling and the payment will keep slipping. The right move is to let that client go, politely, and free the slot for a client who pays the agreed price.

The pattern underneath

A discount requested after delivery is pressure, not negotiation, and it only works if you treat the agreed price as flexible. Hold it with the agreement rather than an argument about value, never discount for nothing, refuse to link the discount to payment, find out whether a real problem hides behind the request, and price the pattern into the next project's terms. The clients who respect the price stay, the clients who do not reveal themselves early, and your rate stops being something a client can renegotiate after you have already done the work. The price was set before the work, and the work is done. That is the whole argument, and it is enough.
