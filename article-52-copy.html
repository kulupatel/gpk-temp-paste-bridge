How to Vet a New Client's Payment History Before You Start

Everyone checks a client's portfolio before signing. Almost nobody checks whether the client pays. Then the project ends, the invoice goes out, and the freelancer discovers what a two-minute background check would have revealed: this client has a reputation, and it is not a good one.

Vetting a client's payment behavior before you start is not paranoia. It is the cheapest insurance in freelancing. Ten minutes of research can save you ten weeks of chasing.

Start with the obvious: search the client's name plus words like "scam," "didn't pay," "late payment," and "review." Freelancers talk. Forums, Reddit threads, and review sites carry payment complaints that never make it to a testimonial page. One complaint is noise; a pattern is data.

Next, look at how they talk about money before there is any. A client who winces at a 50% deposit, haggles your rate down 40% in the first call, or says "we will sort out the paperwork later" is showing you their payment culture in advance. Believe the preview. Clients who are easy about terms before the work are usually easy about payment after it; clients who fight every clause will fight the invoice too.

Ask for a reference, and ask it the right way. Not "are you legit" but "can you put me in touch with a freelancer you have paid in the last six months?" A healthy client answers this in a day. A client who dodges, delays, or gets offended has told you everything. You are not asking for a character reference; you are asking for a payment reference, and legitimate businesses have them.

Run the paid trial. Before a big engagement, propose a small paid test project with your full payment terms attached: deposit, net terms, late fee, the works. How they handle a $500 invoice predicts how they will handle a $5,000 one. If the trial invoice takes 45 days and three reminders, you have your answer before the real money is at stake.

Check the structural signals. Does the client have a named accounts-payable contact, or does "just send it to me" cover the whole finance function? Real companies have payment processes: PO numbers, approval chains, payment runs. A one-person operation that wires money from a personal account can still pay perfectly, but you want to know which kind you are dealing with so your terms match reality.

Ask two direct questions in the kickoff call, and watch the reaction, not just the answer. One: "What are your standard payment terms?" Two: "Who approves invoices, and how long does approval usually take?" Clients with nothing to hide answer plainly. Clients who get vague, defensive, or promise "we are very fast, do not worry" without specifics deserve a deposit-heavy structure.

Finally, write down what you found. A one-paragraph note per client: terms agreed, AP contact, trial result, any flags. Six months later, when the client asks to move to Net 30 "like everyone else," your note is the memory that keeps you from agreeing to something you already know is a bad idea.

None of this replaces good contracts and fast invoicing. Vetting is the filter before the work; the contract is the protection during it; the chase system is the recovery after. Run all three and late payment becomes a rare event instead of a business model.
