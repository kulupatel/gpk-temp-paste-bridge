How to Ask for a Deposit Without Losing the Client

The freelancer who always chases invoices and the freelancer who rarely chases them usually differ in exactly one habit: the second one asks for money before the work starts. Deposits are the single strongest late-payment prevention tool that exists, stronger than any chase email. Yet most freelancers never ask, because they are afraid the client will walk away.

Here is the uncomfortable math. Clients who refuse any deposit are the clients most likely to pay late, or not at all. If asking for a deposit loses you a client, it lost you the kind of client you did not want. The freelancers who charge deposits consistently report that it is a filter, not a barrier: it removes exactly the projects that would have caused the most pain.

Why deposits work better than contracts alone

A contract clause about late fees is a threat about the future. A deposit is money that already changed hands. Psychologically, it does two things a contract cannot. First, it converts the client from a prospect into a payer; people who have paid something behave like people who intend to keep paying. Second, it caps your worst-case loss. If a $2,000 project dies unpaid at the halfway point, a 50% deposit means you lost half a day's argument, not two weeks of work.

The standard deposit is 30 to 50 percent of the project fee, due before work begins. For ongoing retainer work, the equivalent is first-month payment up front. For very small fixed jobs under a few hundred dollars, full payment up front is normal and nobody blinks.

The exact script to use

The mistake is treating the deposit as a special request. Present it as standard practice, because it is standard practice. Here is a script that has survived contact with real clients:

"Just a note on how I structure payments: I take a 50% deposit to reserve your project slot and begin, with the balance due on delivery. I will send the deposit invoice along with the contract today. Once both are back, I will get started."

Notice what the script does. It says "how I structure payments," not "would you mind." It names the deposit and the balance in one breath, so the client hears a process, not a demand. It links the deposit to a concrete benefit, the reserved project slot, which makes the money feel like it buys something immediate.

Send the deposit invoice the same day you send the contract. Never start work on a promise of payment. The client who says "the deposit is coming, just start" is telling you, with words, that they do not respect the payment structure you just set. Believe them.

Handling the three objections you will hear

"We don't pay deposits." This comes almost exclusively from companies with more than fifty employees. Their procurement process genuinely may not allow it. The fallback: ask for the first milestone paid in full before work begins, framed as a paid discovery or kickoff phase. You get money up front under a different name, and the filter still works, because a company that cannot even pay a small kickoff invoice is telling you about its cash flow.

"Can we do 20%?" Yes, if the alternative is no deposit at all. A small deposit beats no deposit, and the negotiation itself signals the client is engaged with paying. Take the 20%, note it in the contract, and keep the balance schedule tight.

"I'll pay you when the project is done." This is the objection that should make you walk away, politely, every time. A client with no history with you is asking you to extend them credit while you carry all the risk. Professionals do not do this. Your polite exit: "I understand. My payment structure is fixed, so I am probably not the right fit for this one. Happy to recommend someone else." Then watch how fast some of them reconsider.

Deposits change who you attract

Something freelancers notice about six months after going strict on deposits: the client mix changes. The hagglers and the slow-payers stop booking, because your process quietly screens them out. The clients who remain pay on time, because they were the kind of people who pay on time all along. Deposits do not just protect individual invoices. They reshape your whole book of business toward clients who respect it.

The freelancer who never asks for a deposit is not being nice. They are volunteering to be the client's free line of credit. Stop volunteering.
