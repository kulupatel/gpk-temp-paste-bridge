The Client Who Pays the Deposit, Then Asks for It Back: How to Hold a Deposit That Is Already Yours

The deposit arrived on time. It was the first good sign in the whole engagement: a client who paid before the work started, who took the booking seriously, who treated your terms as real. Then, a week later, before you have started the work, the message arrives. Something has changed, the project is on hold, the budget has moved, the timing no longer works, and could you send the deposit back. The client is polite about it. They are not angry. They are asking, not demanding. And the request feels reasonable, because no work has been done yet, and the money is just sitting there, and sending it back would be the easy, friendly thing to do.

It feels reasonable because the deposit looks like money for work not yet done, and money for work not yet done looks like money that should be returned if the work never happens. But that is not what a deposit is. A deposit is not a prepayment for hours you have not worked. It is the price of the commitment itself. It is what the client pays for you to hold the dates, turn down other work, start the preparation, and take their project seriously as a booking rather than a possibility. The moment you accepted the deposit, you spent something: the slot in your calendar, the other client you said no to, the preparation you had already begun. The deposit is the client's share of that cost, and it was earned the moment it was paid, not the moment the work starts.

That does not mean you should never return a deposit. Sometimes returning it is the right move, and this article will say when. But the default answer is no, and the reason is not stubbornness. It is that a deposit which comes back on request is not a deposit at all. It is a free option on your time, and a client who learns that the deposit is refundable on request will treat every future booking the same way, and so will every client they tell about it.

1. Why the refund ask arrives

The refund ask usually has one of five causes, and knowing which one you are dealing with decides how firm your answer should be.

The first is cold feet. The client signed, paid, and then the reality of the spend caught up with them. They are not angry with you. They are nervous about the money, and the refund request is a way of undoing a decision they now regret. Cold feet is the most common cause, and it is also the most negotiable, because the client still wants the work, they just want the commitment to feel smaller.

The second is a cheaper alternative. The client found someone who will do it for less, or an in-house person who can do it, or a tool that claims to do it, and the deposit is now standing between them and the cheaper option. This client is not asking for a refund because the project changed. They are asking because the price comparison changed, and your deposit is the exit fee they would rather not pay.

The third is an internal change. The budget was cut, the manager changed, the project was cancelled from above, and the person you are dealing with is caught between your invoice and their own organisation. This client is often genuinely stuck, and they are often embarrassed about it, which is why the message is polite and full of apology. The apology is real. It does not make the deposit refundable.

The fourth is a cash flow problem dressed as a scheduling problem. The client says the timing no longer works, or the project is delayed, when the real problem is that the money is needed elsewhere this month. The delay story is kinder than the money story, and clients tell it because it feels less awkward. If you offer to reschedule rather than refund, this client will often take the reschedule, which tells you everything about what the real problem was.

The fifth is a test. A small number of clients ask for the deposit back simply to see whether you will give it back, because a freelancer who returns a deposit on request is a freelancer whose terms are negotiable, and a freelancer whose terms are negotiable is a freelancer who can be pushed on price, scope, and deadlines later. This is the rarest cause, and it is also the one where a firm no matters most, because the refund request is not the end of the negotiation. It is the beginning of it.

None of these causes makes the client a bad person. All five are normal, human reasons to ask. But a normal reason to ask is not a reason to agree, and the deposit exists precisely for the moment when the client's plans change, because that moment is when your calendar, your preparation, and your turned-down work are already spent.

2. What a deposit actually is

A deposit is three things at once, and the refund argument only makes sense if you forget two of them.

First, it is a commitment device. It is the thing that separates a client who has booked you from a client who is thinking about booking you. Without it, every enquiry is a maybe, and every maybe costs you the same calendar slot, the same preparation, and the same declined work, with no compensation when the maybe evaporates. The deposit is what makes the booking real, and a booking that can be undone for free was never a booking.

Second, it is compensation for the slot. When a client books you for a week in March, that week is gone from your market. You cannot sell it twice, and you cannot get it back when the client cancels, because the other enquiries for that week have already been answered, or have gone elsewhere, or have expired. The deposit is the client's payment for the exclusivity of that slot, and exclusivity is a service you have already delivered, whether or not any project work has started.

Third, it is the start of the work, not the prelude to it. By the time a deposit is paid, you have usually done the scoping call, the proposal, the planning, the preparation, the file setup, and the first round of thinking about the project. None of that appears on an invoice as hours, but all of it is work, and the deposit is the only payment that covers it when the project never reaches the main delivery.

This is why the wording in your contract matters, and why the single most valuable sentence you can add to your terms is a plain one: "The deposit is non-refundable and secures your booking dates." That sentence does three jobs. It tells the client the rule before they pay, so the refund request is not a surprise to either side. It gives you something to quote when the request arrives, so the answer is the contract speaking, not you being difficult. And it stops the negotiation before it starts, because a client who agreed to a non-refundable deposit in writing has already accepted the term, and asking you to break it is asking you to break your own agreement, which is a much bigger ask than a refund.

If your current terms do not say the deposit is non-refundable, add the sentence before your next booking, and say it in the proposal too, in the same plain words, so the client sees it twice before paying. The time to establish the rule is before the money moves, not after the refund request arrives.

3. The answer that holds the line

The answer that works is short, warm, and final, and it works because it gives the client something real while keeping the deposit where it belongs. The sentence is: "The deposit secures your booking and is non-refundable, as agreed in the contract, but I am happy to hold it as credit toward the project whenever you are ready to restart it."

That sentence does four things. It states the rule, so there is no ambiguity. It points to the agreement, so the rule is not your personal preference but a term the client accepted. It offers credit, so the client has not lost the money, they have simply parked it against future work, which is a genuine concession that costs you nothing now. And it keeps the door open, so the client hears a yes to the relationship, not a rejection of the person.

If the client wants to reschedule rather than cancel, say yes to that, and say it plainly. "Of course, let us move the dates. The deposit holds your new slot." A reschedule is the best outcome for everyone: the client keeps their booking, you keep your calendar full, and the deposit does the job it was paid to do. Offer the reschedule before the client asks for it, because a client who is offered a reschedule and still wants a refund is telling you the problem was never the timing.

If the client wants a smaller scope instead of a cancellation, that can work too, as long as the deposit stays. "We can reduce the scope to fit the new budget, and the deposit counts toward the smaller project." The deposit is not a payment for a specific deliverable. It is a payment for the booking, and a smaller booking is still a booking.

What you should not do is offer a partial refund as a compromise, because a partial refund concedes the principle while keeping the argument. The client who gets half back will come back for the other half, and the client who learns that the deposit is half negotiable will treat the next term as half negotiable too. The deposit is either refundable or it is not, and if your terms say it is not, then the answer is the whole deposit, held as credit, not half of it sent back as a peace offering.

And put the answer in writing, in the same thread, because a verbal agreement about a deposit is a conversation, and a written answer is a record, and the record is what you will quote if the request comes back a second time. "To confirm: the deposit of $750 is held as credit toward the project, and we can restart whenever you are ready." That message takes thirty seconds to write, and it ends the negotiation in a way that a phone call never will, because a phone call can be re-argued and a written confirmation cannot.

4. When a refund is the right move

There are four situations where returning the deposit is the right decision, and it is worth naming them, because a rule with no exceptions is a rule you will break under pressure, and a rule with named exceptions is a rule you can hold.

The first is when you cannot deliver. If you have to cancel the project yourself, because you are ill, overbooked, or unable to do the work, the deposit comes back, in full, immediately, with an apology. This is not generosity. It is the basic deal: the deposit secures your availability, and if you are not available, there is nothing to secure.

The second is when you missed the start date. If the client booked you for March, and you moved the project to May without agreement, and the client no longer needs the work in May, the delay is yours, and the deposit should go back. A deposit secures a date. If you broke the date, you broke the term, and the client is entitled to the money back.

The third is a genuine hardship with a clean exit. A client whose business has closed, or who has had a serious personal emergency, is not testing you, and returning the deposit in that case is a human decision, not a business one. The test is whether the hardship is real and verifiable, and whether the client is asking once, politely, rather than negotiating. If both are true, a refund can be the right call, and it will often bring the client back later, because people remember who was kind when it mattered.

The fourth is when the cost of keeping the deposit is higher than the deposit. If the client is going to dispute the charge, leave public complaints, or spend the next month arguing, and the deposit is small, the refund may be cheaper than the fight. This is a business calculation, not a surrender, and it should be made once, deliberately, with the number in front of you, not in the heat of an angry exchange. If the deposit is $200 and the dispute will cost you a week, send it back and move on. If the deposit is $2,000, the calculation runs the other way.

In every other case, the answer is the one in section 3: the deposit is held as credit, the door stays open, and the money stays where the contract put it.

5. The client who keeps asking

Some clients will ask twice. The first answer was clear, the credit was offered, the reschedule was proposed, and the client comes back with a longer message, a harder tone, or a new reason. This is the moment where most freelancers give in, because the second ask feels more serious than the first, and the fear is that refusing twice will damage the relationship beyond repair.

It will not, and the reason is that the relationship is already damaged, by the client's change of plan, not by your answer. A client who keeps asking for a deposit back after a clear, polite, written no is not looking for a compromise. They are looking for a crack, and every concession you make teaches them that asking again works, which means they will ask again about the next thing, and the thing after that.

So the second answer is shorter than the first, and it repeats the same terms without adding anything new. "As I mentioned, the deposit is non-refundable and is held as credit toward the project whenever you are ready to restart. I am happy to move the dates or reduce the scope to fit the new budget." That is the whole answer. No new offer, no new concession, no apology for the policy, because apologising for a term you both agreed to tells the client the term was a mistake, and it was not.

If the client threatens a chargeback or a public complaint, do not argue, and do not refund out of fear. Save the contract, save the payment record, save the thread where the client agreed to the terms and paid the deposit, and let the process run. A chargeback on a deposit the client agreed was non-refundable, with a written contract and a payment record, is a dispute you can win with documents, and a freelancer who refunds every time a chargeback is mentioned is a freelancer who will be threatened with one on every project.

And when it is over, decide whether the client is worth keeping on new terms, with a larger deposit, milestone splits, and a pause clause, or whether the pattern is the client, and the right move is to keep the credit on file, let the project go, and free the calendar for someone who treats a booking as a booking. A deposit that is refunded on request is not a deposit. It is a discount on your time, given to the client who asked loudest, and the next client will ask louder.

The pattern underneath

A deposit is not a prepayment for work not yet done. It is the price of the commitment itself: the slot you held, the work you turned down, and the preparation you had already begun, all of which were spent the moment the booking was made. Hold the line with a short, warm, written answer that states the term, points to the agreement, and offers the deposit as credit toward a restart, a reschedule, or a smaller scope. Return it only when you cannot deliver, when you broke the date, when the hardship is real, or when the fight costs more than the money. And when the client asks twice, repeat the same answer without adding anything new, because a deposit that comes back on request was never a deposit at all, and the freelancer who defends it is defending the value of their own time.
