What to Say on the Phone When a Client Ignores Your Invoices

Two polite reminder emails have gone out. The invoice is 21 days overdue. Nobody has replied. This is the moment most freelancers keep sending emails, and it is the moment to stop. A phone call does what a seventh email cannot: it forces a human being to give you an answer in real time.

Most freelancers dread this call. They picture confrontation, awkwardness, and a damaged relationship. In practice the call is usually under three minutes, perfectly civil, and more effective than a month of email. Here is how to make it.

Call at the right moment, not earlier

Do not call on day three. Calling too early looks desperate and trains clients to expect you will always escalate personally. The right moment is after your emails have clearly failed: two sent reminders, no reply, and the invoice past the due date by at least a week. By then the call is not aggressive. It is the obvious next step, and the client knows it.

Call during business hours in the client's timezone, ideally mid-morning or mid-afternoon. Avoid Monday morning and Friday afternoon. Have the invoice number, amount, and due date written in front of you before you dial.

The three-minute structure

Open with warmth and precision. "Hi Sarah, it is Alex from [your business]. Hope you are well. I am calling about invoice 2047 for $2,400, which was due on September 10. I wanted to check it landed safely on your end." Notice what this does: it assumes a benign explanation, it names exact facts, and it puts the ball in their court.

Listen, then pin a date. Whatever they say, the stall, the AP delay, the "I thought accounting handled that", your only job is to extract a concrete payment date. "Understood. What date can I put down for payment?" Not "soon," not "this week." A date. If they offer "the end of the month," that is a date. Write it down while they talk.

Close with a promise of confirmation. "Great, I will send you a quick email confirming the 30th, and you will let me know if anything changes. Thanks, Sarah." The whole call should take under three minutes. You have been friendly, professional, and immovable.

Handling the stalls, live

You will hear every classic stall line, and each one has a clean reply.

"I will look into it." Your reply: "I appreciate that. When should I expect to hear back, Tuesday or Wednesday?" Force the timeline, politely.

"Accounting handles that." Your reply: "No problem, can you put me in touch with someone in accounting directly? Or forward my invoice to them while I am here?" Never accept a redirect to a department you cannot name.

"Can you send it again?" Your reply: "Of course, I will resend it right after this call. And the payment date will be?" Stalls disguised as requests still get a date attached.

Anger or guilt-tripping is rare and usually theatrical. Stay flat, stay warm, repeat the date question. "I hear you, and I want to keep working together. What date should I put down?" The calmer you are, the more the awkwardness works for you.

The call is worthless without the email after

Here is the mistake that undoes a good call: hanging up and doing nothing. Within the hour, send the confirmation email. Three sentences: "Great speaking with you. Confirming payment of $2,400 (invoice 2047) is scheduled for September 30. If anything changes, let me know and we will sort it out."

That email is the entire point. Verbal promises evaporate. The email turns the call into a written record, and it gives you a clean escalation basis if the date passes unpaid. When you follow up, you are no longer chasing. You are referencing a commitment.

The phone call works for one reason: ignoring an email costs the client nothing, but ignoring a person who has been polite and specific costs them something. Use the call at the right stage, keep it under three minutes, extract a date, and confirm it in writing. Most overdue invoices die in the email graveyard. The call is how you pull them out.
