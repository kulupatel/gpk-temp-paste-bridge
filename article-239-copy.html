The Purchase Order Problem: How to Get Paid When Your Client Needs a PO Number First

You did the work. You sent the invoice. Three weeks later it comes back, not paid and not disputed, just returned, with a one-line note: no valid purchase order number. You have never seen one, and nobody told you one was needed. The payment clock never started, because in their system your invoice was never valid.

This is not a client refusing to pay. It is a client whose system cannot pay you, because the document that authorises the spend does not exist yet, or was never linked to your invoice. Large organisations run on purchase orders, and many of them run a written rule with a blunt name: No PO, No Pay. HSBC publishes one. Philips publishes one. NHS trusts, universities, and councils publish them too. An invoice without a valid PO number is returned unpaid, not queried, not queued, returned.

1. What a purchase order actually is

A purchase order, usually shortened to PO, is the buyer's document: the client telling you, in writing, exactly what they are buying, at what price, and under which terms. Its unique PO number is the thread that ties the whole transaction together: the order, the delivery, and your invoice.

It is not an invoice. An invoice is your document, sent after the work is done, asking for payment. A PO is theirs, sent before the work starts, authorising the spend. The PO says we agree to buy this. The invoice later says here is the bill for what you ordered. When your invoice quotes the PO number, their accounts team can match the two in seconds. When it does not, there is nothing to match against, and the invoice sits until somebody chases it, which is you, three weeks later, discovering that a field was missing.

The matching has a name in finance teams: the three-way match. The PO, the record that the work was delivered, and the invoice must all agree. Same amount, same scope, same reference. If any one of the three is missing or mismatched, payment stops.

Two things about the number. First, it is the buyer's number, not yours: you cannot invent one, and you cannot use your own invoice number in its place. Second, quoting it does not make an invoice payable on its own. If the order was never approved, or the PO was for a different amount, the number will not save you.

Most small clients never use POs. You are most likely to meet one when you work with mid-sized or large organisations, government agencies, or universities. If a client mentions raising a PO, treat it as a good sign: the budget is approved, the money is set aside, and somebody with authority has already said yes.

2. Get the PO before you start

The single most important move is also the simplest: ask for the PO number before you start work, and do not start without it when the client uses POs. It belongs in the kickoff conversation, not in the first chase email. "Do you issue a PO for this, and is there a number I should put on invoices?" Ask it in writing.

When the PO arrives, read it before you start. Check the number, so you can quote it exactly; the amount, which must match your quote; the scope, which must describe the work you agreed to do; the payment terms and expiry date, because POs expire, usually at the end of a financial year; and the legal entity name, which must be the company you are working for.

If the amount is lower than your quote, or the scope is wrong, stop and fix it before you start. If there is no PO at all and the client says "we will sort the paperwork later", treat that as a risk decision, not admin. For a large job with a new client, wait: starting on a promised PO means doing the work and then needing a document that requires somebody else's approval to exist.

Put the rule in your standard terms, in plain language, so it is a policy rather than a negotiation. "Where your organisation requires a purchase order, I will not start work until I have the PO number in writing, and every invoice will quote it." Clients who use POs expect that sentence. To them it is how every supplier works.

3. Put the number on the invoice, prominently

Once you have the PO number, it has to be visible. Put it at the top of the invoice, near your own invoice number, where the first person who opens it can see it without searching. An accounts payable clerk who has never met you decides from the fields on the page whether your invoice can be entered into their system. A PO number buried in a paragraph is a PO number that gets missed.

Quote it exactly. One wrong digit, one transposed pair, and the system cannot match it, and the invoice is returned. Copy it from the PO document, character by character, and check it against the original before you send.

Match the amount to the PO. If the invoice is larger than the PO amount, the extra will not be paid under that PO, and the whole invoice may be parked. Get a revised PO, or a second PO, before you do the extra work, not after you have invoiced for it. The same applies to scope: if the PO says design work and the invoice says design plus copywriting, the mismatch is a reason to park the invoice.

Send the invoice to the address on the PO, in PDF, and confirm receipt within 48 hours. The PO usually names the billing address and sometimes the AP contact. Use them. An invoice sent to your day-to-day contact instead of the billing address is an invoice that has to be forwarded, and forwarded invoices get lost.

4. When the PO never arrives

Sometimes the work is done and the PO still has not arrived, and now you have to chase a document instead of a payment. Chase the right person: go back to whoever commissioned the work, not to accounts payable. Accounts payable can only tell you the invoice is non-compliant, which you already know. The requester is the person who can raise a purchase order, or ask their manager to approve one.

Ask in writing, and ask for the number itself. A reply that says "yes, we use POs" is not a PO number. You want the reference string, and ideally a copy of the PO document. Give it a date, too. "I need the PO number by Friday so I can invoice" is a request with a deadline. "Could you send the PO when you get a chance" is a request that will be answered after everything else.

If the PO is genuinely in progress, ask whether they can issue an emergency PO reference. Some organisations can supply one within a few working days, and that reference is enough to get your invoice into the system.

And do not reissue fresh invoices while you wait. A reissued invoice does not restart anything, because the problem was never the invoice. It creates a duplicate in their system, which is a second problem. Keep the original invoice as the only record, and chase the PO on its own schedule until it arrives.

5. The five PO traps

Even with a PO in hand, five traps can still cost you money.

The first is expiry. POs expire, and an expired PO is as useless as no PO at all. Check the date when the PO arrives, and ask for the expiry to be extended in writing if the work will run past it.

The second is the amount cap. A PO authorises a specific amount. If the PO covers $3,000 and your invoice is $3,400, the extra $400 is not payable under that PO, and the whole invoice may be parked. Get a revised PO before doing the extra work.

The third is scope mismatch. The PO describes what was ordered. If the work drifted, or the client asked for extras verbally, the invoice will describe something the PO does not, and the mismatch is a reason to park it. Get a revised PO before you invoice.

The fourth is a PO that is already fulfilled or closed. A PO can be spent or closed early when a budget line is shut, and a closed PO will not pay your invoice. If an invoice is returned against a PO you know exists, ask the requester to check the remaining balance on it.

The fifth is the wrong entity. A PO issued to a parent company does not authorise payment from a subsidiary, and an invoice addressed to the wrong legal name will be returned. Invoice the exact buyer name on the PO.

6. The prevention system

None of this is hard. It is a ten-minute check at kickoff, and it is the difference between a PO client who pays on time and one who costs you a month.

Add a PO field to your invoice template, so the number has a place to go on every invoice, and a missing number is visible before the invoice is sent. Add a PO line to your kickoff checklist, next to the deposit and the due date: PO number, PO amount, PO expiry, billing address, AP contact, payment terms. Six fields, one message, asked before the work starts.

And keep the rule for yourself as well as for the client. Never start a large job for a PO client without the number in writing. Never invoice a PO client without quoting it. Never do extra work on a PO job without a revised PO. Three boring rules, all cheaper than discovering three weeks later that the payment clock never started.

The pattern underneath

A purchase order is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It turns a promise into an approved spend, and it is why the biggest, slowest clients can also be the most reliable payers. The freelancer who asks for the number before starting is not being difficult. They are being paid on time. The freelancer who invoices first and asks later is the one waiting weeks for an approval that should have existed before the work began. Ask the question at kickoff, read the document when it arrives, quote the number exactly, and the PO becomes proof, in writing, that the money is already there.
