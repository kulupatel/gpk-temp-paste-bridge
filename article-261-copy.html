The Client Who Asks for a Pro Forma Invoice First: How to Use It Without Letting It Become the Payment

There is a request that arrives before the work starts, or at the very beginning of it, and it sounds routine, and it sounds professional, and it is, in most cases, exactly that: "Can you send a pro forma invoice first?" Or: "We need a pro forma before we can raise the PO." Or: "Finance needs a pro forma to approve the budget." The request is normal, it is common with larger clients, and it is, in itself, no problem at all, and a freelancer who refuses it will look difficult, and a freelancer who sends it without understanding it will discover the problem later, because a pro forma invoice is not an invoice, and a client who treats it like one, or a freelancer who treats it like one, will both end up confused about what is owed, when it is owed, and what has actually been agreed, and the confusion is where the payment gets lost, because a pro forma that is mistaken for a real invoice is a real invoice that never gets sent, and a real invoice that never gets sent is a payment that never arrives, and the freelancer who understands the difference can use the pro forma exactly as it is meant to be used, as a tool that gets the work approved, and then send the real invoice, on time, with the real due date, and get paid.

The first thing to understand is what a pro forma invoice actually is, because the name hides it. A pro forma invoice is a quote that looks like an invoice. It has the same shape, the same lines, the same amounts, the same tax, and the same total, and it is used to show a client, or a client's finance team, exactly what the real invoice will say, before the work is done, so the client can approve the budget, raise a purchase order, or get the spend signed off. What it is not, and this is the part that matters, is a request for payment, and it is not a record of a debt, and it does not create a due date, and it does not start a payment clock, and a freelancer who sends a pro forma and then waits to be paid is a freelancer who is waiting for a payment that nobody owes yet, because nobody owes anything on a pro forma, and the client knows it, and the finance team knows it, and the only person who might not know it is the freelancer, and that is the trap, and the trap is worth naming plainly, because it is the trap that costs freelancers months.

The second thing to understand is why the request is so common with larger clients, because it is common for a good reason, and the freelancer who understands the reason can work with it, instead of against it. A large client does not pay a freelancer the way a small client does. A small client reads an invoice and pays it, from their own account, in days. A large client has a process, and the process has steps, and the steps have owners, and the owners have forms, and the forms need numbers, and the numbers have to match, and the pro forma is the document that carries the numbers through the process, from the freelancer, to the project manager, to the finance team, to the approver, to the purchase order system, and each step takes days, and the whole process takes weeks, and the freelancer who sends the pro forma is not waiting to be paid, the freelancer is waiting for the process to finish, and the process finishes with a purchase order, and the purchase order is the document that says the work is approved, and the work is approved, and the freelancer can start, and the real invoice, sent when the work is done, is the document that gets paid, and the pro forma was never the payment, it was the permission, and a freelancer who treats it as permission, and not as payment, is a freelancer who gets paid, because they send the real invoice, on time, with the real due date, and they chase it, on the real schedule.

1. The rules: what a pro forma is, and what it is not

There are four rules that govern a pro forma invoice, and a freelancer who follows all four will never lose a payment to one, and a freelancer who breaks any one of them will discover the breakage months later, when the payment does not arrive, and nobody can say why.

The first rule is that a pro forma is a quote, not a demand. It should say so, on the document itself, in plain words, at the top, in the title, and in the body, because a document that looks like an invoice and does not say it is not one will be treated like one, by the client, by the finance team, and by the freelancer's own records, and a document that is treated like an invoice, but is not one, is a document that creates confusion, and confusion is where payments get lost. The title should read "Pro Forma Invoice," not "Invoice," and the body should say, in one sentence, "This is a pro forma invoice for budget approval and purchase order purposes, and it is not a request for payment," because that sentence is the protection, and it protects the freelancer, and it protects the client, and it protects the finance team, and it protects the record, and a freelancer who writes that sentence on every pro forma has never lost a payment to a confusion about what the document was.

The second rule is that a pro forma has no due date. A real invoice has a due date, because a real invoice is a demand for payment, and a demand needs a deadline, and a pro forma is not a demand, so it has no deadline, and a freelancer who puts a due date on a pro forma has created a document that looks like a demand, and a client who sees a due date on a pro forma will either treat it as a demand, and pay it, which is a payment for work that has not been done, and that creates a new problem, or will treat it as a mistake, and ignore it, and the freelancer who put the date on it will have created a date that nobody respects, and a date that nobody respects is a date that damages every date after it, because the client learns that the freelancer's dates are not real, and a freelancer whose dates are not real is a freelancer who does not get paid on time. The pro forma should carry the validity period instead, "This quote is valid for 30 days," which is a deadline for the price, not for the payment, and that is the right deadline, because it protects the freelancer's price, and it does not pretend to be a payment deadline, and a client who sees it understands it, and a finance team that sees it understands it, and nobody is confused.

The third rule is that a pro forma is numbered differently from a real invoice. A real invoice has a number, and the number is sequential, and the number is the record, and a pro forma that carries a real invoice number is a pro forma that has stolen a number from the sequence, and a sequence with a gap in it is a sequence that cannot be audited, and a freelancer who has to explain a gap in their invoice numbers to a client, or to an accountant, or to a tax authority, is a freelancer who has created a problem for themselves, and the problem is avoidable, because a pro forma should carry its own numbering, "PF-2026-001," or "PROFORMA-014," or any sequence that is visibly different from the real invoices, so the record shows, at a glance, which documents were quotes, and which were demands, and nobody has to explain anything, and the freelancer who does this has a record that is clean, and a clean record is a record that gets paid, because it is a record that can be proved.

And the fourth rule is that a pro forma is never entered as income, and never chased. A freelancer who enters a pro forma into their accounts as if it were a real invoice has invented income that does not exist, and a freelancer who chases a pro forma as if it were overdue has invented a debt that does not exist, and both inventions create the same problem, which is that the freelancer's own records say they are owed money that nobody owes them, and the freelancer who believes their own records will chase a client for a payment that is not due, and the client will be confused, and the confusion will damage the relationship, and the relationship is the thing the freelancer needs, because the relationship is what gets the real invoice paid, and the freelancer who keeps the pro forma out of the income, and out of the chase, and in its own place, as a quote, is a freelancer whose records are true, and whose chase is aimed at real debts, and whose clients are not confused, and that is the freelancer who gets paid.

2. The reply: send it, and say what happens next

The reply to a pro forma request is simple, and it has three jobs: to agree, to send it, and to say what happens next, because the request is not the problem, the problem is what happens after it, and a reply that says what happens next is a reply that prevents the problem.

The reply that does all of this sounds like this:

"Of course, I have attached a pro forma invoice for the work we discussed. It shows the full scope, the fee of 2,400, and the payment terms, so your finance team has everything they need to raise the purchase order. Once I have the purchase order number, I will confirm the start date, and I will send the real invoice when the work is delivered, with payment due 14 days after that. Please let me know if finance needs anything else from me."

Notice what that message does. It agrees, immediately, with no hesitation, because the request is reasonable, and a freelancer who agrees to a reasonable request is a freelancer who is easy to work with, and easy to work with is a quality that gets a freelancer hired again. It states what the pro forma is for, budget approval and a purchase order, so the client knows the freelancer understands the process, and the client knows the freelancer is not confused about what the document is. It names the next step, the purchase order number, so the client knows what the freelancer is waiting for, and the freelancer knows what to chase, because the chase after a pro forma is a chase for a purchase order, not a chase for a payment, and a freelancer who chases the right thing is a freelancer who gets it. And it states what happens after that, the real invoice, sent when the work is delivered, with payment due 14 days after that, so the client knows the real invoice is coming, and knows when it is due, and knows the terms, before the work even starts, and there is no surprise later, and no confusion about the date, and no version of "I never received it," because the client was told, in advance, when the real invoice would arrive, and what its terms would be.

Notice what it does not do. It does not say "please pay this," because a pro forma is not payable, and asking for payment on a document that is not payable is a mistake that confuses everyone, and a confused finance team is a finance team that does nothing, and nothing is the worst outcome, because nothing means no purchase order, and no purchase order means no work, and no work means no payment. It does not say "let me know when you have paid," because nobody is going to pay a pro forma, and a freelancer who asks that question is a freelancer who does not understand their own document, and a client who sees that will lose confidence, and confidence is the thing that gets a freelancer paid, because a client pays a freelancer they trust, and a freelancer who does not understand their own invoice is a freelancer who cannot be trusted with the money. And it does not leave the next step unstated, because a reply that says "here is the pro forma" and nothing else is a reply that leaves the client to guess what happens next, and a client who guesses will guess wrong, and the wrong guess is that the pro forma is the invoice, and the invoice is the payment, and the payment is due, and the freelancer who says what happens next has prevented that guess, and the prevention is the whole point of the reply.

There is one practical point about the reply, and it is about the document itself. The pro forma should be a PDF, with the words "Pro Forma Invoice" at the top, in the title, and the sentence about it not being a request for payment in the body, and the validity period, "valid for 30 days," and the pro forma number, in its own sequence, and the scope, the fee, the tax, the total, and the payment terms that will apply to the real invoice, so the client can see, in one document, everything they need to approve, and the freelancer can see, in one document, everything they agreed, and the document is the record, and the record is the protection, and the protection is what makes the real invoice, when it arrives, a document the client has already seen, already approved, and already budgeted for, and a document that has already been approved is a document that gets paid.

3. The three traps: the payment that never comes, the PO that never arrives, and the scope that grows

There are three traps in a pro forma request, and each one is quiet, and each one is designed to look like process, and each one costs the freelancer money, and each one has an answer.

The first trap is the payment that never comes, because the freelancer never sends the real invoice. This is the most common trap, and it is the simplest, and it happens like this: the freelancer sends the pro forma, the client approves it, the work starts, the work finishes, the freelancer waits to be paid, and the payment does not arrive, because the freelancer never sent the real invoice, because the freelancer thought the pro forma was the invoice, and the client thought the pro forma was a quote, and both of them were waiting for the other to do something, and neither of them did, and the money sat nowhere, because no document demanded it, and the freelancer who discovers this, months later, has to send the real invoice then, and the client is surprised, and the finance team is confused, and the payment takes another month, and the freelancer has lost three months to a document they misunderstood. The answer is the rule: a pro forma is never the invoice, and the real invoice is sent when the work is delivered, on the day it is delivered, with the due date stated, and the freelancer who writes that into their own process, pro forma for approval, real invoice on delivery, never waits for a payment that nobody owes, because they have sent the document that creates the debt, on the day the debt is created, and the chase starts on the real schedule, and the payment arrives on the real date.

The second trap is the purchase order that never arrives, because the freelancer never chases it. A pro forma is sent for a purchase order, and a purchase order is a document the client has to create, and the client has to create it through their own process, and their own process takes time, and the freelancer who sends the pro forma and then waits, silently, for the purchase order, is a freelancer who is waiting for a process they cannot see, and the process will take as long as nobody chases it, which is forever, because a process that nobody chases is a process that nobody prioritises, and the freelancer who chases the purchase order, politely, on a schedule, is the freelancer who gets it, and the chase sounds like this: "Just checking in, has finance been able to raise the purchase order for the pro forma I sent on 4 March? I am ready to start as soon as I have the number." That is not a payment chase, it is a process chase, and it is a chase the freelancer is entitled to make, because the freelancer sent the document the process needed, and the process is now the client's job, and the freelancer who chases it, once a week, in writing, in the same thread, is the freelancer who gets the purchase order, and the purchase order is the permission, and the permission is the work, and the work is the payment, and the chain starts with a chase that is not about money, but about a number.

The third trap is the scope that grows, because the pro forma described one thing, and the work became another. A pro forma that says "website redesign, 2,400" and a project that becomes "website redesign, plus three landing pages, plus a logo refresh, plus a month of revisions," is a project where the pro forma no longer matches the work, and the real invoice, when it arrives, will not match the pro forma, and the finance team will reject it, because the numbers do not match, and the client will say "that is not what we approved," and the freelancer will say "but you asked for more work," and the argument will be about the scope, and the scope is the thing the pro forma was supposed to fix, and the freelancer who lets the scope grow without a new pro forma has let the protection lapse, and the answer is to treat every scope change as a new pro forma, or as a written variation, with the new amount, and the new total, and the client's written agreement, before the extra work starts, because a variation agreed in writing is a variation that gets paid, and a variation agreed in a call, or in a message that says "sure, add it in," is a variation that will be disputed, and a disputed variation is a variation that does not get paid, and the freelancer who sends a new pro forma for the new scope, with the new total, and gets the client's written agreement, has protected the payment, and the protection is the document, and the document is the record, and the record is what gets paid.

4. When the client treats the pro forma as the invoice

Sometimes the client, or the client's finance team, treats the pro forma as if it were the real invoice, and pays it, or tries to pay it, or records it as a debt, and the freelancer has to be able to handle that, because a payment on a pro forma is a payment for work that has not been done, and a payment for work that has not been done is a deposit, and a deposit is a different thing from a payment, and the freelancer who treats it as a payment has made a mistake, and the freelancer who treats it as a deposit has handled it correctly.

If the client pays the pro forma, the reply is simple, and it has three parts: thank them, name it as a deposit, and state what happens next. The reply sounds like this: "Thank you, I have received 2,400. I am treating this as a deposit for the work, and I will send the real invoice when the work is delivered, with this amount credited against it, so there will be no double payment. I will confirm the start date now, and the work will be delivered by 28 March." Notice what that does. It thanks the client, because the client has paid, and a client who has paid deserves thanks, and thanks are the cheapest goodwill there is. It names the payment as a deposit, so the record is clear, and the client knows the freelancer understands what happened, and the finance team knows the freelancer understands what happened, and nobody is confused. And it states what happens next, the real invoice, with the deposit credited, so there is no double payment, and no argument about it later, because the freelancer has said, in writing, in advance, exactly how the deposit will be treated, and the client has that message, and the message is the record, and the record is the protection.

If the client, or the finance team, records the pro forma as a debt, and then disputes the real invoice, because the numbers do not match, or because they say they have already paid, the reply is also simple, and it has the same three parts: state the facts, quote the document, and name the next step. The reply sounds like this: "The document I sent on 4 March was a pro forma invoice, marked as such, and it said it was not a request for payment. The real invoice is 52, for 2,400, sent on 28 March, and it is due on 11 April. The pro forma was for budget approval, and the real invoice is the payment request, and they are two different documents, for two different purposes." Notice what that does. It states the facts, the pro forma was marked, and it said what it was, and the freelancer can quote the document, because the document says it, in plain words, at the top, and in the body, and a client who is shown their own document, with the words on it, has no argument left, because the document is the evidence, and the evidence is the freelancer's. And it states the real invoice, with the number, the amount, and the date, so the conversation is about the real debt, and the real debt has a deadline, and the deadline is 11 April, and the payment is the matter.

5. The pro forma as a tool, not a delay

A pro forma invoice, used correctly, is a tool, and it is a tool that helps the freelancer, because it gets the work approved, and it gets the purchase order raised, and it gets the budget signed off, and it gets the freelancer into the client's process, and a freelancer who is inside the client's process is a freelancer who gets paid, because the process is what pays, and the process needs numbers, and the pro forma carries the numbers, and the freelancer who sends it is the freelancer who has done the client's admin for them, and the client is grateful, and gratitude is a quality that gets a freelancer hired again, and paid on time.

The way to use it as a tool is to send it fast, because a pro forma that takes a week to send is a pro forma that delays the purchase order by a week, and the purchase order delays the work by a week, and the work delays the real invoice by a week, and the real invoice delays the payment by a week, and a freelancer who sends the pro forma the same day it is requested has saved a week, and a week is the difference between a payment in March and a payment in April, and the freelancer who saves a week is the freelancer who gets paid a week earlier, and a week earlier is a week of cash flow, and cash flow is the business.

The second way to use it as a tool is to make it complete, because a pro forma that is missing the tax, or the payment terms, or the scope, is a pro forma that the finance team will send back, with questions, and the questions will take days, and the days will delay the purchase order, and the delay will delay the work, and the work will delay the payment, and the freelancer who sends a complete pro forma, with the scope, the fee, the tax, the total, the payment terms, the validity period, and the sentence that says it is not a request for payment, has sent a document that the finance team can approve without asking a single question, and a document that needs no questions is a document that moves, and a document that moves is a document that gets approved, and an approved document is a purchase order, and a purchase order is work, and work is payment.

And the third way to use it as a tool is to follow it up, because a pro forma that is sent and then forgotten is a pro forma that sits in an inbox, and an inbox is where documents go to be forgotten, and a freelancer who follows up, once a week, in writing, in the same thread, asking for the purchase order number, is a freelancer who keeps the document moving, and a document that keeps moving is a document that gets approved, and the follow-up is not a chase, it is a reminder, and a reminder is a service, and a service is what a freelancer sells, along with the work, and the freelancer who reminds is the freelancer who is remembered, and the freelancer who is remembered is the freelancer who gets paid.

6. Prevention, and the process that never loses a payment to a pro forma

The prevention is the process, and the process is simple, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the process that freelancers skip most often, and it is the process that would have stopped most pro forma problems before they started.

The first prevention is the template, a pro forma template that already says "Pro Forma Invoice" at the top, and already says it is not a request for payment in the body, and already carries its own numbering, and already has a validity period instead of a due date, and already has the payment terms that will apply to the real invoice, so the freelancer does not have to remember any of it, and does not have to build it each time, and does not have to make a mistake, because the template remembers, and the freelancer just fills in the numbers, and a freelancer with a template is a freelancer who never sends a pro forma that looks like a real invoice, and never loses a payment to a confusion, because the confusion was designed out, in advance.

The second prevention is the checklist, a short list that the freelancer runs through before sending any pro forma, and the list is this: does it say "Pro Forma Invoice" at the top, does it say it is not a request for payment, does it carry a pro forma number, not a real invoice number, does it carry a validity period, not a due date, does it show the scope, the fee, the tax, and the total, and does it say what happens next, the purchase order, the start date, and the real invoice on delivery. A freelancer who runs that checklist, every time, in thirty seconds, has never sent a pro forma that caused a problem, because the checklist catches the problem before the document is sent, and a problem caught before it is sent is a problem that never happens, and a problem that never happens is a payment that never gets lost.

The third prevention is the diary, a note in the freelancer's own diary, or tracker, on the day the pro forma is sent, that says "Pro forma sent 4 March, chase purchase order 11 March, real invoice on delivery," because a pro forma that is sent and then forgotten is a pro forma that never becomes a purchase order, and a purchase order that never arrives is work that never starts, and work that never starts is a payment that never arrives, and the freelancer who writes the follow-up into their diary, on the day the pro forma is sent, is a freelancer who follows up, and a freelancer who follows up is a freelancer who gets the purchase order, and the purchase order is the permission, and the permission is the work, and the work is the payment, and the chain is unbroken, because the diary kept it unbroken.

And the fourth prevention is the habit, the habit of sending the real invoice on the day the work is delivered, without waiting, without asking, without delaying, because the real invoice is the document that gets paid, and the pro forma was the document that got the work approved, and the two documents have two different jobs, and a freelancer who does both jobs, on time, every time, is a freelancer who never loses a payment to a pro forma, because the pro forma did its job, and the real invoice did its job, and the payment followed, on the real date, and the record is clean, and the client is not confused, and the freelancer is paid.
