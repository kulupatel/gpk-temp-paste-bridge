Late Payment and Your Taxes: How Unpaid Invoices Affect What You Owe

Here is a painful detail most freelancers discover too late: the tax treatment of an unpaid invoice depends on how you report your income, and getting it wrong can mean paying tax on money you never received.

This is general information, not tax advice. Tax rules differ by country and change over time, so run your situation past an accountant before making decisions. But the concepts below will help you ask that accountant the right questions.

Cash basis vs accrual basis: the core split

There are two main ways to recognize income, and they treat unpaid invoices completely differently.

On the cash basis, you report income when the money actually arrives. An unpaid invoice does not exist for tax purposes until it is paid. This is simpler, it matches your bank account, and it is how most solo freelancers file. The downside: unpaid invoices are invisible, which can make your real losses harder to document.

On the accrual basis, you report income when you invoice, regardless of when the client pays. This means a $5,000 unpaid invoice counts as $5,000 of taxable income, even though your bank account says otherwise. Larger businesses and some jurisdictions require this method. If that sounds unfair, it is, which is why the bad-debt rules exist.

Most freelancers can choose. If you are on cash basis, late payments cost you cash flow but not phantom tax. If you are on accrual, you need the next section.

Bad-debt deductions: the fix for accrual filers

If you report on the accrual basis and a client never pays, most tax systems let you claim the unpaid amount as a bad debt. The general pattern: the debt must be genuinely uncollectible, you must have made real efforts to collect, and you document both.

"Real efforts" is exactly what the rest of this business is about. Your escalation emails, the final notice, the small-claims filing, all of it is not just collection work. It is the paper trail a bad-debt claim requires. Keep one file per dead invoice: the contract, the invoices, every chase message with dates, and the final decision to write it off.

Timing matters too. You generally claim the deduction in the year the debt becomes worthless, not the year you invoiced. That is another reason the 30/60/90-day decision discipline pays off: it gives you a clean date when "unpaid" became "uncollectible."

The year-end planning angle

December is when unpaid invoices become a tax question, not just a cash-flow one. Before the year closes, sort your receivables into three piles: likely to pay, being chased with a plan, and dead.

The dead pile is where tax planning lives. If you are on accrual, writing off the dead pile before year-end can reduce your taxable income for the year. If you are on cash, there is no deduction, but the review still matters: every dead invoice is a process failure to fix before January.

One caution: do not manufacture write-offs. A debt you could still collect with one phone call is not a bad debt. The rule rewards honest documentation, not creative accounting.

Cash-basis freelancers: your version of the problem

If you file on cash basis, unpaid invoices do not inflate your tax bill. But they create a subtler problem: expense timing. You already paid for the software, the subcontractor, the ads that won the project. Those expenses hit this year's return while the income may never arrive.

The fix is operational, not fiscal. Tighten payment terms (deposits, milestones, Net 7) so your income lands in the same year as your expenses. And track your real collection rate: total collected divided by total invoiced, per year. If it drops below the high nineties, your terms are too loose, whatever your tax method.

The takeaway

Taxes do not cause late payment, but they shape how much it costs you. Cash-basis filers feel the cash-flow pain; accrual filers feel phantom income until they document a proper write-off. Either way, the defense is the same disciplined system: document every chase, decide at 90 days, write off honestly at year-end, and tighten terms every January. Your accountant handles the forms. The system is yours to build.
