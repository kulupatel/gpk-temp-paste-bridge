The Client Who Sends Payment to the Wrong Account: How to Recover a Misdirected Payment

The client says they paid. You check your account, and the money is not there. You check again the next day, and it is still not there. The client sends a screenshot that shows a payment went out, and the amount is right, and the date is right, but the account number on the screenshot is not yours. It is an old account you closed, or a mistyped digit, or another freelancer's details from a copied invoice. The money has left the client, and it has not reached you, and both of you are now staring at the same gap from opposite sides.

A payment sent to the wrong place is not a payment. That is the rule this article works from, and everything else follows from it. The invoice is still unpaid until the money reaches you, the client still owes you, and the work of fixing the mistake belongs to the person who made it, which is usually the client, with your help. Stay calm, stay factual, and work the steps in order. Most misdirected payments are recovered in days, and the ones that are not usually turn out to be something else entirely.

1. Confirm the facts before you blame anyone

The first step is not to chase the money. It is to find out exactly where it went, because "I paid" and "you were paid" are two different claims, and the screenshot is the evidence that separates them. Ask the client for three things, in writing: proof of the payment, meaning the transaction confirmation or bank receipt, not a message saying it was sent; the exact account or email the payment was sent to, copied from the payment record rather than typed from memory; and the date and reference the payment carries.

Then compare those details against your own invoice. Is the account number on the payment the one on your invoice, or is one digit different? Is the email address yours, or is it an old address you stopped using? Is the amount exactly the invoice amount, or is it short by a fee or a rounding difference? Is the reference the invoice number, or something vague like "freelance work" that could belong to anyone? Most misdirected payments reveal themselves in this comparison within minutes: a transposed digit, an old account, a payment sent to a different supplier with a similar name.

Keep your tone neutral through all of this. "Thanks for sending that through. The account on the receipt does not match the account on my invoice, so the payment has gone somewhere else. Let me check my side, and could you check with your bank what happened to it?" You are not accusing the client of lying. You are reading two documents side by side and reporting what they say, and that is a much stronger position than an argument about who is telling the truth.

2. A payment that has not reached you is not a payment

This is the point clients resist most, and it is the point you must hold. From the client's side, the money has left their account, so in their mind the job is done. From your side, the money has not arrived, so the invoice is unpaid. Both of those things are true at the same time, and the second one is the one that matters for your books, your cash flow, and your right to keep chasing.

Say it plainly and without apology. "I can see the payment left your account, and I believe you sent it. But it has not reached the account on my invoice, so the invoice is still outstanding until the money arrives or is recovered." That sentence is not a threat. It is a description of where the money is, and it keeps the responsibility where it belongs: with the person who sent the payment to the wrong place, until the payment is put right.

What you should not do is mark the invoice paid, or pause your chasing, while the money is missing. An invoice marked paid is an invoice you will never chase again, and a client who sees you treat a missing payment as settled will not hurry to fix it. Keep the invoice open, keep the due date visible, and keep the follow-up going on the original timeline. The payment is not late because the client is difficult. It is late because it went to the wrong place, and it stays late until it reaches the right one.

3. Get the client to recall or redirect, with you helping

Once the facts are clear, the fix is usually straightforward, and the client is the one who has to start it, because only the sender can ask their bank to recall a payment or trace where it went. Ask the client to contact their bank or payment provider and request a recall or a trace, and to send you the reference number for that request in writing. A recall of a bank transfer to a wrong account often succeeds if the receiving account has not spent the money, and a trace will at least confirm where the money is sitting.

If the payment went to an account you used to own, contact your old bank yourself and ask whether the payment arrived there and whether it can be forwarded or returned. If it went to a payment app under a mistyped email, the money may be sitting unclaimed, and the provider may return it to the sender automatically after a set period. If it went to another person entirely, that person is the one who has to return it, and the client's bank is the one who has to ask them, which is slower, but it is still the client's bank's job, not yours.

While the recall is running, agree a fallback in writing. "If the recall has not landed by Friday, please send a fresh payment to the correct account on the invoice, and we will treat the recalled amount as a duplicate if it turns up later." That gives the client a deadline, gives you a date to chase against, and stops the situation drifting while everyone waits for a bank to reply. A misdirected payment with no deadline becomes a story the client tells themselves about a problem that is being handled. A misdirected payment with a Friday deadline becomes a payment.

4. Make the next payment impossible to misdirect

Once this one is resolved, fix the cause so it never happens again, because misdirected payments almost always come from the same few sources: bank details that changed without the client being told, details copied from an old invoice, or details typed by hand from a phone call. Each of those has a simple fix, and all of them belong on your side of the process.

Put your payment details on every invoice, in the same place, in the same format, every time, so the client never has to hunt through old emails for them. If your bank details change, tell every active client in writing before the next invoice goes out, and ask them to confirm they have updated their records. Never give payment details over the phone, where a digit can be misheard; send them in writing, in a format the client can copy and paste, so a typing error is the client's copy, not your dictation. And for a first payment from a new client, ask them to confirm the details back to you before they send it: "Could you confirm the account number you have on file matches the one on the invoice?" One confirmation message prevents a week of tracing.

If you use a payment link or a platform invoice, the same rule applies in reverse: make sure the link on the invoice is the current one, and test it yourself before you send the invoice out. A broken or outdated payment link is the same problem as a wrong account number, and it is one you can catch in thirty seconds before the invoice ever reaches the client.

5. Know when a misdirected payment is really a stall

Most misdirected payments are honest mistakes, and the client who made one will usually help fix it quickly, because they want the invoice closed as much as you do. But a small number are not mistakes at all. They are stalls dressed up as errors, and you can tell the difference by what happens after you ask for the proof.

A client who made an honest mistake will send the proof quickly, contact their bank, give you the recall reference, and meet the fallback deadline. A client who is stalling will do none of those things. They will say they paid and refuse to show the receipt. They will promise to check with the bank and never come back with an answer. They will send a second payment to the wrong place, or claim the bank needs more time, or stop replying altogether. Each of those is a signal, and the signal is that the payment was never really sent, or was never meant to reach you.

When you see that pattern, stop treating it as a banking problem and start treating it as a late payment, which is what it is. Go back to your normal escalation: a firm written note naming the invoice, the amount, and the date, with a clear statement that the invoice remains unpaid until the money reaches the account on the invoice, and a pause on further work until it does. Do not let a story about a wrong account number buy the client another month, because a client who can produce a screenshot of a payment to the wrong account can also produce a payment to the right one, and the fact that they have not is the answer.

The pattern underneath

A payment that has not reached you is not a payment, no matter how sincerely the client believes they sent it. Confirm the facts from the proof, hold the invoice open until the money arrives, get the client to start the recall and agree a fallback date, make your payment details impossible to misdirect next time, and recognise the stall when the proof never arrives. The money is either on its way back or it was never really sent, and the steps above will tell you which one it is within days.
