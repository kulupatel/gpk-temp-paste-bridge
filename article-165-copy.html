The Client Who Asks You to Start Before the Deposit Clears: How to Hold the Line on Deposits

The brief is exciting, the client is enthusiastic, and the deposit is "on its way." They ask you to start now, just a little, just the first draft, and the money will land by Friday. Friday comes, the money has not, and you have already done a week of work for a client who has not yet paid anything. Starting before the deposit clears is how freelancers turn a paid engagement into an unpaid audition. The client is rarely trying to trick you. They are usually just eager, or disorganised, or used to suppliers who start on a promise. But eagerness is not payment, and the freelancer who holds the line on deposits does it with a rule that is simple enough to say out loud: no cleared deposit, no work started, no exceptions that are not written down.

1. Say the rule before the client asks to break it

A deposit rule only works if the client hears it before they need to break it. At the point of agreement, say it plainly: work starts when the deposit clears, and the start date moves if the deposit moves. Put it in the same message as the fee and the timeline, so it is part of the deal, not a special condition invented later. When the client then asks you to start early, you are not refusing a reasonable request. You are pointing at a term they already accepted, and the conversation stays easy: happy to start the day it clears, and here is what the timeline looks like from that date. Freelancers who never state the rule upfront end up negotiating it in the worst possible moment, when the client is already excited and the refusal feels personal. Stated upfront, it is a term. Stated after the ask, it is an argument.

2. Offer a small, paid first step instead of a free one

The client who wants you to start early usually wants one thing: proof that the project is moving. Give them movement that is paid for, not free work dressed up as a favour. A paid discovery call, a paid audit, a paid first milestone that is small enough to approve quickly: any of these lets the client see progress without you working unpaid. The key is that the first step is a deliverable with its own price and its own invoice, not a slice of the main project given away early. When the deposit for the main project clears, the project starts in full. If the client will not pay even for a small first step, that tells you everything you need to know about how the main deposit will go, and you have learned it before doing a week of free work. A paid first step is not a compromise on your rule. It is your rule, in a form the client can say yes to today.

3. Never let "the deposit is sent" replace "the deposit cleared"

"Sent" and "cleared" are different words, and the gap between them is where unpaid work lives. A client can honestly believe they sent a payment that is stuck, delayed, or sent to the wrong details, and a freelancer who starts on "sent" is working on a promise, not on money. Your rule should name the event that starts work: the deposit clearing in your account, not the client pressing send. When a client says it is sent, the reply is warm and simple: great, I will start the day it lands, and I will confirm the moment it does. Check the account, confirm promptly when it arrives, and start immediately, so the client learns that cleared money means instant momentum. Clients who experience that once rarely ask you to start on a promise again, because they have seen that payment and progress are linked, and that the link works in their favour too.

4. If you ever make an exception, write it down and cap it

There will be a client worth an exception: a long-standing client, a referral from someone you trust, a project with a hard deadline that cannot move. Make the exception, but make it a written, capped exception, not a quiet slide into unpaid work. Write it in one message: starting early as a one-off, the deposit is due by a named date, work pauses on that date if it has not cleared, and the early work is limited to a named first deliverable. A cap matters more than the exception itself, because an uncapped exception is just the rule disappearing. With a cap, the client knows the boundary, you know the limit of your exposure, and if the date passes unpaid, the pause is not a surprise. It is the term you both agreed to. Exceptions made this way do not erode the rule. They prove the rule exists, because even the exception has a date and a limit.

5. The pause is the rule working, not the relationship failing

The hardest moment is not refusing to start early. It is pausing work when a deposit that was promised still has not arrived, and the client is unhappy about it. Remember what the pause is for. It is not a punishment, and it is not the end of the relationship. It is the mechanism that keeps a small problem small: a few days of waiting, instead of a month of unpaid work and a resentful freelancer. Tell the client that, in those words, when you pause: work resumes the day payment clears, the timeline moves by the same number of days, and nothing already delivered is lost. Most clients pay within days of a pause, because the pause makes the cost of delay visible to them, not just to you. The ones who do not pay have shown you, cheaply, that the project was never going to be paid for on time, and you have learned it after a capped amount of work, not after the whole project.

The pattern underneath

Deposits are not paperwork. They are the first test of whether a client pays, and the freelancer who starts only on cleared money passes that test before doing any work. State the rule upfront, offer a paid first step, treat cleared as the trigger, cap every exception in writing, and let the pause do its job.
