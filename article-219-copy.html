The Client Who Approves Your Work Verbally, Then Denies It: How to Make a Yes Stick

The call goes well. The client says the work looks great, says to go ahead, says the invoice will be fine. You deliver, you invoice, and then the reply arrives: "I never approved that." Suddenly the work you did on a spoken yes is being treated as work you did on your own initiative, and the invoice is being treated as a surprise.

A verbal approval is real in the moment and useless later, because memory is unreliable and incentives change. When payment is due, the version of the conversation that helps the client is the version they remember. Your job is not to stop taking approvals by phone or video call. Your job is to turn every spoken yes into a written record before you act on it, so the approval survives the moment it was given in.

1. Treat a verbal yes as a draft, not a decision

When a client approves work on a call, your next sentence should be a confirmation, not a celebration. Say it plainly before you hang up: "Just to confirm, you are approving the homepage copy as delivered, and I will invoice the final milestone on that basis." That sentence does two things. It gives an honest client a chance to correct a misunderstanding while correction is still cheap, and it gives a less honest client much less room to rewrite the conversation later.

Then send the same confirmation in writing within the hour, while the call is still fresh for both of you. A short email is enough: "Confirming our call today: you approved the homepage copy as delivered, and I will send the final invoice for the agreed amount this week." Do not wait until the next day. Memory fades fastest in the first hours, and a confirmation sent the same day reads as routine admin. A confirmation sent a week later reads as a freelancer building a case, even when it is exactly the same email.

2. Never start the disputed work before the written confirmation lands

This is the rule that costs freelancers the most money when they break it. The client said yes on the call, so you start the extra pages that evening, and the confirmation email sits unanswered. By the time the client replies, the work is done, and the client can now negotiate from a position where the work already exists.

Instead, make the written confirmation the starting gun. "I will start the extra pages once you reply to this confirmation" is not bureaucratic. It is the cheapest protection you own. If the client replies quickly, you have lost nothing. If the client does not reply, you have learned something important before spending hours: the approval was softer than it sounded. A client who genuinely approved the work will confirm in one line. A client who will not confirm is telling you the yes was never firm, and you should not build on it.

3. Put the approval in the invoice itself

Your invoice is not just a payment request. It is a record of what was agreed, and it should carry the approval with it. Instead of invoicing a bare amount, write the line item as a fact: "Final milestone, homepage copy approved on the call of 12 November, as confirmed by email the same day." That single line changes the conversation if payment stalls. The client is no longer disputing a vague invoice. They are disputing a dated approval they were given a chance to correct, and most clients will not do that in writing.

Keep the same habit for every approval, not just the big ones. A short note in your own records, with the date, the call, and the exact words used, is worth more than a perfect memory. When a client says "I never approved that," your answer should never be "Yes, you did." It should be "Here is the confirmation I sent that day, which you did not correct." The first answer is an argument. The second is a record, and records end arguments.

4. When denial happens anyway, answer with the record, not with heat

If a client denies a verbal approval despite your confirmation, do not escalate the tone. Reply once, calmly, with the paper trail attached: the confirmation email, the delivery date, and the invoice line that references both. Ask one direct question: "Can you point to what changed between the approval on 12 November and today, so I can address the specific concern?" That question forces the conversation back to facts. A client with a real concern will name it, and you can fix a real problem. A client using denial as a delay tactic will struggle to answer, and the struggle itself usually ends the tactic.

Do not offer a discount to make the denial go away. A discount in this situation teaches the client that denying an approval earns a lower price, and the next approval will be denied for the same reason. If there is a genuine defect in the work, fix the defect and say so. If there is no defect, hold the invoice at the agreed amount and move to the next stage of your chase sequence. The approval record is your leverage, and giving it away for a quick partial payment wastes it.

5. Build approvals into the next agreement

The lasting fix is a clause, not a habit you have to remember under pressure. Add one sentence to your terms: approvals given on calls take effect when confirmed in writing, and work proceeds after that confirmation. Clients rarely object to this sentence, because it sounds like good admin, which is what it is. It also removes the argument entirely. Nobody has to debate what was said on a call, because the agreement already says the written confirmation is the approval that counts.

Add a second habit for larger projects: a short approval log. One line per milestone, with the date, the approver, and the confirmation email it links to. Share it with the client at the end of the project. Clients who see their approvals listed calmly, with dates, almost never dispute them, because disputing a log means disputing their own inbox. You are not building a legal weapon. You are building a shared memory, and shared memories get paid.

The pattern underneath

A verbal yes feels like trust, and it often is. But trust without a record is a gift to the version of the client who exists on payment day, not the version who existed on approval day. Confirm every spoken yes in writing the same day, start the work only after the confirmation, carry the approval in the invoice, answer a denial with the record rather than with anger, and write the confirmation rule into your next agreement. Used that way, a phone call stays the fast, human way to approve work, and the email that follows it is what makes the approval real.
