The Client Whose Payment Is Stuck in Their Finance Team: How to Get Past the Approver You Never Meet

Your contact approved the work, praised the delivery, and said the invoice is in. Weeks later, nothing has arrived. Every follow-up gets the same reply: finance is processing it, it is in the queue, someone will get to it. You cannot argue with a queue, and you cannot charm a department you have never spoken to. This is one of the most common late-payment traps for freelancers, because the person you have a relationship with is not the person who releases the money, and the person who releases the money has no relationship with you at all. Getting paid here is not about being firmer with your contact. It is about moving your invoice from an anonymous queue into a named person's hands, with a reference number, a date, and a next step that someone owns.

1. Stop chasing your contact and start asking for the process

When payment stalls inside a finance team, your contact usually cannot speed it up, and repeated reminders only make them dread your messages. Change the question. Instead of asking when you will be paid, ask how their payment process works: who in finance handles supplier payments, what reference number your invoice carries, whether a purchase order number is required, and which day of the month payments are released. These are factual questions, not pressure, so your contact can answer them without feeling blamed. The answers turn a vague stall into a map. If there is no purchase order number and finance requires one, you have found the blockage. If payments go out only on the 15th and you invoiced on the 16th, you have found the timeline. If nobody knows who handles your invoice, you have found the real problem: your invoice belongs to no one.

2. Get a named person and a reference number, in writing

An invoice in a general queue is easy to ignore. An invoice attached to a name and a number is much harder to lose. Ask your contact to introduce you, by email, to the accounts payable contact, and ask for the invoice reference or tracking number in the same message. Once you have a name, write to that person directly, briefly, with everything they need in one message: invoice number, amount, due date, purchase order number if there is one, and your payment details. Do not include the history of the project or your frustration with the delay. Accounts staff process documents, not stories, and a clean, complete message gets processed faster than a long complaint. Keep a note of the name, the date, and what they promised. From this point on, your follow-ups go to the named person, with the reference number in the subject line, so every message lands in the right file.

3. Find out what is actually missing

Most finance stalls have a boring administrative cause: a missing purchase order number, a bank detail form that was never completed, an invoice that does not match the approved quote, or a document sent to the wrong inbox. Ask directly: is anything missing from my invoice or my supplier record that is holding this up? This question works because it gives finance an easy way to help you. People would rather tell you about a missing form than defend a slow process. If something is missing, supply it the same day and confirm receipt. If nothing is missing, ask for the scheduled payment date in writing. A date changes everything. An invoice with a payment date is a plan. An invoice without one is a hope, and hopes do not get chased by finance teams.

4. Use your contact as an escalation route, not a messenger

There is a point where polite finance follow-ups stop working, and that is when your contact becomes useful again, in a different role. Instead of asking them to chase finance for you, ask them to escalate inside their own company: a short message from a project lead to a finance manager carries weight that ten freelancer emails do not. Frame the request as a business problem, not a personal favour: the invoice is past its agreed term, the project is complete, and you need their help getting it onto the next payment run. Give them the reference number, the amount, and the due date so they can forward one message without doing extra work. Most contacts will do this once, willingly, because an unpaid supplier is their problem too. If they will not, that tells you something important about how the client treats suppliers, and it is information to use when deciding whether to work with them again.

5. Build the finance step into the next engagement

The real fix for a finance stall happens before the next invoice, not after it. At the start of the next project, ask three questions: who is the accounts payable contact, is a purchase order number required, and what is the payment run schedule? Get the answers in writing, add them to your project notes, and send the first invoice with the purchase order number on it, to the right inbox, on the right day. Some freelancers also add a short line to their terms: invoices are due within the agreed term from receipt by accounts payable, not from project completion, which removes the grey area where finance claims the clock never started. None of this is confrontational. It is the same diligence any supplier brings to a new account, and clients who run proper finance processes will respect it. Clients who cannot answer these questions are telling you, in advance, that payment will be slow, and you can price or decline accordingly.

The pattern underneath

A payment stuck in a finance team is rarely personal and rarely malicious. It is an invoice without an owner, a number, or a date. Get the process mapped, get a named contact and a reference number, find the missing document, escalate through your contact when the queue stops moving, and build the finance step into the next engagement. The goal is not to fight a department you will never meet. It is to make your invoice the easiest one in their queue to pay.
