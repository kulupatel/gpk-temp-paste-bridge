The Payment Terms You Should Never Agree To

Most late-payment disasters are signed before the work starts. The payment terms sit in the contract, you skim them, you sign, and six weeks later you are chasing money under rules you agreed to. Here are the terms that should make you stop, renegotiate, or walk away.

1. Net 60 or Net 90 with no early-pay option

Sixty or ninety days to pay means the client uses you as an interest-free lender for two or three months. Large companies do this because vendors let them. You do not have to. Counter with Net 14 for new clients, or Net 30 at the absolute longest, plus a 2% early-pay discount for payment within 7 days. The discount reframes the deal: fast payment earns something instead of slow payment costing nothing. If the client genuinely cannot move faster than Net 60, raise your deposit so most of the money arrives before the wait begins.

2. "Paid when paid" clauses

This is the clause where the client pays you only after their own client pays them. You are a freelancer, not their bank's credit department. Your contract is with them, not with whoever owes them money. Their cash-flow problem is not yours to finance. Replace it with fixed terms: "Payment due Net 14 from invoice date, regardless of client's own receivables."

3. Payment "on acceptance" with no acceptance criteria

"Payment on acceptance of deliverables" sounds reasonable until you notice the contract never defines acceptance. No criteria, no deadline, no default acceptance after a set number of days. The client can stall forever by never formally accepting. Insist on three things: what counts as acceptance (a written sign-off or a defined review period), how long they get (say, 5 business days), and what happens if they go quiet (work is deemed accepted after the period). Silence becomes acceptance, automatically.

4. No late fee, or a late fee "at our discretion"

A contract with no late-fee clause removes your single cheapest enforcement tool. A clause that says late fees apply "at our discretion" is barely better: the client decides whether the rule applies to them, which means it never does. Your line: "Late payments accrue 1.5% interest per month after the due date." Stated before the work, in writing, it is a term. Raised after the invoice is late, it is a threat. Terms get paid; threats get argued about.

5. Set-off clauses that let them deduct "any amounts"

Watch for language like "we may deduct any amounts we believe are owed." That word "believe" lets the client reduce your invoice over a disputed revision, an alleged delay, or anything else, without your agreement. Counter with: deductions only for amounts both parties agree to in writing. If they want a dispute process, give them one, but never a unilateral discount.

6. Vendor-portal onboarding with a 30-day wait

Some enterprise clients pay only through a procurement portal that takes weeks to set up: registration, tax forms, banking verification, approval chains. If onboarding starts after the invoice, your payment clock starts a month late. The fix is scheduling, not refusal: complete the portal setup before the project starts, and write the start date into the contract. If the portal process will genuinely delay your first payment beyond your terms, raise the deposit to cover the gap.

How to push back without sounding difficult

None of this requires a fight. The phrase that ends most term disputes is: "These are my standard terms." Standard terms are not personal, not negotiable line by line, and not a judgment on the client. Send them with the proposal, before the work, every time. The client who balks at a 1.5% late fee in the proposal was going to be the client who paid 90 days late. The terms did not create the problem. They revealed it early, when it was cheap to learn.
