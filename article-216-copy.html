The Client Who Keeps Asking for a More Detailed Breakdown: How to Answer Without Restarting the Clock

The invoice is in, the work is approved, and then comes the request: "Could you break this down in more detail?" You send the breakdown. A week later comes another request: "Could you split this by day, or by task?" You send that too. Then a third: "Could you explain this line again?" Each request sounds reasonable on its own. Together they form a pattern: the payment clock keeps restarting, and the invoice never becomes due, because there is always one more detail missing.

This is the breakdown stall. Sometimes the client genuinely needs detail for their records, and a good breakdown solves it in one round. But when the requests keep coming, detail is not the problem. Delay is. Your job is to answer the first request fully, set a limit on the rounds, and refuse to let a new question move the due date. Detail is a service. Endless detail is a stall tactic wearing a polite face.

1. Send the full breakdown once, and send it complete

The first breakdown request is your best chance to end the pattern. Do not drip-feed detail across three emails. Send one complete breakdown that answers every question a reasonable reader could ask: each deliverable, the hours or units behind it, the agreed rate, the total, and the payment terms, all in one document. Attach it as a PDF and paste the summary into the email body, so nobody has to open a file to see the numbers.

Write the covering line carefully: "Here is the full breakdown. Everything on the invoice is itemised below, and the due date stays as stated on the original invoice." That last clause matters more than the breakdown itself. It states, before the next request arrives, that detail does not change the deadline. A client who needed detail for their books now has it. A client who wanted delay now knows the next request will not buy more time.

2. Answer the second request by pointing, not by rebuilding

The second breakdown request is the test. If you rebuild the breakdown from scratch, you have accepted that every question earns a new document, and there will always be another question. Instead, point. Reply with the existing breakdown attached again, and name the exact lines that answer the new question: "The day split you asked for is on page 2, lines 4 to 9. The task split is on page 1. Let me know if any figure there does not match your records."

Pointing is not rude. It is helpful, and it shows you have already done the work. It also creates a record: the detail was supplied, on a date, in full. If the client asks a third time for the same detail in a new shape, you can point again, and the pattern becomes visible to both of you. A client who keeps asking after two complete answers is no longer asking for information. They are asking for time, and your replies should stop giving it.

3. Put a number on the rounds

Unlimited rounds of questions are unlimited delay. So put a number on them, politely and early. After the second round, write: "I am happy to clarify any figure that does not match your records. After this breakdown, any further questions are best handled on a short call, so we can close the invoice out this week." A call is the right offer, because it ends the email chain. Stall tactics thrive in email, where each message buys days. A call forces a decision in minutes.

If the client refuses the call and sends a fourth written request, you have your answer about what is happening. Reply once more, briefly: "The breakdown supplied on [date] covers every item on the invoice. The invoice remains due on [date]. Please let me know if any figure is disputed, so we can resolve that specific item." The word disputed is doing quiet work here. It separates a genuine disagreement about a figure, which you will resolve, from a general request for more detail, which you will not keep feeding.

4. Never let a question move the due date

The breakdown stall works because each new question feels like it resets the conversation, and a reset conversation feels like it resets the deadline. It does not. The due date was set when the invoice was issued, and a request for detail does not pause it unless you agree that it does. So never agree, even by silence. Every breakdown email should restate the due date in plain words: "The invoice remains due on 17 November."

If the due date passes while questions are still coming, chase the payment, not the questions: "The invoice is now past its due date. The full breakdown was supplied on [date]. Please confirm when payment will be released, or tell me which specific figure is disputed so we can settle that item." This message refuses the premise that an unanswered question is a reason not to pay. Payment is due. A disputed figure can be settled alongside payment of the undisputed balance, which is the standard any accountant will recognise.

5. Write detail into the next quote, so the stall has nowhere to start

The best defence against a breakdown stall is a quote that already contains the breakdown. Before the next project, itemise the quote the way you would itemise the invoice: deliverables, units, rates, totals, payment terms. When the client approves that quote, they have already approved the breakdown. A later request for more detail then has a short answer: "The breakdown matches the approved quote, attached."

This also protects you with new clients. A client who wants an unitemised lump sum quote and then demands a forensic breakdown at invoice time is showing you their payment style before you start. Offer the itemised quote happily, and watch the reaction. Clients who accept it are clients who pay. Clients who resist it are telling you, in advance, how the end of the project will go.

The pattern underneath

A breakdown request is fair once and a stall when it repeats. Answer the first request completely, answer the second by pointing at what you already sent, cap the rounds with a call, never let a question move the due date, and itemise the next quote so the stall has nowhere to start. Detail serves the client when it closes the invoice. When it keeps the invoice open, it is not detail any more. It is delay, and the correct response is a date, a pointed answer, and a payment chase that does not wait for the next question.
