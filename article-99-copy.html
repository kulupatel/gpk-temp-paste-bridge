The 'Under Review' Stall: How to Get an Invoice Out of Approval Limbo

You sent the invoice, the client confirmed they received it, and then nothing. When you ask, the answer is always the same: "It's under review." Weeks pass. "Still under review." Nobody can tell you who is reviewing it, what they are reviewing, or when the review ends. "Under review" is not a status; it is a parking lot. Here is how to get your invoice towed out of it.

1. Ask for the name, not the status

"Under review" is a process answer designed to end the conversation. Your reply should restart it with a name: "Thanks, could you tell me who is reviewing it? I'd like to make sure they have everything they need." An approval process has an approver; a real one has a name. If your contact can name the person, you now have a target and a timeline. If they cannot, you have learned something important: there is no review happening. The invoice is simply sitting somewhere, and your contact is avoiding the awkwardness of admitting it. Either way, the name question moves you forward.

2. Pre-arm the approver at kickoff

The cheapest way to beat approval limbo is to prevent it before the project starts. At kickoff, ask three questions and write down the answers: who approves invoices, how long does approval normally take, and what format do they need? One freelancer habit that pays for itself: send a short note to the named approver before the first invoice goes out. "Hi, I'm the designer on the Q4 project. My first invoice will land around the 15th; here is what it will look like." When the invoice arrives, it is expected, not a surprise. Surprises go under review. Expected invoices get approved.

3. The weekly nudge that names the blocker

Once an invoice is parked, vague follow-ups ("Just checking in!") get vague answers. Name the blocker instead: "Hi, my invoice #1042 from March 3 is with [Name] for review. Is there anything missing from my side that is holding it up?" This does two things. First, it forces your contact to check with the actual person rather than repeating the status. Second, it creates a paper trail where the delay has a name and a date attached to it. Do this weekly, same day, same thread. Predictability is pressure: your contact learns that every Monday there will be a message with a name in it, and the fastest way to stop the messages is to get the approval done.

4. Go around the bottleneck, not the person

If the named approver is unresponsive after two nudges, do not go over your contact's head in a way that embarrasses them. Instead, ask your contact for help going sideways: "Would it help if I sent the invoice package directly to [Name]? Sometimes having the invoice, the contract, and the delivery confirmation in one email makes the review faster." You are offering to do their job for them, which most contacts accept gratefully. The "package" matters: invoice PDF, the signed agreement, and proof of delivery in one email. Reviewers stall when they have to hunt for context. Hand them everything in one place and the review has nothing left to review.

5. Set the review deadline in your next contract

For the next engagement with this client, put the review window in writing before work starts: "Invoices are due within 14 days of receipt. If an invoice is under review for more than 7 business days, please let me know who is reviewing it and what is outstanding." This sounds formal, but it is just a question asked early instead of late. Clients who intend to pay will not object; the clause only threatens the ones who use "under review" as an indefinite delay. One line in the terms, and the parking lot gets a tow-away zone.

The pattern underneath

"Under review" persists because nobody owns it. Every tactic above does the same thing: attach a name, a date, or a deadline to the delay. Processes move when people are accountable. Your job is to make the invoice someone's specific problem, politely, every week, until it is paid.
