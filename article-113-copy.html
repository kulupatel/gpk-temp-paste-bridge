The Milestone Payment: Getting Paid Before the Project Is Done

Waiting until the end of a project to get paid is one of the riskiest choices a freelancer can make. You do the work, hand over everything, and then hope the client pays. If they do not, you have lost the work and the money, and your only leverage is gone with the delivered files. Milestone payments fix this by splitting the project into paid stages. Here is how to set them up so you get paid as you go.

Why end-of-project billing fails

A single final invoice concentrates all your risk in one moment. The client has the finished work, so the pressure to pay comes only from goodwill and follow-up emails. Meanwhile you have already spent the hours. Milestones flip this: the client pays at each checkpoint, so you never have more than one stage of work exposed at a time. This is not about distrust. Agencies, builders, and enterprise software vendors all bill in stages. It is how professionals work.

Keep it to three milestones

Do not create eight milestones for a small project; that is admin work nobody wants. Three is the sweet spot for most freelance engagements: a kickoff payment to start, a midpoint payment when the core work is done, and a final payment on delivery. A common split is 30/40/30 or 40/30/30. The kickoff payment matters most. A client who will not pay 30 percent up front is telling you something before you have done any work, which is the cheapest time to learn it.

Define what each milestone means

Vague milestones cause fights. "Phase 1 complete" means different things to you and the client. Define each milestone with one concrete, observable event: "Design concepts delivered," "First draft of all 12 pages sent," "Final files transferred." Write these into the quote or contract before work starts. When the milestone event happens, the invoice goes out the same day. No event, no invoice; no invoice, no payment. The clarity protects both sides.

Tie the next stage to the last payment

This is the rule that makes milestones work: work on the next stage begins after the previous milestone is paid, not when it is invoiced. State this in writing at the start: "Each phase begins on receipt of the previous milestone payment." Most clients respect this automatically once it is stated. For the ones who do not, the rule gives you a calm, contractual reason to pause. You are not refusing work; you are following the schedule you both agreed to.

What to do when a milestone payment stalls

Sometimes a milestone invoice goes unpaid and the next phase is waiting. Do not start the phase. Send one short message: "Just checking on invoice #X for the kickoff milestone, due [date]. Once it clears I will start [next phase] right away." This reframes the pause as logistics, not conflict. If 7 days pass with no response, call. If 14 days pass, treat it as a red flag about the whole project, not just the invoice. A client who stalls on milestone one will stall on milestone three too, and you want to know that while you still hold undelivered work.

Small projects get mini milestones

Milestones work for small jobs too. A $600 blog package can be 50 percent to start and 50 percent on delivery. A $1,500 website can be 40/30/30. The principle scales down: never do 100 percent of the work before receiving any money. The only exception is tiny repeat-order clients with a perfect payment history, and even there a deposit keeps the habit healthy.

Put the schedule in the contract

One sentence in your agreement makes all of this enforceable: "Payment is due in three milestones: [X] percent on signing, [Y] percent on [event], [Z] percent on final delivery. Work pauses if any milestone payment is more than 7 days overdue." That sentence does three jobs: it sets the schedule, it gives you the pause right, and it makes the client take the terms seriously from day one. Most payment problems are really clarity problems, and this sentence removes the ambiguity.
