The Client Who Cancels Mid-Project and Refuses the Kill Fee: How to Enforce the Cancellation Clause

The project was halfway done when the message arrived: priorities changed, the work was cancelled, and the client expected to pay only for the hours already logged, if that. The kill fee in your terms, the fee that covers the booked time, the turned-down work, and the half-finished deliverable, was dismissed as unfair. This is the moment a cancellation clause either earns its keep or turns out to have been decoration.

A kill fee is not a penalty. It is the price of reserving your calendar and doing work that cannot be sold to anyone else. When a client cancels mid-project, they are not just stopping future work. They are leaving you with a gap you cannot fill at short notice and a deliverable that has value to no one but them. The clause exists to make that cost visible before the project starts, not to win an argument after it ends.

1. Know what the clause actually says

Before you reply to a cancellation, read your own terms. Many freelancers discover at this exact moment that their kill fee is vague: a percentage with no base, a trigger with no date, or a clause that was never in the signed agreement at all. If the fee is 50 percent of the remaining fee on cancellation after the first milestone, say so, with the number. If it is a flat fee, name it. Vagueness here is not a negotiating tactic. It is the reason the client feels entitled to argue.

If no kill fee exists in the signed terms, be honest about that too. You cannot enforce a clause the client never agreed to, and pretending otherwise will cost you more credibility than the fee is worth. In that case, your claim is for work completed plus demonstrable costs of the cancellation, and you should frame it exactly that way: hours delivered, files produced, and bookings turned down that you can name.

2. Answer with the work, not with the clause

The first reply should not open with the contract. It should open with what was done. List the completed milestones, the drafts delivered, the meetings held, and the next milestone that was already in progress. Then state the amount owed under the terms, broken into work completed and the cancellation fee, as two separate lines. A client who sees a single lump sum reads a punishment. A client who sees completed work plus a named fee reads a bill.

Keep the tone factual and short. Something like: "Confirmed, the project stops here. Work completed to date is [amount]. Under section [number] of our agreement, cancellation after the first milestone carries a fee of [amount], covering the reserved time through [date]. Total due is [amount], payable by [date]. I will hand over all completed files on receipt." No anger, no lecture about professionalism, no threat. The facts carry the weight.

3. Hold the handover until payment clears

The half-finished deliverable is your leverage, and it is also the thing the client most wants. Do not hand over source files, working drafts, or passwords before the cancellation amount clears. This is not hostage taking. It is the same rule as any other invoice: payment first, release second. State it plainly in the cancellation reply so it is a policy, not a surprise later.

If the client argues that they already paid for the work in progress, answer with the record: what was paid, what it covered, and what remains. A deposit covers the first milestone, not the whole project, and saying so with dates and amounts ends most arguments. If they paid nothing yet, the handover rule matters even more, because the files are the only thing standing between a cancelled project and an unpaid one.

4. Offer one clean exit, not a negotiation

Some clients refuse the fee on principle and will pay a smaller number to make the problem go away. You may choose to accept a reduced settlement, but only if it is a settlement: a single payment, a fixed date, and a written close that says the account is settled in full. Never accept a vague promise of future work in place of the fee. A client who cancelled once will cancel the replacement project too, and you will have traded a real claim for a second disappointment.

If the client will not pay anything, escalate on the same ladder as any unpaid invoice: a firm written notice with a pay-by date, then a final notice, then the small-claims or collections route your jurisdiction allows. The cancellation does not change the ladder. It only changes the first letter, which should reference the clause and the work completed rather than a missed due date.

5. Write the next clause before you need it

Every cancelled project teaches the same lesson, and the fix belongs in the next agreement, not in the argument. A strong cancellation clause names four things: the trigger (written notice from either side), the fee (a percentage of the remaining fee, or a flat amount, tied to the project stage), the handover rule (files released on payment), and the payment deadline (for example, 14 days from the cancellation notice). One sentence each is enough.

Add a second line that most freelancers skip: cancellation does not cancel invoices already issued. Without it, a client can cancel and claim the outstanding invoice died with the project. With it, the completed work stays payable on its original terms, and the kill fee is a separate, visible line. Clients who read that clause before signing rarely argue about it after cancelling, because they agreed to it when the project felt exciting rather than expensive.

The pattern underneath

Cancellation disputes are rarely about the money alone. They are about a client who wants the cost of changing their mind to be zero, and a freelancer who absorbed that cost silently until now. The fix is not a harder personality. It is a clause written before the work starts, a first reply that lists completed work before naming the fee, a handover held until payment clears, and a settlement that closes the account in writing. Name the price of stopping early, and the clients who respect it will never test it.
