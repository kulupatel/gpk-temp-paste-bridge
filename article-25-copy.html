The Fake Client Playbook: How to Spot a Client Who Never Intended to Pay

Most late payers are real clients with bad habits: disorganized, cash-strapped, or strategic about who they pay first. They are worth chasing, managing, or firing on your terms.

But there is a smaller, nastier category: the client who never intended to pay at all. These are not late payers. They are scams wearing a project brief. The good news is that fake clients repeat the same patterns, and once you know the playbook, you can spot it before you do a single hour of work.

1. No verifiable footprint

A real business leaves traces: a working website with real content, company registration you can look up, staff with real profiles, clients you can name. A fake client has none of this. The website is a one-page template. The email is a free personal address with no company domain. They refuse video calls, always with a plausible excuse.

Before you start any project over a few hundred dollars, spend ten minutes verifying. Search the company name plus the word "scam" or "reviews". Check the domain registration date. Look at whether their "team" exists anywhere. Ten minutes of skepticism now is cheaper than forty hours of unpaid work.

2. Rush plus an unusually generous budget

"Urgent project, big budget, need someone who can start today." The rush is the weapon. Urgency is designed to bypass your normal vetting: no contract review, no deposit conversation, no questions about the brief. A generous budget sweetens it so you feel lucky instead of suspicious.

Real clients with real urgency still sign contracts and pay deposits. The fake ones treat your paperwork as an insult. When speed is the reason to skip every protection you have, that is the signal, not the project.

3. They refuse deposits, contracts, or any paperwork

"Let's keep it simple." "We don't do contracts for small projects." "Just start and we will sort out payment after." Every version of this sentence means the same thing: they want nothing in writing that obligates them to pay.

A legitimate client might negotiate terms. They do not refuse terms entirely. No contract, no deposit, no milestone schedule: these are not simplifications. They are the whole scam.

4. Everything moves off email to chat apps

They found you on a marketplace or by email, but within two messages they want to move to Telegram, WhatsApp, or some chat app where messages can be deleted and nothing is archived. This is partly about dodging platform protections and partly about making the paper trail disappear.

Keep all project communication on email or the platform's messaging system. If a client insists on moving off the record before you have even started, ask yourself what record they are afraid of.

5. The overpayment trick

This one is a classic and it still works. The client "accidentally" sends too much, often by check or a payment that takes days to clear, then asks you to refund the difference or forward it to their "supplier." The original payment eventually bounces. The refund you sent was real money.

The rule is absolute: never refund an overpayment until the original funds have fully cleared and are irreversible. And never forward money to a third party on a client's behalf. You are a freelancer, not a bank.

6. The scope bait and switch

The tiny paid test project goes perfectly. Then comes the "big project": ten times the size, strangely vague on payment terms, starting immediately. The test built your trust, and the trust is being spent on the scam.

Treat every new project as a new client. Same deposit, same contract, same milestones. Trust earned on project one does not transfer to project two.

What to do when you spot the pattern

If two or more of these signals are present, slow down, do not speed up. Ask for a video call. Insist on a contract with your payment terms. Require a deposit before you start, with milestones and payment between them for anything large. A real client finds this professional. A fake client disappears, which is the best possible outcome: they just saved you weeks of unpaid work.

For deliverables, watermarks on drafts and staged delivery with payment between stages mean the worst case is a lost milestone, not a lost project. And if money has already changed hands in a scam, report it: to the platform, to your bank, and in many countries to the relevant fraud reporting service. You will probably not get the money back, but the report makes the next scam harder.
