The Client Who Offers More Work Later Instead of Paying Now: How to Price a Promise

The offer arrives dressed as good news. The client cannot pay the invoice in full this month, but they have three more projects lined up, and if you are flexible now, there will be plenty of work later, and the later work will be paid promptly, and the relationship will be worth far more than this one invoice. It sounds like a partnership. It feels like the client is offering you something, when in fact they are asking you to do something very specific, which is to accept a promise in place of a payment, and to fund their cash flow with your unpaid work until the future they describe arrives.

The promise of more work is the oldest currency in freelancing, and it is worthless as a payment method for a simple reason: it cannot be spent, it cannot be enforced, and it is made by the same person who is currently not paying you. A client who is offering future work in place of present payment is telling you two things at once. The first is that they value your work enough to want more of it. The second is that they do not have the money, or the will, to pay for the work they already have. The first is flattering. The second is the one that decides what happens to your invoice.

That does not mean the future work is a lie. Sometimes it is real, and sometimes it arrives, and sometimes it pays. But a promise that might arrive later is not a substitute for money that is due now, and the freelancer who treats it as one has traded a certain debt for an uncertain hope, and has done it on the client's terms, at the moment when their bargaining position was strongest, which was before they agreed.

1. Why the promise feels so convincing

The promise works because it plays on the two things every freelancer wants most: security and growth. A pipeline of future work sounds like security, and a client who wants to give you more work sounds like growth, and the two together make the unpaid invoice feel like an investment rather than a loss. The client is not asking you to write off the money. They are asking you to wait for it, and to keep working while you wait, and the waiting is presented as a short bridge to a better arrangement.

It also works because it flatters the relationship. The client is telling you that you are their freelancer, that they have chosen you, that the future belongs to the two of you. Refusing the offer feels like refusing the relationship, and freelancers are rightly reluctant to do that, because relationships are the business, and a freelancer who turns down every client who is late once will soon have no clients at all.

And it works because it is vague. "More work later" has no date, no scope, no price, and no penalty for not arriving, which means it can never be broken in a way you can point to. A client who promised more work and never sent it has not broken a promise in any sense you can chase. They have simply changed their mind, or their budget, or their plans, and the invoice you waited on is still unpaid, and the future you waited for is still future.

The way to see through the promise is to ask what it would be worth if you had to sell it. Nobody would buy a promise of more work from a client who is currently late paying, because the promise is only as good as the person making it, and the person making it is currently demonstrating that their payment word is not reliable. That is not a judgement on their character. It is arithmetic. A promise from a late payer is worth less than a promise from a prompt payer, and a promise from a prompt payer is still not a payment.

2. The test: would you take this deal from a stranger

The fastest way to judge the offer is to strip away the relationship and ask whether you would accept it from a client you had never met. A stranger owes you $2,400, and offers you no money now, but promises three more projects later, each worth about the same, and asks you to keep working in the meantime. Would you take that deal? Almost nobody would, because the stranger has given you no reason to trust the promise, and the promise is the only thing being offered.

The relationship does change the answer, but it changes it by a little, not by a lot. A client who has paid you on time five times and is late once has earned some trust, and a short wait for a client with a clean record is a reasonable courtesy. But a short wait is a week, or a named date, not an open-ended arrangement, and it is a wait with a payment date attached, not a wait with a promise of more work attached. The difference is the date. A date is enforceable. A promise is not.

So the test has two parts. First, what is the client's record: how many times have they paid on time, and how late is this invoice, and how much is it. Second, what is the client actually offering: a date by which the invoice will be paid, or a hope that more work will appear. If the offer is a date, you can work with it. If the offer is a hope, you should not, because a hope is not a payment plan, and a payment plan is what an unpaid invoice needs.

There is a third part to the test, and it is the one freelancers skip most often. Ask yourself what you are giving up by waiting. While you wait for this client to pay, you are not chasing, you are not pausing work, you are not taking the next step, and the invoice is getting older, and older invoices are harder to collect. The promise costs you the escalation time, and escalation time is the one thing you cannot get back, because a client who is 90 days late is a different problem from a client who is 14 days late, and the difference is the two months you spent waiting for a promise.

3. The answer that keeps the work and the money

The answer that works is the one that accepts the future work and refuses to trade it for the present invoice, and it works because it gives the client everything they actually offered, while keeping your payment terms intact. The sentence is short: "I would love to do the next projects. This invoice is due now, and I can start the next one once it is paid."

That sentence does three things. It says yes to the future work, so the client hears a yes, not a rejection. It names the invoice as due now, so the payment is not postponed by the conversation. And it links the future work to the present payment, so the promise and the payment are no longer separate things: the client gets the next project when the current one is paid for, which is exactly how a healthy relationship works.

If the client genuinely cannot pay in full now, agree a payment plan, and agree it with a date, not a hope. "Half this Friday, half in two weeks, and the next project starts when the first half arrives." That is a plan. It has dates, amounts, and a consequence, and it lets the client keep working with you while they catch up, without letting the debt grow. What you should never agree to is a plan that says "pay when the next project starts", because that is the promise again, with a different name, and the next project will start, and the invoice will still be unpaid, and you will be one project deeper into the same trap.

And put it in writing, in the same message, because a verbal agreement about a late invoice is a conversation, and a written agreement is a record, and the record is what you will quote when the date arrives and the money has not. "To confirm: $1,200 by Friday 14 November, $1,200 by Friday 28 November, and the next project starts once the first payment has arrived." That message takes thirty seconds to write, and it is the difference between a plan and a hope.

4. When the future work is real, and how to take it safely

Sometimes the future work is real, and the client is genuinely lining up more projects, and the right move is to take them, because a growing client is the best kind of client. The way to take them safely is to change the terms for the future work, so that the problem that created this invoice cannot repeat itself.

The first change is the deposit. A client who is late on the current invoice pays a deposit on the next project, before the work starts, and the deposit is not negotiable, because the record is the reason. That is not a punishment. It is a term that matches the risk, and a client who wants the future work will accept it, because they want the work more than they object to the deposit.

The second change is the milestone split. Instead of one invoice at the end of the next project, agree two or three smaller invoices, each due on delivery of a stage, so that no single invoice is ever large enough to hurt if it goes late. A client who pays in stages is a client whose lateness costs you a stage, not a project.

The third change is the pause clause, and it should be in the agreement for the next project before the work starts: "Work pauses if any invoice is more than 14 days overdue." That sentence is the protection that the current project did not have, and it is the sentence that makes the future work safe to accept, because it gives you a way to stop before the next invoice joins this one in the unpaid pile.

And there is one more change, which is the hardest to make and the most important: do not start the next project until the current invoice is paid, or at least until the first payment of the agreed plan has arrived. A client who wants you to start the next project while the current invoice is unpaid is asking you to work on credit, and credit is a loan, and a loan to a late payer is a loan you should not make. The next project will still be there when the invoice is paid. The invoice will not be easier to collect after you have done more unpaid work for the same client.

5. The client who keeps promising and never pays

There is a version of this client who is not a good client having a bad month, and it is worth naming, because the treatment is different. This is the client who always has more work coming, who always mentions the next project when the invoice is due, who always has a reason why this month is tight and next month will be better, and for whom next month never arrives. The promise is not a plan. It is a habit, and the habit is the payment strategy.

You can recognise this client by the pattern, not by any single conversation. The invoice is always a little late. The promise is always a little vague. The next project is always just about to start. And the total owed grows, slowly, because each new project adds a new invoice before the old one is paid, and the client is always one project ahead of their payments, which means you are always one project behind your money.

The treatment for this client is not another conversation. It is a stop. Pause the work, send the final notice, and name the date by which the balance must be paid before any new work starts. Do not accept the promise of more work as a reason to keep going, because the promise is the mechanism by which the debt grows, and the only way to stop the debt growing is to stop the work. A client who is offended by a pause was never going to pay for the work anyway, and a client who pays to restart the work has just proved that the money was there all along.

And when the balance is paid, decide whether the client is worth keeping on new terms, with a deposit, milestone splits, and a pause clause, or whether the pattern is the client, and the right move is to finish the work, take the money, and let the future work go to someone else. A pipeline of promises is not a pipeline. It is a debt with a friendly name.

The pattern underneath

More work later is a promise, and a promise is not a payment, because it has no date, no amount, and no penalty for not arriving, and it is made by the person who is currently not paying you. Accept the future work, and refuse to trade it for the present invoice: name the invoice as due now, agree a payment plan with dates if the client cannot pay in full, and put it in writing in the same message. Take the future work only on new terms, with a deposit, milestone splits, and a pause clause, and never start it until the current invoice is paid. And when the promise is a habit, not a plan, stop the work, because the promise is the mechanism by which the debt grows. The future is worth having. It is not worth paying for with money you are already owed.
