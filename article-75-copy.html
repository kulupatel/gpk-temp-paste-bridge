Invoice Factoring for Freelancers: When Selling Your Unpaid Invoice Makes Sense

Invoice factoring means selling an unpaid invoice to a third party at a discount. They pay you most of the invoice now, chase the client themselves, and keep the difference. It sounds like magic for a freelancer staring at a 90-day-overdue invoice. Sometimes it is genuinely useful. Often it is a bad deal dressed as a solution. Here is the honest breakdown.

How it actually works

You have a $2,000 invoice that is 60 days overdue. A factoring company offers to buy it. The typical structure:

  • They advance you 70 to 90 percent of the invoice value up front (say 80 percent: $1,600).
  • They collect from the client.
  • Once collected, they take a fee, usually 1 to 5 percent of the invoice value per month it stays unpaid, and pass the remainder to you.
  • If the client never pays, what happens depends on the contract type: recourse factoring means the debt comes back to you; non-recourse means the factor eats the loss (and charges more for it).

The important part: factoring is not a loan against your invoice. It is a sale of the debt. You are trading a percentage of the money for speed and certainty.

When factoring is the right call

Factoring makes sense in exactly two situations:

1. The invoice is real but stuck in a slow corporate machine. Big companies with 90-day payment cycles and vendor portals will eventually pay, but your rent is due now. If the client is solvent and the invoice is undisputed, a factor will advance you 80 percent and you get on with your life. The fee is the price of liquidity, and sometimes that is a fair trade.

2. You would rather take a haircut than keep chasing. If an invoice has been stuck at 90-plus days, you have sent the escalation sequence, and the client is still responsive but slow, factoring converts an uncertain $2,000 in three months into a certain $1,600 this week. That is not failure. That is a pricing decision about your time and stress.

In both cases, the invoice needs to be undisputed. Nobody buys an invoice the client says is wrong. Factoring companies verify with the client before advancing money.

When factoring is a terrible idea

The invoice is disputed or the client is broke. No factor touches invoices where the client disputes the work or the client is visibly insolvent. If you cannot collect, neither can they, and they know it.

You are using it as a first resort. Factoring fees on a freelancers typical invoice size are steep. A 3-percent-per-month fee on a 60-day collection is 6 percent of the invoice, which annualizes to roughly 36 percent. As a routine cash-flow tool, it is wildly expensive compared to the structural fixes: deposits, milestone billing, and the two-invoice rule. Factoring treats the symptom; your payment terms treat the disease.

The contract is recourse and you did not notice. Recourse factoring means if the client does not pay within the agreed window, the factor returns the invoice to you and expects their advance back. You have paid a fee for a bridge loan with extra steps. Read the contract, and assume recourse unless the paperwork explicitly says non-recourse.

The cheaper alternatives, in order

Before you sell an invoice, run through this list:

  • The pause. Stop work until the oldest invoice clears. Free, immediate, and it works on solvent clients.
  • The demand letter. A formal written demand with a 10-to-14-day date, sent with proof of delivery, moves more invoices than any third party. It costs you an hour.
  • A settlement offer. Offering a client 10 percent off for payment this week beats factoring fees and keeps the relationship.
  • Small claims. Filing fees are modest in most places, and many clients pay the moment they are served. It is slower than factoring but you keep the full amount.
  • Factoring. The last resort for undisputed invoices from solvent-but-slow clients, when you need the money now.

The bottom line

Factoring is a legitimate financial tool, not a scam, and not a savior. It is a liquidity trade: you buy speed and certainty with a slice of the invoice. Used as a last resort on a real, undisputed invoice from a client who will eventually pay, it is reasonable. Used routinely, it is a tax on weak payment terms, and the terms are the cheaper thing to fix.
