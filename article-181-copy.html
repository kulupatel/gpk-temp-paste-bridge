The Client Who Pays the Wrong Amount by Mistake: How to Correct an Overpayment or Underpayment Without an Argument

The payment arrives, and the number is wrong. Not dramatically wrong, just wrong enough to matter. A client pays 850 against a 950 invoice, or 1,050 against a 950 invoice, and now you have a small problem sitting inside a relationship you want to keep. The temptation is to ignore a small shortfall, or to quietly pocket a small overpayment, and both temptations are mistakes. A shortfall you ignore becomes the new price, because the client will assume the amount they paid was the amount owed. An overpayment you keep becomes a debt you owe, and it will surface at the worst possible moment, usually when the client audits their books or asks for a refund with interest. A wrong amount is not a confrontation. It is a reconciliation, and reconciliations go smoothly when they are fast, factual, and boring. The client made an arithmetic mistake, or paid from an old quote, or transposed two digits. Your job is to show them the numbers, not to assign blame, and to fix the balance in the same message that reports it.

1. Check your own numbers before you contact the client

Before you tell a client they paid the wrong amount, make sure you are right. Open the invoice, the quote, and any agreed changes, and confirm the amount owed, the amount received, and the difference. Check for the innocent explanations first: a deposit already paid that the client subtracted, a discount you agreed in writing, a second invoice the client combined into one payment, or a bank fee the client deducted. If any of those explain the gap, there is no correction to make, and a message claiming a shortfall would damage your credibility. Do this check the same day the payment arrives, while the details are fresh and the client's own records match yours. A correction sent a week later, after the client has closed their books for the month, feels like a new claim, not a fix. A correction sent the same day feels like good bookkeeping, and clients respect good bookkeeping even when it asks them for more money.

2. Report the difference as a fact, not an accusation

The message that corrects a wrong amount should be the shortest message you send all month. State the invoice number, the amount invoiced, the amount received, and the difference, and then state what happens next. For a shortfall: the invoice was 950, the payment received was 850, the remaining balance is 100, due by a specific date. For an overpayment: the invoice was 950, the payment received was 1,050, the excess is 100, and you are holding it as a credit or refunding it, whichever you propose. No commentary on why it happened, no suggestion that the client was careless, no lecture about checking figures. Clients make payment errors for boring reasons, usually a mistyped digit or an old figure copied from an earlier email. A factual message lets them fix it without embarrassment, and a client who is not embarrassed pays the balance quickly. A message that implies carelessness makes the client defensive, and a defensive client starts looking for a reason the invoice itself was wrong.

3. For a shortfall, give a date and a simple way to pay the balance

A shortfall message without a date is a request the client can postpone forever, because the main invoice already feels paid in their mind. Give the balance its own due date, short and specific, ideally within seven days, and give the same payment details as the original invoice so there is no friction in paying it. Do not reissue the whole invoice unless your accounting needs it. A short balance note, or a line on your next statement, is usually enough, and it keeps the correction proportional to its size. If the shortfall is tiny, under the cost of chasing it, say so honestly and write it off in writing, with the record kept. A written write-off of a small amount is a decision, not a loss, and it is far better than silent resentment. But never write off a shortfall silently. A silent write-off teaches the client that the lower amount was correct, and the next invoice will be paid at the same lower figure.

4. For an overpayment, offer the choice and act fast

An overpayment belongs to the client until you return it or they agree to leave it as credit, and the longer you hold it without saying anything, the worse it looks. Tell the client the same day, offer the two honest options, and act on their answer within days. Option one is a refund of the excess to the original payment method. Option two is a credit against the next invoice, recorded in writing with the amount and the invoice it applies to. Most clients choose the credit, because it is less paperwork, and a credit has a side benefit: it ties the client to a next invoice. But never assume the credit. A client who wanted a refund and finds a credit on their account will feel their money was kept, and that feeling poisons an otherwise good payment. Refunds of overpayments are also the cleanest protection against a later claim, because the record shows you reported the excess immediately and returned it on their instruction.

5. Fix the cause so the next payment is right

A wrong amount that happens once is an accident. The same wrong amount twice is a system problem, and the system is usually yours. Look at what made the error easy: an invoice that lists the total in two places with different figures, a quote and an invoice that do not match, a deposit that is easy to forget, or payment details that invite the client to type the amount from memory. Fix the document, not the client. Put one total, in one place, in large type, on every invoice. Show deposits already paid as a line, so the balance due is unmistakable. When a client pays in instalments, send a short statement after each payment showing what remains. These are small formatting choices, and they prevent most payment errors before they happen. If a particular client keeps paying the wrong amount, add a confirmation step: a short message when the invoice is sent, stating the exact amount and the due date, so the client has one clean figure to copy. Prevention here is cheap, and it is much cheaper than correcting the same error on every invoice.

The pattern underneath

A wrong payment amount is a bookkeeping event, not a dispute, and it stays that way when you check your own figures first, report the difference as a fact, give a shortfall a date and an easy payment route, offer a real choice on an overpayment, and fix the document that made the error easy. The goal is not to win an argument about 100. It is to keep the ledger clean and the relationship intact, so that the next invoice is paid in full, on time, and for exactly the right amount.
