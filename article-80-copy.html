The Payment Contact Playbook: What to Do When Your AP Contact Leaves

Your invoice was approved, the payment was scheduled, and then your AP contact resigned. Three weeks later the invoice is "in the system" and nobody can find it. The company did not stiff you on purpose. The person holding your payment simply vanished, and your invoice vanished with them.

This happens constantly, especially with agencies and mid-size companies where one person runs accounts payable. Here is the playbook for getting your invoice back on the rails.

Why your invoice died with the contact

Invoices do not live in the company's official system the way you imagine. They live in a person's workflow: an email thread, a spreadsheet row, a Slack message. When that person leaves, your invoice becomes an orphan. Nobody inherits it, nobody knows it is overdue, and the new hire starts from a clean slate with no memory of your invoice at all.

The longer the gap, the worse it gets. After a month, the new contact treats your invoice as a cold claim they have never heard of. Your urgency reads as surprise. So the playbook starts the moment you hear the news, or better, the moment you notice silence.

Step 1: find the new owner fast

Do not email your old contact's address and hope. Ask for the replacement by name. Email the hiring manager or your original client contact:

"Hi [name], I heard [old contact] has moved on. Could you connect me with whoever now handles vendor payments? I have an outstanding invoice I want to make sure lands with the right person."

Keep it brief and assume nothing. Your goal is a name and a direct email, not a rehash of the history. One request, one day. If nobody answers in 48 hours, call. A departure window is the one moment everyone expects admin cleanup, so use it.

Step 2: re-send the invoice as a fresh package

Never forward a long thread with "following up on this." The new contact does not care about your history with their predecessor. Send one clean package:

  • The original invoice PDF, re-dated with the original invoice date (do not backdate or redated it to today; the due date stays the one you agreed)
  • A one-line summary: what the invoice is for, the amount, the original due date
  • Any proof of approval: the old contact's approval email, the PO number, the acceptance confirmation

Write it as if this is the first time anyone at the company has seen the invoice, because for this person, it is.

Step 3: get written confirmation of the new terms

This is the step everyone skips, and it is the one that prevents the problem from repeating. In the same email, ask three things:

  • Who is the billing contact going forward (name and direct email)?
  • What is the standard payment cycle from invoice receipt?
  • Is there anything else they need from you: vendor form, W-9, portal registration?

Get the answers in writing. You now have a fresh payment record with the new owner, which is stronger than whatever you had with the old one.

Step 4: chase like it is a new invoice

Reset your chase clock from the re-send date, but keep the original due date in the subject line. Your first follow-up goes out one week after the re-send, referencing the new contact by name. Do not apologize for re-sending. Companies restructure; invoices get lost. Nobody is surprised.

If the new contact stalls, escalate to your client sponsor, the person who hired you. One line is enough:

"Hi [name], my invoice is with [new AP contact] but has not moved in two weeks. Could you nudge from your side?"

The sponsor has internal authority you do not. Use it early rather than letting the invoice age into the forgotten pile again.

The prevention habit

From now on, never let a single person be your only payment link to a client. At kickoff, get two things: the AP contact AND the name of the person who approves their work (the budget holder). Save the invoice, the PO, and the approval in your own records, not just in the email thread.

People leave. Invoices should not leave with them.
