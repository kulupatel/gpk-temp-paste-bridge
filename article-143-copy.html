The Client Who Asks for a Discount After the Work Is Done: How to Hold Your Price

The work is delivered. The client is happy. Then, with the invoice already in their inbox, comes the message: "Could you do a bit better on the price?" This is not a negotiation. A negotiation happens before the work starts, when both sides can still walk away. A discount asked for after delivery is a test of whether your price was ever real, and every time you pass the test by cutting it, you teach the client that your quotes are opening offers. Here is how to hold your price without losing the client.

1. Recognise what the ask really is

An after-delivery discount request is almost never about the money. The client agreed to the price, received the work, and can afford it. What they are doing is checking whether the price moves, because if it moves once, it will move next time too. That is why the size of the requested cut barely matters. A 10 percent cut on this invoice becomes the starting price of the next project, and the next discount request starts from there. Seeing the ask clearly, as a precedent request rather than a one-off favour, makes the answer easier: you are not deciding about this invoice. You are deciding about every future invoice from this client.

2. Answer the work, not the price

The worst reply to a discount request is a counter-offer, because a counter-offer confirms that the price was negotiable all along. The best reply points back to what was agreed and delivered, without anger and without apology. "The quote was $X for the scope we agreed, and that scope is what was delivered. I am happy to discuss pricing for the next project before we start, but I cannot change the price of work that is already complete." This is not a confrontation. It is a reminder of a fact: the price was set, the work was done, the invoice matches both. Clients who were testing you usually pay at this point, because the test has an answer and the answer is no.

3. Never trade a discount for a promise

The discount request often comes dressed as a deal: a lower price now in exchange for more work later, a testimonial, a referral, or exposure. Do not take it. Future work that does not exist yet cannot pay for work that already does, and a client who needs a discount to promise future work will need another discount when that future work arrives. If the client genuinely wants a better rate for ongoing work, that is a separate conversation, held before the next project starts, with the volume or commitment written down. A testimonial is worth having, but it is not worth 10 percent of an invoice, and trading one for the other teaches the client that your prices are paid for in favours.

4. Offer something that is not money

Sometimes the client is not testing you. They are embarrassed about the budget, or their boss is pushing back, and they need something to show for the conversation. Give them something that costs you nothing and costs them nothing in price: a longer payment window of a week, a breakdown of the invoice by deliverable so they can justify it internally, or a small extra that you can offer once without setting a precedent, like a short follow-up review. These gestures let the client save face without you saving them money. The line to hold is simple: flexibility on everything except the number at the bottom of the invoice.

5. Change the next quote, not this invoice

If the same client asks for a discount twice, the problem is no longer the client. It is your pricing structure, because a price that gets challenged every time is a price that does not look justified. The fix belongs in the next quote, not in a cut to the current invoice: show the scope more clearly, break the price into parts the client can see, or add a deposit so the final invoice is smaller and less tempting to challenge. And if a client keeps asking after delivery despite all of this, the honest conclusion is that they are not a discount client, they are a client who does not respect agreed prices. The next quote goes up, not down, or the client goes on the list of people you no longer work with.

The pattern underneath

A discount asked for after the work is done is a request to rewrite history: to pretend the agreed price was a suggestion. It was not. It was the price, for the scope, delivered as promised. Answer the work, not the price. Never trade a cut for a promise. Offer flexibility everywhere except the total. And fix the next quote if the pattern repeats, instead of cutting the invoice in front of you. The client who pays your price once will pay it again. The client who gets a discount once will ask for it forever.
