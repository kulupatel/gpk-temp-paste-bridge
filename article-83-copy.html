The Paid Trial Trap: When the "Test Project" Never Turns Into Real Work

"Let us start with a small test project, and if it goes well, there is plenty more work." You have heard this sentence. Sometimes it is true. Often it is a polite way to buy your best work at a discount with no commitment behind it. Here is how to tell the difference, and how to structure the test so it cannot trap you.

The two kinds of test projects

Real test projects have three properties: a defined deliverable, a defined decision date, and a defined next step. "Design three homepage concepts by Friday; we decide Monday; the winner gets the full site build." That is a real test.

Trap test projects have none of these. "Do a quick mockup so we can see your style" has no deadline, no decision maker, and no promised follow-up. It is free spec work with the word "test" glued on. The absence of structure is the tell.

Cap the trial before it starts

Never start a test without writing down four things, even in a plain email:

  • Exactly what the test delivers (one page, three concepts, one week of ads, not "some designs")
  • What it costs (paid trials are always paid; the fee should cover your time, not just your costs)
  • When the decision happens (a real date, not "soon")
  • What happens next (the full project scope, timeline, and price, agreed in principle now)
"Happy to do the test: three homepage concepts, $600, delivered Friday, decision by next Wednesday, and the full build is the $4,000 scope we discussed if it moves ahead."

If the client will not agree to these four lines in writing, there is no test. There is a hope.

Price the test like real work

The classic trap is pricing the test as a loss leader: cheap enough to win, expensive enough to hurt. Then the "plenty more work" never materializes and you have done your best work for your worst rate.

Price the test at your normal rate. The test is not a discount for the promise of future work; the promise of future work is not money. If the client is serious, your normal rate is fine. If they flinch, they were buying cheap work, not testing a partnership.

The convert-or-close script

The decision date arrives and there is silence. Do not chase for another test, and do not start the full project on a verbal "looks great." Send one email:

"Hi [name], the test wrapped on Friday and the decision date was Wednesday. Should I hold next week for the full build, or should I release the slot?"

This is a close, not a nudge. It forces the decision they avoided. If they need more time, they say so and you have a new date. If the work was never real, the silence tells you, and you have lost one test fee instead of a full project.

The rule that prevents the next trap

Limit yourself to one test per client, ever. A client who needs a second test did not learn anything from the first. They learned that you will keep auditioning. Your portfolio, your references, and one paid test are enough evidence for any serious buyer.

And never do an unpaid test "because the client is big." Big clients have the largest budgets for paying test fees. The size of the client is a reason to charge, not a reason to work free.
