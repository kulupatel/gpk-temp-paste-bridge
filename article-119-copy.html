The Startup Equity-for-Work Trap: How to Say No Without Losing the Client

"How about equity instead of cash?" For a freelancer, that sentence is a payment problem wearing a pitch deck. Equity does not pay your rent, your tools, or your taxes. Yet saying no badly can cost you a client who might have paid just fine in cash. Here is how to handle the equity offer without a fight.

Why equity-for-work is almost always a bad deal

Equity in an early startup is a lottery ticket, not a wage. The company is worth zero today by its own books, the shares are illiquid, and your slice usually comes with a vesting schedule measured in years. Meanwhile your invoice is real, due now, and non-negotiable in the eyes of your landlord. A $5,000 invoice traded for "0.1% of a company we hope is worth millions" has an expected value close to zero: most startups fail, and the ones that succeed rarely make early advisors-turned-freelancers whole.

There is a second, quieter problem. Mixing work and equity muddies the relationship. Are you a contractor or a co-founder? If the client stops paying attention to your invoices because you are "one of us now," you have lost both the paycheck and the clarity. Contractors get paid on terms. Co-founders get diluted. Pick one.

The one situation where it can work

Equity can supplement cash, never replace it. If a startup client genuinely cannot pay your full rate but you believe in the business and want the relationship, the honest structure is: your normal rate, on your normal terms, minus a small agreed discount, with the remainder taken as equity on paper with a clear written agreement. Cash first, paper second. If they cannot afford your discounted rate in cash, they cannot afford you at all.

Even then, get three things in writing before you start: the exact number of shares or the percentage (not "we will figure it out later"), the vesting terms, and what happens to your equity if the engagement ends. If the founder will not put the equity offer in writing, they were never really offering equity; they were offering a discount.

Say no without burning the bridge

The decline script is short and warm:

"Thanks for thinking of me that way. I keep my work strictly cash, so my equity never complicates the working relationship. My rate is [amount], and my terms are [Net 14 with a deposit / milestone billing]. Happy to talk about scope so it fits your budget."

Notice what this does. It frames the refusal as a policy, not a judgment on their company. It declines the form of payment, not the client. And it immediately pivots to the real conversation: what scope fits their cash budget. Most founders accept this instantly, because it was never about the equity being fair; it was about cash flow.

If they push back ("but the equity could be worth so much"), hold the line kindly: "It might be, and I hope it is. But my business runs on invoices paid on time, and mixing the two is how freelancers end up working for free. Let us find the scope that works for cash."

Protect the next conversation

Some equity offers arrive after the work is done, attached to an unpaid invoice: "We are cash-strapped, how about shares instead of the invoice?" That is not an offer; that is a late-payment stall. Treat it like one. Your invoice is due on its terms. Renegotiating payment method after delivery is a discount you never agreed to, and you should answer exactly as you would answer any other late payment: with your escalation timeline, not a negotiation.

The pattern to watch for is the equity-first client: proposals that lead with upside, contracts with vague payment clauses, and a founder who seems annoyed that you keep asking about due dates. Vet these clients the same way you would any red-flag prospect: a clear deposit, short payment terms, and a stop-work clause. Startups that respect your terms usually turn into great long-term clients. The ones that do not respect them were never going to pay on time anyway, in cash or otherwise.
