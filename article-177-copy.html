The Client Who Wants a Discount After the Work Is Done: How to Hold the Agreed Price

The work is finished, the client is happy with it, and then comes the request: could you knock something off the price? It arrives after delivery, when the work is already in the client's hands, and it is framed as a small favour, a gesture, a way to keep the relationship good for next time. It is not small. A discount after the work is done rewrites the agreement retroactively, and it teaches the client that your price is an opening offer, not a price. The answer is not to argue about the value of the work. It is to hold the agreed price calmly, and to separate a genuine commercial conversation from a discount request that only ever goes one way.

1. Remember that the price was agreed before the work, not after it

The strongest fact in this conversation is also the simplest: the client agreed the price before you started, and you delivered what was agreed. Say that, plainly, without heat. The quote was this amount, for this scope, and the work delivered matches it. A discount request after delivery asks you to agree a new price for work that is already done, which is a strange negotiation, because the client already has the thing they are negotiating over. Clients who ask for a post-delivery discount usually know this, which is why the request is framed as a favour rather than a complaint. If there were a real problem with the work, they would say so, and a real problem gets a real fix. A discount with no complaint attached is not about the work at all. It is about seeing whether the price moves.

2. Do not justify the price with your costs or your time

The temptation is to defend the price by explaining your hours, your costs, or how hard the work was. Resist it. The moment you justify the price, you accept that the price was up for debate, and the debate then becomes about whether your hours were worth it, which is a debate you cannot win, because the client was not buying your hours. They were buying the outcome, at an agreed price, and they have it. Keep your reply to the agreement and the delivery: happy with the work, glad it is useful, and the invoice stands at the agreed amount. A short reply is stronger than a long one here. A long justification invites a counter-argument about every line in it. A short statement of the agreed price leaves the client with only one move, which is to pay it or to say plainly that they will not, and most clients will not say that about work they are already using.

3. Offer something that is not a price cut

If you want to keep the relationship warm, offer something that costs you little and does not touch the price. A small piece of advice, a short check-in after launch, a note on how to use what you delivered, or priority booking for the next piece of work. These gestures show goodwill without moving the number, and they give the client something to feel good about without teaching them that asking after delivery gets money off. What you must not offer is a discount on the next job as a consolation, because that simply moves the discount forward by one project and trains the same behaviour for next time. Goodwill is free to give when it is not a price cut. A price cut is never free, because it is remembered at the next quote, and the next quote starts from the discounted number, not the original one.

4. If a discount is ever genuinely right, make it a trade, not a gift

There are rare cases where a lower amount makes commercial sense, for example when the scope genuinely shrank during delivery, or when you want to convert a one-off client into a paid retainer. In those cases, never give the discount as a gift. Trade it for something of equal value, in writing: a shorter payment term, a public testimonial you can use, a retainer agreement signed now, or a scope reduction that matches the lower price. A traded discount is a business decision with a reason and a record. A gifted discount is a precedent with neither. If the client will not trade anything for it, that tells you everything about whether the discount was ever needed. It was not. It was a test, and the correct answer to a test is a calm no, not a concession dressed up as strategy.

5. Write the next quote so this conversation cannot happen again

The lasting fix sits in the next agreement, not in this argument. State the price, the scope, and the payment terms in writing before work starts, and add one plain line: the agreed price is fixed for the agreed scope, and any change to scope is priced before it begins. When that line exists, the next discount request answers itself, and you do not have to improvise a defence of your price after delivery. Clients who run real businesses will not object to a fixed-price line, because their own prices work the same way. Clients who object are telling you they expect the price to be negotiable after the work is done, which is exactly the arrangement the line prevents. Your price is not a starting point for a negotiation that happens after delivery. It is the agreement, and agreements are kept.

The pattern underneath

A discount after delivery feels like a small gesture, and functions like a rewritten agreement that follows you into every future quote. Hold the agreed price, do not justify it with your costs, offer goodwill that is not a price cut, trade rather than gift any discount that is genuinely right, and write the next quote so the conversation cannot recur. The goal is not to be rigid with a good client. It is to keep the price as the price, so that the next job starts from the number you agreed, not the number you were talked down to after the work was already in the client's hands.
