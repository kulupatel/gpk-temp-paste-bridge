The Client Who Wants Free Work First: How to Say No to Spec Work Without Losing the Client

"Just do a quick sample and we will see." "One free trial piece, then we can talk about a contract." Every freelancer hears it, and every freelancer knows what it usually means: the sample becomes the deliverable, the contract never arrives, and the follow-up goes unanswered. Spec work is not a foot in the door. It is unpaid work with no agreed price, no deadline, and no obligation on the other side. Saying no to it is not losing a client. It is the filter that separates real clients from people shopping for free labour, and there are ways to say it that keep the conversation alive.

1. Name what they are actually asking for

The first move is to translate the request out loud, politely. "So you would like a finished piece before we agree on price or scope, to judge whether to hire me." Said plainly, most clients hear how one-sided that sounds, and many back off on their own. This is not a confrontation. It is clarity, and clarity is doing the client a favour too: a client who genuinely wants to test fit will accept a paid alternative once they see the shape of the ask. A client who only wanted free work will push back, and that pushback is the most useful information in the conversation, because it tells you the project was never going to pay.

2. Offer a paid test instead of a free one

Never answer a free request with a flat no and nothing else. Offer the smallest paid version of the work: a paid discovery call, a paid audit, a single paid piece at your normal rate with a clear deliverable. Frame it as risk reduction for both sides: "A paid trial piece means you own the work outright and I can give it full attention, and if we continue, it counts toward the project." Real clients say yes to this, because a small paid test is cheaper than a bad hire. Free-work shoppers say no, because the test was never about fit. Either way, you have your answer in one message, without an argument about principle.

3. Show past work instead of making new work

The legitimate reason a client asks for a sample is that they have not seen proof you can do the job. Answer that reason directly, without creating anything new. Send two or three past pieces closest to their brief, with a sentence each about the result: what the client needed, what you delivered, what happened next. If your past work is under NDA, describe the brief and the outcome without naming the client or showing the piece. A portfolio answers the fit question faster than a sample, and it costs you nothing. If the client still insists on something custom after seeing relevant past work, the request was never about proof.

4. Put the boundary in writing before the project starts

The freelancers who get trapped by spec work are usually trapped by vagueness, not by one request. Fix it at onboarding: your proposal states that all work is paid, that revisions happen within a paid engagement, and that no deliverable is produced before a deposit or signed agreement. When the next "quick sample" request arrives, you are not inventing a policy in the moment. You are pointing at one that already exists, which is much easier to hold under pressure. Boundaries stated before money is discussed feel professional. Boundaries invented after a client is already excited feel personal, and personal boundaries get negotiated away.

5. Know when the conversation is over

Some prospects will keep pushing after the paid alternative, the portfolio, and the written policy. At that point, stop selling. A short, warm close protects your reputation and your time: thank them, restate that you would love to work together on a paid basis, and leave the door open. Do not chase them, do not discount the sample to win them, and do not warn other freelancers about them. The client who needed free work was never your client, and the hours you save are hours you can spend on a client who pays. Saying no to one free project is not losing income. It is refusing to fund someone else's business with your unpaid time.

The pattern underneath

Spec work survives because it is framed as an opportunity, and opportunities feel rude to refuse. Reframe it as what it is: unpaid work with no agreed price and no obligation. Name the ask, offer a paid test, show past work instead of making new work, put the boundary in writing before the next project, and close warmly when the prospect keeps pushing. The freelancer who says no to free work does not lose clients. They lose the prospects who were never going to pay, and they keep the calendar clear for the ones who will.
