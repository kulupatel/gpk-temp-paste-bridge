The Client Who Says "I Thought That Was Included": How to Answer a Scope Dispute That Is Really a Payment Stall

There is a sentence that arrives exactly when the invoice does, and it sounds reasonable enough that many freelancers apologize before they have finished reading it: "I thought that was included." The client is not refusing to pay, not exactly. They are redrawing the agreement after the work is done, and the new drawing has your fee covering more than the old one did. A revision round becomes "included." A second format becomes "included." A feature you quoted separately becomes "included." And the invoice, which was correct when you sent it, suddenly looks like an overcharge to the only person whose opinion matters for getting paid.

The reason this sentence works so well as a stall is that it is almost impossible to argue against in the form it arrives in. It is phrased as a memory, not a demand. The client is not saying "I refuse to pay for this." They are saying "my understanding was different," and disagreeing with someone's understanding feels rude, confrontational, and bad for the relationship. So the freelancer does what feels polite: they absorb the extra work, they reduce the invoice, or they agree to "discuss it later," which means they agree to work for free and hope the client remembers the favour. The client, meanwhile, has learned something valuable: that the scope of the agreement is negotiable after the work is finished, and that the freelancer will renegotiate it downward rather than hold the line.

The way to answer "I thought that was included" is not to argue about what anyone thought. It is to answer with the document. A scope dispute is a claim about a written agreement, and written agreements settle it in minutes, while memories never settle anything. The freelancer who can point to the quote, the proposal, or the email where the scope was set does not need to win an argument. They need to show the client the page, offer the two honest options, and let the client choose. The freelancer who cannot point to anything has no answer at all, which is why this article ends with prevention, and why the prevention section is the most important one in it.

1. "Included" is a claim about a document, not a feeling

The first step is to change the question. When a client says "I thought that was included," the question they are asking, whether they know it or not, is "what did we agree?" That question has an answer in the paperwork, and the paperwork is the only place it can be answered fairly. So the first reply is not a defence of the invoice and not an apology. It is a short, warm message that moves the conversation from memory to the document: "Happy to check that. Let me look at the quote I sent on the 4th and confirm what was in the scope, and I will come back to you today."

That message does three things. First, it takes the heat out of the exchange, because you are agreeing to check rather than refusing outright. Second, it puts a specific document on the table, which is the document that actually decides the question, and it tells the client you are going to consult it rather than argue from memory. Third, it buys you time to read the document properly before you answer, which matters more than it sounds like it does, because the answer you give next is the one that sets the terms for everything after it.

Then read the document. Really read it, including the parts you wrote quickly and the parts you assumed were obvious. There are only three possible findings, and each one calls for a different reply. The item was explicitly included, and the client is right. The item was explicitly excluded, or quoted separately, and the client is wrong. Or the document says nothing about the item at all, and the scope was genuinely ambiguous, which means both sides have a reasonable reading and the answer is a negotiation, not a correction.

The honest freelancer checks which of the three it is before replying, and tells the client the truth even when the truth is inconvenient. If the item was included and you charged for it anyway, say so plainly, correct the invoice, and apologize for the mistake. That costs you the amount of one item, and it buys you something worth far more: a client who now believes you when you say the scope is the scope, because they have seen you correct yourself when the document was against you. If the item was excluded or quoted separately, say so plainly, and quote the exact words. If the document is silent, say so plainly too, and propose a split that reflects the shared ambiguity rather than pretending the document says something it does not.

The one reply that never works is the vague one: "I am pretty sure it was not included," or "I think we discussed that separately," or "my understanding was that this was extra." Vague answers invite vague counter-answers, and a dispute that starts vague stays vague until someone gives up, which is usually the freelancer. Specificity is the whole answer. Quote the line, name the date, and let the document do the disagreeing.

2. Answer with the quote, not with an argument

When the document is on your side, the reply should be short, warm, and built around the exact words. Something like this: "I checked the quote from the 4th. The scope lists the homepage design and three revision rounds, and the extra revision round and the mobile layout are listed under additional work at 150 each. The invoice matches the quote, so the 300 for those two items stands. Happy to walk through the quote line by line if that helps."

Notice what that message does not do. It does not accuse the client of misremembering. It does not explain why the extra work cost money, or how long it took, or how hard it was. It does not apologize for the invoice. It states what the agreement said, shows that the invoice matches it, and offers to walk through it together. The tone is helpful, not defensive, because the freelancer has nothing to defend: the agreement is doing the work, and the freelancer is simply reading it aloud.

There is a temptation, when the client pushes back, to add justifications: the hours, the difficulty, the fact that the client approved the extra work in a message on the 12th. Resist it. Justifications invite the client to argue about the justifications, and the argument drifts from the agreement to the effort, which is a debate the freelancer cannot win, because effort is subjective and the client can always say the work looked easy. The agreement is objective. Stay with it.

If the client approved the extra work in writing, that approval is part of the document, and it belongs in the reply too: "You confirmed the extra revision round in your message on the 12th, and I went ahead on that basis." That sentence is not an accusation. It is a fact, and it reminds the client that the extra work was not something you decided to do on your own. It was something they asked for, and you did, and the invoice is the price of what they asked for.

When the client accepts the answer, and most clients do once they see the quote, close the matter quickly and graciously. Confirm the corrected understanding, restate the amount and the due date, and move on. Do not lecture the client about reading quotes carefully, and do not extract an apology. The goal is a paid invoice and a working relationship, and both are easier to keep when the correction feels like a clarification rather than a victory.

3. The three kinds of "included," and how to tell them apart

Not every "I thought that was included" is the same claim, and the response that works depends on which kind it is. The first kind is a genuine misunderstanding. The client read the quote, formed a reasonable impression that the item was covered, and was surprised by the invoice. This happens most often when the scope was described in broad language, like "website design" or "full branding package," which can honestly be read two ways. A genuine misunderstanding is usually easy to spot: the client asks the question once, listens to the answer, and either accepts it or proposes a fair compromise. The answer here is patience and a clear reading of the document, and if the document is genuinely ambiguous, a fair split is often the right outcome, because the ambiguity was partly your drafting.

The second kind is vague scope, and it is the most common. The scope was never written down in enough detail to decide the question, so the client is not misreading the document, they are filling in a blank, and their blank says "included" while yours says "extra." This is not a dispute about honesty. It is a dispute about a gap, and the gap belongs to whoever wrote the scope, which is usually the freelancer. When the gap is yours, the fair answer is to own it, settle this invoice in a way that shares the cost, and fix the scope document before the next project, so the same gap never opens again. A freelancer who charges the full amount for an item the scope never mentioned is technically within their rights and practically making an enemy, and the enemy will cost more than the item did.

The third kind is a deliberate stall, and it is the one this article is really about. The client knows the item was extra, or could find out in thirty seconds by reading the quote, and is using "I thought that was included" as a way to delay payment, reduce the invoice, or test whether the freelancer will fold. The signs are consistent: the claim arrives with the invoice rather than with the work, it covers several items at once rather than one, it changes shape when you answer it, and it is followed by a new reason when the first one fails. The client who says "I thought that was included" on Monday, "I never approved that" on Wednesday, and "the budget has changed" on Friday is not confused about the scope. They are working through a list of reasons not to pay, and the scope claim was simply the first one on the list.

A deliberate stall needs a different response from a genuine misunderstanding, because patience rewards it. The answer is the document, quoted exactly, plus a firm date, plus a consequence that follows naturally if the date passes: "The quote lists these two items as additional work at 150 each, and you confirmed the extra round on the 12th. The invoice is 2,000, due on the 20th. If the scope question is the only thing holding payment, let me know today and we can close it. If the payment has not arrived by the 20th, I will pause the next phase until the balance is settled." That message is firm without being angry, it gives the client a clear way to close the matter, and it names the consequence in advance, which is what makes it work. A client with a genuine misunderstanding will accept the clarification and pay. A client running a stall will move on to the next reason, and each new reason tells you more about the pattern, which is information you need for the next decision.

4. The message that settles it

When the document is clear and the client is still pushing, the most useful message is one that offers two honest options and asks the client to choose. The options are: the item is extra, and the invoice stands; or the item is removed, and the invoice is reduced by exactly the cost of the item, with the work undone or not delivered. There is no third option in which the item stays and the price disappears, and saying so plainly is what ends the negotiation.

A message that does this well sounds like this: "I understand the confusion, and I have checked the quote. The extra revision round and the mobile layout were quoted as additional work at 150 each, which is why they are on the invoice. There are two ways we can handle it. I can keep them in, and the invoice stays at 2,000, due on the 20th. Or I can take them out, and the invoice drops to 1,700, and the mobile layout stays as it was before the extra work. Either is fine by me, just let me know which you prefer by tomorrow so I can update the invoice."

That message works because it makes the choice concrete. The client is no longer arguing about a word. They are choosing between two real outcomes, and both of them are fair, and one of them costs them the work they want. Most clients, faced with that choice, choose to keep the work and pay the invoice, because the work is worth more to them than the argument is. The clients who choose to remove the work have also told you something useful: they did not value the extra work at its price, and you now know not to do unpriced extra work for them again.

There is a version of this message for the case where the document is silent and the scope was genuinely ambiguous. Then the honest answer is to share the cost: "The quote does not mention the extra round either way, so I understand the confusion. I will split the difference on this one: I will reduce the invoice by 150, half the cost of the two items, and we will put the scope in writing before the next phase so this does not come up again." That is a concession, and it should be named as one, and it should come with the condition that the scope gets written down next time. A concession without a condition is a precedent. A concession with a condition is a one-time fix that makes the next project cleaner.

5. Prevention: write the scope before the work starts

Every "I thought that was included" dispute is cheaper to prevent than to settle, and prevention is a single habit: write the scope down before the work starts, in plain words, with the inclusions and the exclusions both named. The scope does not need to be a legal document. It needs to be an email or a one-page quote that says what is included, what is not included, what costs extra and how much, and how many revision rounds are covered. The most useful line in any scope document is the exclusions line, because exclusions are where these disputes live. "Includes: homepage design, three revision rounds, delivery in two formats. Does not include: additional revision rounds, extra formats, copywriting, photography." That paragraph takes two minutes to write and settles every "included" dispute before it starts, because the client has read it and agreed to it before the work began.

The second prevention habit is the change order, and it is the habit that saves the most money. Whenever the client asks for something outside the scope, answer in writing before you do it: "Happy to add that. That is outside the scope we agreed, so it will be 150, and I will add it to the invoice. Shall I go ahead?" That message takes thirty seconds, and it does two things. It gives the client a price before the work is done, when the price is easy to accept, and it creates a written record that the extra work was requested and priced, which is the document you will quote if the client later says they thought it was included. The freelancer who sends that message never has to argue about extra work, because the extra work was agreed in writing before it existed.

The third prevention habit is the simplest: when the client asks for something small and you decide to do it for free, say so in writing. "I will include this one at no charge this time." That sentence costs nothing and prevents the most expensive misunderstanding in freelancing, which is the client who believes a free favour was part of the package, and expects it free on every project after. A favour that is named as a favour stays a favour. A favour that is silent becomes an expectation, and expectations are what the next "I thought that was included" is made of.

The pattern underneath

"I thought that was included" is a claim about a document, and the answer is always the document. When the document is on your side, quote it exactly, offer the two honest options, and let the client choose. When the document is silent, own the gap, share the cost once, and fix the scope before the next project. When the claim keeps changing shape, stop answering the claim and start answering the pattern: a firm date, a named consequence, and terms that put the scope in writing before the work starts. The freelancer who writes the scope down before the work begins almost never has to argue about it after the work is done, and the freelancer who answers extra work with a price, in writing, before doing it, never has to argue about that either. The document is the answer, and the habit of writing it down is the prevention.
