Chasing Invoices Abroad? What UK, EU, and US Clients Actually Respond To

The same overdue invoice, sent to three clients, needs three different emails. A freelancer I know learned this the expensive way: she sent her usual blunt payment reminder to a London client and got a frosty reply that set the relationship back months. The invoice was valid, the tone was wrong. Chasing late payment across borders is not just a wording problem. It is a culture problem.

And behind the culture sits something more concrete: the law. In the UK and the EU, statute law gives freelancers real, automatic leverage over late payers, so the chase email can stay calm and let the legislation do the talking. In the US, there is no federal late-payment statute for private business deals, so your contract is the leverage and the email has to carry more weight. Once you understand which side of that divide your client sits on, the right tone becomes obvious.

US clients: be short, direct, and put the penalty up front

American business culture rewards directness. A US client reading a long, apologetic chase email will often skim past the ask entirely. What works: a short email where the amount, the due date, and the consequence sit in the first three lines.

There is a legal reason the US email has to work harder. Unlike the UK and EU, the United States has no federal statute giving freelancers an automatic right to late-payment interest on private business invoices. The federal Prompt Payment Act only covers federal government contracts. For everyone else, whatever is written in the contract is the whole game, which is exactly why US clients are used to contracts having teeth and why stating the penalty plainly is what makes them believe you will enforce it.

There are some state-level exceptions worth knowing. New York's Freelance Isn't Free Act requires written contracts for freelance work over a threshold amount and payment within 30 days of completing the work, with double damages possible for nonpayment. California's Freelance Worker Protection Act creates similar protections. If your client is in one of those states, that is useful background, but the contract still does the heavy lifting.

Example opener that works on US clients:

Subject: Overdue: invoice #1042, $1,800, due March 14

Hi [Name], invoice #1042 for $1,800 was due March 14 and is still unpaid. Please confirm a payment date today. A 1.5% monthly late fee applies from here, per the terms on the invoice.

Notice what is missing: no apology, no throat-clearing, no "hope you are well." In the US, that reads as professional, not rude. Reference the contract, name the fee, and ask for one concrete thing: a payment date today. Follow-ups stay short. The second email can be a single paragraph restating the balance and the running fee total. With US clients, length signals weakness and precision signals seriousness.

One more US-specific habit: invoice terms like Net 30 are the default expectation, and many larger companies run Net 45 or Net 60 vendor cycles as standard practice. If you quote Net 30 on your invoice and the client pays on Net 60, that is a contract problem you can fix on the next project by writing shorter terms in, not a chase problem you can argue about now. Negotiate the term before the work, enforce the term after it.

UK clients: climb the polite-to-formal ladder

British business culture runs on understatement, which means a blunt American-style email lands as aggression. UK clients respond better to a ladder: each email slightly more formal than the last. Start warm and give them a way to save face.

The good news is that UK law is the strongest friend a freelancer can have. The Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 gives any business, including sole traders and freelancers, an automatic right to charge statutory interest on overdue commercial invoices: the Bank of England base rate plus 8%, calculated as simple interest from the day the payment became late. On top of that, you can claim fixed compensation per overdue invoice: £40 for debts under £1,000, £70 for debts between £1,000 and £10,000, and £100 for debts over £10,000, plus reasonable recovery costs on top. If no payment date was agreed, the default is 30 days after you invoiced or finished the work, whichever is later. You do not need to have mentioned any of this in your contract for it to apply.

That is why the ladder works so well with UK clients. They know this legislation exists, and each step up the ladder signals that you know it too.

Email one can be genuinely friendly:

Hi [Name], I hope all is well. I wanted to flag that invoice #1042 appears to still be outstanding on your side. Could you let me know when I might expect it?

Email two, a week later, drops the warmth and adds formality:

I am writing to follow up on invoice #1042, now 14 days overdue. Please arrange payment by [date].

Email three, the formal stage, states your position in plain terms:

Invoice #1042 is now 28 days overdue. Under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts legislation, I am entitled to claim statutory interest and compensation on overdue invoices. Please confirm payment by [date] so we can settle this without further steps.

Keep it informational, never threatening. That sentence, calmly stated, does more work than any exclamation mark. In practice, many UK freelancers report that the first email mentioning the legislation is the one that gets paid. This is tone guidance, not legal advice; the specifics of your situation are yours to check.

One practical tip: quote the running total in the formal email. A line like "Statutory interest of £23.40 plus £70 compensation now applies, on top of the £1,800 invoice" turns an abstract right into a number on the page, and numbers get forwarded to finance departments.

EU clients: win on the invoice, not in the chase

Chasing payment inside the EU varies a lot by country, and that is exactly the point: the chase email matters less than the invoice itself. In many EU markets, business norms around payment terms are set at the contracting stage, and clients expect everything spelled out up front. If your invoice already states the payment term, the due date, and the late fee in plain language, the chase email can be short in any country.

EU law gives you a solid floor to stand on. The EU Late Payment Directive (2011/7/EU), which every member state has implemented in its own national law, sets a default payment term of 30 days when nothing else was agreed. Contract terms can stretch to 60 days, but anything beyond that has to be expressly agreed and must not be grossly unfair to the creditor. Interest on late payment is automatic from the day after the deadline: the European Central Bank's reference rate plus 8%, and the creditor is entitled to a fixed €40 per overdue invoice as compensation for recovery costs, without even sending a reminder. Reasonable extra recovery costs can be claimed on top.

The country-level texture matters for tone, though. German and Dutch clients tend to treat agreed payment dates as hard commitments, so a factual reminder referencing the agreed term usually resolves things quickly. In Southern European markets, longer payment cycles are more common in practice, and the chase email works better as a steady, professional rhythm than as a single stern shot. None of this changes the legal floor, but it changes the email.

The short version of the EU playbook:

Hi [Name], a reminder that invoice #1042 (€2,100) was due March 14. Per the agreed terms, a late fee now applies. Could you confirm a payment date?

The lesson from EU freelancers who rarely chase at all: the email is just the reminder. The agreement did the real work months earlier. In the EU more than anywhere, prevention beats recovery, because the directive rewards the creditor who put everything in writing from the start.

The one rule that works everywhere

Put payment terms in writing before the project starts, not inside the chase email. Every country-specific tactic above gets easier when the invoice already carries the term, the due date, and the fee. Clients do not argue with terms they agreed to. They argue with surprises.

Before your next project with an international client, run through this checklist:

  1. Written agreement before work starts, naming the payment term and due date.
  2. Late fee or interest clause on every invoice, stated plainly.
  3. Invoice sent to the right person, with the due date printed on it, not buried in your email body.
  4. Your first chase goes out the day after the due date, in the tone that fits the client's country.
  5. Every chase points back to the agreed term, never to your frustration.

If you take one thing from this article: your chase emails should only ever point back to something the client already signed. The email is the pointer. The paperwork is the leverage.
