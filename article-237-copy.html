The Client Who Sends New Bank Details by Email: How to Spot a Payment-Details Scam

The email arrives in the middle of an ordinary week. It looks like it comes from your client, or from their finance team, and it says the company has changed banks. Here are the new account details. Please use them for the next invoice, or for the payment that is already on its way. The tone is normal. The signature is right. The logo is there. And if you do what it asks, your money goes to a stranger, and nobody involved will be able to get it back.

This is the most dangerous message a freelancer can receive, because it does not look like a scam. It looks like admin. A late payment costs you time. A payment sent to the wrong account costs you the whole amount, and the bank will usually tell you that a transfer you authorised yourself is your own loss. The defence is not suspicion of every client. It is one rule, applied every time, without exception: payment details are never changed on the strength of an email alone.

1. Why this scam works on careful people

The scam is called business email compromise, and it works because it exploits the exact habits that make a freelancer easy to work with. You reply quickly. You trust your client. You do not want to be the person who questions a routine finance update and delays a payment by a week. The attacker knows this, and the message is written to reward all of it: short, polite, plausible, and slightly urgent.

The attacker usually gets in through one of three doors. The client's email account is compromised, so the message comes from a real address. A lookalike domain is registered, one letter different, so the message looks right at a glance. Or the attacker has been watching the thread, waiting for the moment an invoice is due, and then sends the change exactly when a new payment is expected, so the timing itself feels like confirmation.

None of this requires the attacker to know anything special about you. They need to know that a payment is coming, and that you will act on an email. That is the whole attack, and it succeeds against careful people precisely because the message asks for something small: update the details, nothing else changes. The size of the ask is the disguise.

So the first thing to understand is that falling for this is not carelessness. It is the normal result of treating an email as proof, and the fix is not to become suspicious of your clients. The fix is to move the proof somewhere an email cannot reach.

2. The rule: no change of payment details without a second channel

The rule is simple, and it has to be absolute, because a rule with exceptions is a rule the attacker can use. Payment details change only after you have confirmed the change through a second, independent channel, and the confirmation has to come from a person you already know, at a number or address you already have.

That means you do not reply to the email asking if it is real, because a compromised inbox will answer yes. You do not click a link in the email, or call a number printed in the email, because the attacker wrote those too. You call the client on the number you already have saved, or you message them on a channel you have used before, and you ask one question: did you change your bank details. If the answer is yes, you ask them to confirm the new details in that same conversation, and you keep a record of it.

The same rule applies in reverse, and this is the part freelancers forget. If a client tells you their payment bounced, or that your invoice has the wrong details, do not change your own payment details on the strength of their email either. Your bank details are yours to state, in the contract and on the invoice, and they change only when you say so, through a channel the client can verify.

Put the rule in writing before you need it. One line in your terms is enough. "Payment details change only by written agreement confirmed on a phone call." That line does two things. It gives you a reason to make the call without feeling awkward, and it tells a genuine client exactly what to expect, so a real change of bank is a five minute conversation instead of a crisis.

3. The five checks that catch the fake

A second-channel confirmation is the only check that settles the question, but there are five quick checks that will catch most fakes before you even pick up the phone, and they are worth doing on every payment-details message you receive.

First, read the sender address slowly, character by character, not the display name. A display name can say anything. The address is the truth, and lookalike domains hide in one letter: a zero for an O, an extra letter, a different ending. If the address is not the exact address you have been using, stop there.

Second, check the thread. A genuine change of bank details usually comes as a reply in an existing thread, or from a person you have dealt with before. A message that starts a new thread, or comes from a name you have never seen in finance, is a warning. It is not proof of fraud on its own, but it is a reason to verify before acting.

Third, look for urgency. A real finance change is not urgent. The client is not in a hurry for you to update your records, because the old details still work until they tell you otherwise. A message that says the old account closes tomorrow, or that a payment will fail unless you act today, is manufacturing the haste that makes people skip verification.

Fourth, check the details themselves. A genuine new account will usually come with a full set of details: account name, number, sort code or routing number, bank name, and often a reference. A fake is often thin on detail, or the account name does not match the company name exactly. A mismatch between the company you work for and the name on the account is a hard stop, not a detail to overlook.

Fifth, check the timing. A payment-details change that arrives in the same week an invoice is due, or just after you sent a chase, is the attacker's favourite window, because you are expecting money and you are primed to act. A change that arrives when nothing is due is much more likely to be real. Timing is not proof, but it is a reason to be careful rather than quick.

If any one of these checks fails, make the call. If all five pass, make the call anyway. The rule is absolute because the cost of one verification call is five minutes, and the cost of one missed fraud is the whole payment.

4. What to do if you already sent money to the wrong account

If you have already paid money, or a client has already paid you, using details from a fraudulent email, act fast, and act in this order. Speed matters more than blame, and the first hour matters most.

First, call your bank immediately and ask for a recall of the transfer. A recall is not a refund, and it does not always work, but it is the only tool that exists, and it works best when the money has not yet been moved on. Give the bank the exact details: the amount, the date, the destination account, and the fact that the payment was induced by a fraudulent email. Get a reference number for the recall, and write down the name of the person you spoke to.

Second, tell the client, on the phone, not by email. The client needs to know that their email may be compromised, or that someone is impersonating them, and that any further payment instructions should be treated as suspect until the matter is resolved. The client also needs to know that the payment did not reach you, so the invoice is still open, and the two of you can agree together on how to re-issue it safely.

Third, report it. Your bank will have a fraud team. Your local police or cybercrime reporting service will take a report, and that report matters, because it creates a record that the payment was fraud, not a mistake, and that record is what you will need for any insurance claim or any later dispute. Keep copies of the fraudulent email, including the full headers if you can get them, because the headers are the evidence of where the message really came from.

Fourth, do not send the payment again to the new details, and do not accept a second set of new details by email. The attacker who succeeded once will try again, often with a message that apologises for the confusion and offers corrected details. Every further instruction goes through the phone call, and every payment waits until the verification is done in person or on a call you initiated.

And be honest with yourself about the loss. A transfer you authorised yourself is very hard to recover in full, and the bank may tell you so. That is painful, and it is also the reason the rule in section 2 has to be absolute. The cost of verification is five minutes. The cost of skipping it is the payment, and sometimes the client as well.

5. Prevention: make your payment details boring and hard to change

The lasting fix is to make your payment details the most boring part of the whole relationship, so boring that any change stands out immediately. That starts with the contract and the invoice, and it continues with the way you talk about money.

Put your payment details in the contract, and on every invoice, in exactly the same form, every time. The same account name, the same number, the same bank, the same reference format. When the details never change, a message saying they have changed is automatically suspicious, and you have a document to compare it against. If your details ever do change, tell the client yourself, on a call, before you send the new invoice, and put the new details in the contract as an amendment, not in an email.

Add one line to your terms, and say it out loud at kickoff. "My bank details are the ones on the invoice. If you ever get a message saying they have changed, call me before you act on it." That line costs nothing, and it protects both sides, because a client who has heard it will call you instead of paying a stranger, and a client who calls you is a client you can warn.

Keep a single record of where each payment went, and check it against the invoice the day the money arrives. The check takes thirty seconds: amount, date, account, reference. A payment that arrives from an unexpected account, or with a reference that does not match, is a question to ask, not a detail to ignore. And a payment that never arrives, after a details-change email, is the exact moment to make the call in section 2, before you send anything else.

The same discipline protects you from the other direction too. Never send a client new payment details by email alone. If your bank changes, or you open a new account, tell the client on a call, send the new invoice with the new details, and confirm on the call that they received it. Your clients are as vulnerable to this scam as you are, and the freelancer who protects their own details is also protecting their clients.

The pattern underneath

A payment-details change that arrives by email is a claim, not a fact, and claims about money are verified on a second channel or not at all. Read the sender address slowly, check the thread, refuse the urgency, check the details, and make the call on a number you already have. If money has already gone to the wrong account, recall it fast, tell the client by phone, and report it. And make your own payment details boring and fixed, so that any change is immediately visible, and any message claiming a change is immediately a phone call. The five minutes a verification call costs is the cheapest insurance in freelancing, because the payment it protects is the whole job.
