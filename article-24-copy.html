Your Late Fee Policy Is Legal in These Countries: What You Can Actually Charge

Most freelancers leave late fees off their invoices for a simple reason: they are not sure the fee is even legal. So they absorb the cost of late payment and hope the next client is better.

Here is the surprise: in a lot of the world, the law is already on your side. In some countries it hands you a late fee even when your contract forgot to mention one. The catch is that the rules differ by country, and this is general information, not legal advice. When real money is at stake, a local lawyer is cheaper than a wrong assumption.

United Kingdom: the law does the work for you

The UK has some of the strongest late-payment protections in the world. Under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998, when a business client pays late you can charge:

  • Statutory interest at 8 percent over the Bank of England reference rate, running daily from the day after the agreed payment date.
  • Fixed compensation per overdue invoice: £40 for debts under £1,000, £70 for £1,000 to £10,000, £100 above £10,000.
  • Reasonable recovery costs on top.

This applies automatically to business-to-business transactions unless your contract sets a different remedy that is "substantial" (in practice, a contract rate is fine if it is not a token gesture). You can also write your own late fee into the contract instead. Either way, "I did not know I could charge" is not your situation in the UK.

European Union: similar rights, one directive

The EU Late Payment Directive (2011/7/EU) gives creditors broadly similar tools: interest of at least 8 percentage points above the European Central Bank reference rate, a fixed €40 compensation per overdue invoice, and reasonable recovery costs. The default payment period between businesses is 30 days, extendable to 60 by agreement unless that is grossly unfair to the creditor.

Each member state implemented the directive with its own details, so check your country's version. But the principle holds across the EU: the law assumes late payment costs you money, and it lets you charge for it.

United States: it is state by state

There is no single federal late-payment rule for private commercial transactions in the US. What controls is your contract. If your contract says 1.5 percent per month on overdue balances, that is what you can charge. If the contract is silent, state law supplies a default prejudgment interest rate, and those vary widely, commonly somewhere between 5 and 10 percent, with different rules on whether they compound.

Two warnings for the US: some states cap late fees, and a fee that is wildly disproportionate can be attacked as an unenforceable penalty. The fix is the same as everywhere else. Put a reasonable rate in the contract before the work starts.

Canada and Australia: the same pattern

Canada's Interest Act means interest must be expressed as an annual rate to be enforceable, and provinces add their own wrinkles, so the rate on your invoice needs to be stated properly. Australia has no single national late-payment statute for private contracts, but late fees are standard practice and the unfair contract terms regime protects small businesses from one-sided clauses. In both countries, as in the US, the contract you signed is what matters most.

What to actually write on the invoice

Knowing the law is step one. Using it is step two, and that is where most freelancers still fail. A late fee policy clients respect follows four rules:

  1. State it before the work. The fee belongs in the contract or proposal, not as a surprise on the third reminder.
  2. Restate it on the invoice. One line: "Payment due within 14 days. Overdue balances accrue 1.5 percent per month." Calm, printed, boring.
  3. Enforce it exactly once, visibly. The first time a fee appears on a statement, every future client who sees your terms takes them seriously.
  4. Never threaten a fee you will not collect. An empty threat teaches clients your terms are decoration.

The late fee is not about the money, though the money is nice. It is about making the cost of paying late visible at the moment the client decides what to pay first. Your invoice competes with every other bill they owe. Make it the expensive one to ignore.
