The Client Who Asks You to Absorb the Payment Processing Fee: Who Pays the Fee, and How to Say No

You invoice $2,000. The client pays by card, and $58 disappears before the money reaches you. Or the client pays through PayPal, and the fee comes out of your side. Then the message arrives: "I paid the full invoice, so the fee is your problem." It sounds like a small thing. It sounds like the cost of doing business. But add that fee up across a year of invoices and it is not small at all. On $60,000 of annual billing, a 3 percent fee is $1,800, and $1,800 is a month of rent, a new laptop, or a holiday you did not take because a client's payment method ate it.

The question is not whether a fee exists. Every payment method has one, and someone always pays it. The question is who agreed to pay it, and the answer should have been settled before the invoice went out. If it was not settled, this article settles it for the next invoice, and tells you how to answer the client who wants you to absorb the fee on this one.

1. The fee belongs to the payment method, not to your price

A processing fee is the cost of the way the client chose to pay. A card payment costs more to process than a bank transfer. A PayPal payment costs more than a direct deposit. When the client chooses the expensive method, the cost of that choice is theirs, in the same way that the cost of express shipping belongs to the person who asks for express shipping. Your price was for the work. It was not for the client's payment preferences.

That distinction matters, because clients often frame the fee as if it were a discount you should offer. "I paid $2,000, so you should be happy with $1,942." But you did not quote $1,942. You quoted $2,000, and the client agreed to $2,000, and the $58 went to a payment processor, not to you. The client paid the full amount and you received less than the full amount, and the difference is not a discount. It is a cost the client created by choosing how to pay, and a cost they should carry.

Say that plainly when it comes up. "The invoice was $2,000, and the card fee of $58 is charged by the payment processor on the method you chose. I need the full invoice amount to reach me, so either the fee is added to the payment or we use a bank transfer, which carries no fee." That sentence is not an argument. It is a statement of how the numbers work, and most clients who hear it stated that clearly will simply switch methods or add the fee, because the alternative is admitting they expect you to pay for their convenience.

2. Build the fee into your price before the invoice, not after it

The cleanest answer to the fee question is to make it disappear from the conversation entirely, by pricing your work so the fee is already covered. If you know a typical client pays by card at around 3 percent, then a $2,000 job is really a $2,060 job when the client pays by card, and you should quote accordingly, or quote $2,000 with bank transfer as the standard method and a card option that carries the fee.

What you should never do is add a surprise surcharge to an invoice after the price was agreed. A client who agreed $2,000 and then sees $2,060 on the invoice will feel cheated, even if the extra $60 is a genuine pass-through, because the number they agreed to has changed. The fee has to be visible before the agreement, not after it. That means one of three things, stated in your quote or your terms: bank transfer is the standard method and carries no fee; card and PayPal are available and the processing fee is added at cost; or your quoted price already includes card processing and no fee is ever mentioned.

The third option is the simplest for clients who always pay by card. Quote the price, take the fee out of your side, and never mention it, because the fee is already in the number they agreed to. The first two options are better when clients pay by a mix of methods, because they keep the bank-transfer clients from subsidising the card clients. Either way, the rule is the same: the fee is settled in the quote, not fought over after the payment lands.

3. Offer a fee-free way to pay instead of arguing about the fee

When a client pushes back on a fee, the fastest way to end the argument is to give them a way to pay that has no fee at all. A bank transfer, a direct deposit, or a payment through a platform that does not charge the receiver all solve the problem without anyone absorbing anything. The client keeps their full $2,000, you receive the full $2,000, and the only person who loses is the payment processor, who was never part of the deal.

So your reply to a fee objection should always include the alternative. "I understand you would rather not pay the card fee. A bank transfer to the account on the invoice carries no fee at all, and it usually clears in one working day. If you would like to use that instead, the invoice stays at $2,000." That reply does two things. It shows the client you are not trying to charge them extra for nothing, and it puts the choice back in their hands: pay the fee for the convenience of the card, or use the free method. Most clients will pick the free method, and the ones who still choose the card have chosen to pay the fee themselves, which ends the argument.

Keep the fee-free method easy. Put your bank details on every invoice, in the same place every time, so the client never has to ask for them. If your bank details change, say so in writing before the next invoice goes out, and confirm the client has the new details. A client who cannot find your bank details will default to the card, and then you are back to arguing about a fee that a visible bank account would have prevented.

4. Never absorb a fee retroactively

The one thing you should never do is agree to absorb a fee after the payment has already been made. A client who paid $2,000 by card, saw you receive $1,942, and then asks you to treat the invoice as paid in full is asking you to accept $58 less than the agreed price, and to do it after the work is done, when you have no leverage left. If you agree once, you have set the price for every future invoice, because the client will pay the same way next time and expect the same treatment.

The answer is to hold the invoice open for the shortfall, politely and in writing. "Thanks for the payment. The card processor took $58, so $58 of the invoice is still outstanding. You can cover it with a bank transfer, or we can add it to the next invoice." That is not a confrontation. It is a statement of fact, and it keeps the shortfall visible instead of letting it disappear into a rounding error you silently absorb. A shortfall that is written down gets paid. A shortfall that is never mentioned gets repeated.

If the client refuses and insists the invoice is paid in full, do not argue about $58 in a way that costs you the relationship, but do not write it off silently either. Note it in writing, adjust your terms for the next project so the fee is covered, and make the next quote reflect the real cost of that client's payment method. The lesson is not that you lost $58. The lesson is that your terms did not cover the fee, and the next quote must.

5. Put the fee rule in your terms before the next invoice

None of this works as a one-off argument. It works as a line in your terms that you can point to, so the fee is never a negotiation again. The line is simple: "Payment by bank transfer carries no fee. Payment by card or PayPal carries the processing fee at cost, added to the invoice. The invoice is paid in full when the full invoiced amount has been received." Three sentences, and the entire argument disappears, because the client agreed to the terms before the work started.

Add that line to your quote template, your contract, and the footer of every invoice, so it is visible at every stage. When a new client asks how to pay, send them the bank details and the fee rule together, in the same message, so the choice is made before the first invoice exists. And when an existing client changes payment methods, say so in writing: "Noted, you will pay by card going forward. The card fee will be added at cost, as per the terms." A fee rule that lives in your terms is a policy. A fee rule that lives only in your head is an argument waiting to happen.

The pattern underneath

A processing fee is the cost of the way the client chose to pay, not a discount on your price. Build it into your quote or state it in your terms before the work starts, offer a fee-free way to pay instead of arguing, never absorb a fee after the payment has landed, and put the rule in writing so it is a policy rather than a negotiation. The client can choose the method. You should never be the one who pays for their choice.
