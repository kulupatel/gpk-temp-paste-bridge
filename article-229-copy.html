The Client Who Pays the Deposit and Then Goes Quiet Before the Work Starts: How to Hold a Deposit Without Losing the Client

The deposit lands, the client is enthusiastic, and then nothing. No kickoff call. No brief. No reply to your scheduling email. A week passes, then two. You have money in the bank and a project that has not started, and the longer the silence lasts, the stranger the position feels. You cannot do the work, because there is no brief. You cannot chase too hard, because the client has already paid you. And you cannot quite bring yourself to spend the deposit, because some part of you expects the client to reappear tomorrow and ask where their project is.

This is the deposit ghost, and it is more common than freelancers admit. Clients pay deposits for reasons that have nothing to do with starting work: to lock a price, to hold a slot while they decide, to show a boss they have started the process, to feel the project is underway when it is not. None of those reasons are your problem. Your problem is a held calendar slot, turned-down work, and money whose status is unclear. The fix is to treat the deposit as what it is: a scheduling fee with an expiry date, not an open-ended retainer on your time.

1. Confirm the deposit in writing the day it lands, and name what it holds

The day the deposit arrives, send a short written confirmation that does three things: thanks them, states the amount and what it secures, and names the next step with a date. "Thanks for the deposit of $1,000 received today against the agreed project fee of $2,500. This holds your start slot for the week of November 16 and covers the first milestone. To begin, I need the brief and the kickoff call booked by November 13."

That message matters because it turns a payment into an agreement. Without it, the client can later describe the deposit however suits them: as a holding fee that never expires, as a down payment on work that has not happened, as money they can ask back whenever they like. With it, the deposit has a job, a date, and a condition, all on the record before any silence starts.

Name the hold period explicitly. A deposit that holds a slot "for the project" holds it forever. A deposit that holds a slot "until November 13, after which the slot is released and rescheduled subject to availability" has a boundary the client can understand and you can enforce. Most clients respect a boundary they were told about in advance. Almost none respect a boundary they discover only when they hit it.

2. Give the silence a deadline you will actually keep

A client who has paid and gone quiet is not a client who has forgotten you. They are a client whose priorities moved, and your project is sitting in a queue behind something louder. A deadline is the thing that moves it back up. So set one, in writing, and make it a date you will genuinely enforce.

The shape is simple: "If I have not heard from you by November 13, I will release the start slot and we can reschedule when you are ready." That is not a threat. It is a statement of fact about your calendar, and it gives the client a clean, low-effort way back: reply, book the call, and the project starts. Clients who intend to proceed almost always reply to a deadline message, because it is easier to reply than to lose the slot they already paid to hold.

The deadline must be one you will keep. A deadline you extend three times is not a deadline, it is a suggestion, and the client will treat it as one. Pick a date that gives the client a fair window, usually seven to fourteen days from your first chase, then hold it. If the date passes with no reply, release the slot and tell them so. You are not cancelling the project. You are freeing a calendar you cannot hold forever on the strength of a payment and a silence.

3. Chase once, firmly, with a restart date

You do not need a five-stage escalation for a client who has already paid you. You need one firm chase, and it should arrive after a few days of silence, not after a month. The message has three parts: a factual restatement, a specific ask, and a restart date.

"I am holding your start slot for the week of November 16, as confirmed when the deposit arrived. To begin I need the brief and a thirty minute kickoff call. If we can book that by November 13, we start as planned. If not, I will release the slot and we can reschedule when you are ready."

Notice what the message does not do. It does not apologise for chasing a client who has paid you. It does not ask whether they are still interested, which invites a vague yes and another month of silence. It does not threaten to keep the deposit, because that argument is for later, if it ever comes. It simply states the position and gives the client one clear action with one clear date. One chase, sent at the right time, resolves most deposit ghosts, because most of them are not ghosts at all. They are busy people who needed a nudge with a date on it.

4. Know what the deposit covers, and say so before you spend it

The question every freelancer asks silently in this situation is whether the deposit is theirs to keep if the client never returns. The honest answer is that it depends on what you agreed, which is why the confirmation message in section one matters so much. A deposit described as a scheduling fee that holds a slot until a stated date is much easier to retain than a deposit described as nothing at all, because the client was told in advance what the money was buying.

If your terms already say deposits are non-refundable, say so plainly in the confirmation message, not in a footnote the client never read. "The deposit is non-refundable and secures your start slot until November 13." If your terms do not say that, do not invent the position mid-silence. Instead, say what the deposit has already bought: the held slot, the turned-down work, the preparation you did. And if the client does reappear months later asking for the deposit back, your answer is a calm restatement of the agreement plus the record of the deadline they missed, not an argument about fairness.

Never spend a deposit on the assumption that the client has vanished for good until the deadline you set has passed and you have told them the slot is released. Spending it early turns a clean position into a messy one, because a client who reappears to find their money spent and their slot gone has a genuine grievance, whatever the silence was. Keep the deposit identifiable until the deadline passes. After that, it is yours under the terms you set, and you can treat it as earned for the slot you held.

5. Release the slot, keep the record

When the deadline passes with no reply, release the slot and tell the client in writing. "As advised, I have released your start slot for the week of November 16. Your deposit remains held against the project, and we can reschedule a start date when you are ready, subject to availability." That message closes the open loop without closing the relationship. The client can return, and when they do, the conversation starts from a clear position rather than from a tangle of silence and resentment.

Keep the record: the deposit confirmation, the chase, the deadline message, and the release message, all in one thread. If the client returns in three months claiming the deposit should buy them an immediate start, that thread is your answer. If they ask for a refund, that thread is your evidence that the money bought a held slot with a stated expiry, and that you kept your side of the arrangement while they kept none of theirs. And if they never return, the record is what lets you close the project cleanly in your own books instead of carrying it as an open question for a year.

A deposit ghost is not a lost client. It is a client whose timing changed, and your job is to make the cost of that change fall where it belongs: on a held slot with a stated expiry, not on an open-ended claim on your calendar. Confirm what the deposit holds, set a deadline you will keep, chase once with a restart date, know what the money covers before you spend it, and release the slot in writing when the date passes. The client can always come back. But they come back to a rescheduled slot, not to a calendar you held for them indefinitely, and that distinction is worth the price of the deposit alone.

The pattern underneath

A deposit that holds a slot without a deadline holds your calendar hostage. Name what the deposit secures and until when, give the silence a date you will enforce, chase once with a clear restart, and release the slot in writing when the date passes. The money stays earned for the slot you held, the record stays clean, and the client keeps a clear path back whenever they are ready.
