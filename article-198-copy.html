The Client Who Asks You to Invoice Someone Else: How to Handle a Third-Party Payer

The brief comes from one person, but the invoice is supposed to go to another. "Invoice my business partner." "Send it to my client, they are covering it." "Put it through the company, not me personally." It sounds like an administrative detail, and it is often presented that way. It is not. It changes who owes you money, and that change can quietly remove your ability to collect it.

The risk is simple: your agreement is with the person who briefed you, but your invoice goes to someone you have no agreement with. If that third party delays, disputes, or refuses, you have no contract with them, no relationship with them, and sometimes no way to reach them. The person who briefed you can then say the debt is not theirs, because the invoice was never in their name. You did the work, but the chain of obligation broke at the invoice.

Third-party payment is not always a trick. Companies pay through holding entities, agencies bill through clients, and partners split costs legitimately. The point is not to refuse it outright. It is to keep the obligation intact while you accommodate it.

1. Keep the original client liable until the money clears

This is the single most important rule, and it should be stated in writing before any work starts: the person who briefed you remains responsible for payment until the invoice is paid in full, regardless of who the invoice is addressed to.

Put it plainly in the proposal or contract: "Invoices will be addressed to [third party] at your request. You remain liable for payment until the invoice is paid in full." That one sentence preserves the whole chain. If the third party pays, everyone is happy. If they do not, you chase the person you actually contracted with, and there is no argument about whose debt it is.

If the client will not accept that sentence, stop and think. A client who wants you to invoice someone else and also wants to be released from liability is asking you to take on an unsecured risk for their convenience. That is a different deal, and it deserves a different price, a deposit, or a refusal. Do not let the awkwardness of the conversation push you into accepting a structure you would not accept if it were stated plainly.

2. Get the third party's details in writing, from the client

Before you invoice a third party, you need more than a name. You need the full legal name, the billing address, a named contact with an email address, and confirmation of how they want to receive the invoice. Get all of it from your client in writing, not verbally, and keep it on the project thread.

This matters for two reasons. First, an invoice sent to the wrong entity, or to a generic inbox nobody monitors, is an invoice that will sit unpaid while everyone assumes someone else is handling it. Second, if the debt ever escalates, you need to show that the invoice went to the entity your client named, at the address they gave. A verbal instruction, remembered differently by each side, is no foundation for a claim.

Also confirm the third party actually expects the invoice. A short message from your client to the third party, copied to you, saying "please expect an invoice from [you] for [project], [amount], due [date]" prevents the most common failure: the third party receiving an unexpected invoice from a stranger and ignoring it, or disputing it because they never agreed to the amount. If your client will not send that introduction, that reluctance is information about how solid the arrangement really is.

3. Invoice the third party, but chase your client

When the invoice goes overdue, chase the person who briefed you, not the third party. You have no relationship with the third party, and chasing them directly can create confusion, damage your client's relationships, and give the third party a reason to claim you had no right to bill them.

Your chase messages should go to your client, with the same schedule you would use for any overdue invoice: a reminder on the due date, a firmer note stating the amount and a pay by date, and a final notice stating the consequence. The only difference is one line: "As agreed, you remain liable for this invoice until it is paid." That line, referring to the sentence you put in writing at the start, is what keeps the chase simple.

Do not let your client redirect the chase to the third party. "Talk to my partner, it is their responsibility now" is the sentence the liability clause exists to prevent. Your answer is calm and factual: the agreement was with them, the invoice was addressed elsewhere at their request, and the obligation stands until payment clears. If they genuinely cannot pay because the third party failed them, that is their problem to solve with the third party, not a reason for you to wait indefinitely.

4. Watch for the warning patterns

Most third-party arrangements are legitimate, but a few patterns should make you stop and tighten the terms before work starts.

The first is vagueness: the client cannot name the payer clearly, keeps changing the entity, or says "just invoice the company" without saying which company. Vagueness about who pays is never an administrative detail. It is the whole deal, and it should be resolved before work, not after.

The second is distance: the third party is in another country, is a company you cannot verify, or is someone the client will not introduce you to. The further the payer is from your client, the harder collection becomes, and the more a deposit matters.

The third is substitution mid-project: the client starts by paying you directly, then halfway through asks you to invoice someone else for the remaining balance. This often happens when the client's own cash flow tightens, and the new payer may have no idea the debt exists. Treat a mid-project switch as a new arrangement: get the liability sentence in writing again, get the third party's details, and consider requiring the outstanding balance to be settled before the switch takes effect.

Any one of these patterns is a reason to require a deposit, shorten the terms, or decline the structure. All three together are a reason to decline the project unless payment comes directly from the person you contracted with.

5. Build the structure into your standard terms

The easiest way to handle third-party payment is to stop treating it as an exception. Add a short clause to your standard terms now, before the next request arrives, so the conversation is never improvised under pressure.

A clause like this covers it: "If you ask us to invoice a third party, you remain liable for payment until the invoice is paid in full. You will provide the third party's full billing details and confirm they expect the invoice. Payment terms, late fees, and work pause provisions apply as normal." That is three sentences, and it answers every question the next request will raise.

With the clause in place, the conversation becomes easy. The client asks to invoice someone else, you say yes, and the clause does the work. The clients who intend to pay accept it without argument, because it changes nothing for them. The clients who object to it are the ones the clause exists to protect you from, and their objection arrives before you have done any work, which is exactly when you want it.

The pattern underneath

A client who asks you to invoice someone else is changing who owes you money, and the only safe answer is a structure that keeps the obligation intact: the original client stays liable until payment clears, the third party's details arrive in writing, the chase goes to your client, the warning patterns trigger tighter terms, and the whole arrangement lives in your standard terms rather than in an improvised conversation. Accommodating a third-party payer is good service. Giving up your claim on the person who briefed you is not service, it is an unsecured loan to a stranger. Keep the chain unbroken, and the invoice goes to whoever the client names, while the debt stays exactly where it belongs.
