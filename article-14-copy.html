The Check Is in the Mail: The 5 Stall Lines Late Payers Use, and the Exact Replies That End Them

Late-paying clients rarely say "I'm not paying." They say things that sound almost reasonable. Each one buys them another week while you wait politely. The trick is not getting angry. The trick is recognizing the line, and answering it with one reply that turns vagueness into a date.

Here are the five you will hear, and what to say back.

1. "It's with our accounting department."

The most common stall line in corporate clients. It may even be true. Your reply does not dispute it; it just puts a name and a date on it:

"Thanks for the update. Could you share the name of the person in accounting handling this, and the date they confirmed payment? I will follow up with them directly so you don't have to chase this internally."

Why it works: you have moved the conversation from "somewhere in accounting" to a named human with a committed date. If no name comes back, you know it was a stall.

2. "The CEO still needs to approve it."

Translation: your invoice is sitting in a pile of someone's non-urgent tasks. Do not ask when the CEO will get around to it. Ask what the normal cycle is, then set the date yourself:

"Understood. What is the usual approval cycle, a few days or the next meeting? I'd like to note a date in my system so I can follow up once, instead of checking in repeatedly."

You are framing your follow-up as a favor to them. You are also getting a date on record, which is the entire game.

3. "We pay on a 60-day cycle."

This one arrives as a policy, delivered as if it were law. But payment terms were supposed to be agreed before the work started, not announced after the invoice. If net 60 was never agreed, it does not apply retroactively. Reply:

"I appreciate you flagging this. My terms on this project were Net 14, which we agreed on before the work began. I'm happy to align on terms for future work, but this invoice is due [date]. Can we get it processed on the agreed terms?"

Calm, contractual, no threats. You are referencing the agreement, not their policy. This is exactly why terms go on the invoice and in the contract before work begins.

4. "Can you resend the invoice? I can't find it."

Sometimes genuine, often a reset button: resending restarts their mental clock. Do not just resend and wait. Resend, restate the due date, and tighten the loop:

"Resending now, attached. For clarity: the original invoice was sent on [date], with payment due [date]. Nothing about the terms has changed, this is just a fresh copy for your records."

The original due date stands. Saying it out loud prevents the "we just received it" reset.

5. "Next week, for sure."

The friendliest stall line, and the most expensive, because it is easy to accept and impossible to act on. Accept it, but attach it to the calendar:

"Great, I'll hold you to next week. Which day should I expect it, and would you like me to send a reminder that morning, or will it go out automatically?"

A named day plus a named mechanism. If next week comes and goes with no payment, your next email does not ask for money. It references the broken commitment: "On [date] you confirmed payment for [day]. It hasn't arrived." That single sentence carries more weight than any demand, because it documents the pattern.

The rule underneath all five

Every stall line is a sentence without a date in it. Your reply's only job is to put the date back in: a named person, a named day, a named mechanism. Do this in writing, in the same email thread, so the whole pattern is visible in one place if you ever need to escalate.
