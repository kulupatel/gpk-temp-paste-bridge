Getting Paid by Agencies: How to Survive the 60-Day Subcontractor Wait

Agency subcontracting is steady work with terrible payment terms. The agency hires you to build the thing for their client, you do great work, and then you wait: Net 30 turns into Net 60, Net 60 turns into "we pay when the client pays us," and suddenly you are lending a business with thirty employees an interest-free loan.

This is not an accident. Agencies are cash-flow middlemen. They bill their client, wait to be paid, then pay you. Your job is to not be the last in that line. Here is how to protect yourself.

1. Ask for the payment terms before you accept the work, not after

Most freelancers ask about the rate and forget to ask about the terms. The rate is only half the deal. Before you say yes, ask: "What are your payment terms for contractors?" If the answer is Net 30, fine. If it is Net 60, or "we pay at the end of the month following invoice," that is a real cost, and you should price for it.

An agency paying Net 60 on a $5,000 invoice is asking you to finance them for two months. If you cannot afford to wait, say so upfront and propose Net 15 or a monthly retainer instead. Agencies negotiate terms with their vendors all the time. You are a vendor.

2. Never accept "paid when paid" clauses

Some agency contracts include a clause that says you get paid when the agency gets paid by their client. This means your payment depends on a relationship you are not part of, a negotiation you cannot see, and a delay you cannot influence. If the agency's client pays late, you pay late, with no recourse.

Push back on this line every time. The standard replacement language is simple: payment terms are between you and the agency, period. If they insist, ask them to cap it: "if the client has not paid within 60 days, the agency pays in full." An agency that will not cap it is telling you they cannot afford to carry the risk. Believe them.

3. Invoice the same day, to the right person

Agencies often run invoice batches: everything received by the 15th gets paid in the next cycle, everything after waits a month. Ask directly: "What is your invoice cut-off, and who should I send invoices to?" Send your invoice on the last day of work, not the first of next month. A five-day delay on your side can mean a 30-day delay on theirs.

Get the name of the person in the agency's finance team, not just your project contact. When an invoice goes quiet, the project manager's "I will check with finance" is the slowest possible path. A direct line to the person who actually releases payments is the fastest.

4. Put a late-fee clause in your agency contract

Agencies pay thousands of vendor invoices a year and they prioritize the ones with consequences. Your invoice sits in a queue with the hosting provider and the stock-photo subscription. A stated late fee (5% per month, or a flat $75 after 30 days) moves you up that queue because now ignoring you has a cost.

Frame it as standard practice, not a threat: "I charge all clients a 5% monthly late fee after 30 days, it is in my standard terms." Agencies see vendor late fees constantly. They will not be offended. They will, however, pay the fee-carrying invoices first.

5. Keep a running tally of what you are owed

Agency work often means multiple open invoices at different ages. When you have three invoices at 20, 45, and 70 days, chase the oldest first and never chase them all in one email. One email per invoice, each with its number, date, and amount, keeps the paper trail clean and makes it easy for finance to match payments.

A simple spreadsheet with invoice number, date, amount, terms, and days outstanding is enough. Check it every Monday. The moment an invoice crosses its terms, the reminder goes out. Agencies pay the squeakiest wheels, and a consistent weekly check-in makes you one.

The pattern underneath

Agency payment problems are structural, not personal. The agency is not trying to stiff you; it is managing its own cash flow at your expense. Every rule above moves you from the end of their payment queue to the front: clear terms upfront, no paid-when-paid clauses, same-day invoicing, stated late fees, and a weekly chase habit. You cannot change how agencies pay. You can change where your invoice sits in their stack.
