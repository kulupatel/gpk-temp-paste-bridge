When Your Client Gets Acquired: What Happens to Your Unpaid Invoices

The press release goes out on a Tuesday. Your client, the one holding $8,400 of your invoices, has been acquired by a bigger company. Your first thought should not be congratulations. It should be: where does my money go now?

Acquisitions are one of the most common ways invoices quietly die. The company still exists, the money is still there, and yet your invoice floats through a reorganization for months while nobody owns it. Here is how to protect it.

The announcement window is your best moment

The days between the announcement and the actual transition are the single best time to get paid. Your AP contact still has their job, still has their budget codes, and is highly motivated to clear outstanding items before the new owners start asking questions. Do not wait for the dust to settle. The dust is where invoices disappear.

Send this the week of the announcement:

"Congratulations on the news. To keep things clean during the transition, could we settle the outstanding balance of $8,400 this week? Happy to resend the invoices as a fresh package if that helps."

You are framing payment as a favor to them, and it genuinely is one. Clean books make the transition easier for everyone.

Your invoice can legally survive the acquisition

Most of the time, the acquiring company inherits the acquired company's contracts and liabilities, including your unpaid invoices. But "most of the time" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. If your client is being dissolved into the parent rather than continuing as a subsidiary, your contract may need to be re-signed or re-issued under the new entity name. Until that happens, nobody's AP system recognizes your invoice, because the vendor entity they paid under no longer exists.

So ask, in writing: "Is my agreement assigned to the new entity, or do we need a new one?" Get the answer in writing too. Verbal reassurances from people who are about to leave the company are worth nothing.

Find the new owner before your invoice is orphaned

After an acquisition, your AP contact is often the first casualty. New finance teams bring their own processes, their own vendor portals, and sometimes a freeze on "legacy vendor" payments while they audit. If you have not introduced yourself to the new AP owner, your invoice sits in a queue labeled "old company" that nobody reviews.

Your job in the first month after the deal closes:

  • Get the new AP contact's name and email in writing. Ask your old contact before they leave, or the client sponsor directly.
  • Re-send every outstanding invoice as a clean package. One email, all invoices attached, a table showing invoice number, date, amount, and total. Do not assume anything carried over.
  • Confirm the new entity's billing details. Legal name, billing address, tax IDs, and whatever the new portal requires. Get it in writing before you invoice under the new name.
  • Ask about the transition payment cycle. Merged companies often run two cycles for months. Know which one you are on.

The two sentences that protect you going forward

If you freelance for startups or mid-size companies, acquisitions are not a rare event. They are a background risk. Add this to your contract once and it covers you every time:

"This agreement may be assigned to a successor entity in the event of a merger, acquisition, or change of control, and the successor shall honor all outstanding payment obligations under their original terms."

One sentence, and you never have to argue about whether the new owners owe you. They do, and the contract says so.

If the invoice stalls anyway

Sometimes the transition freezes everything for 60 to 90 days and your invoice is stuck. Do not panic, but do not go quiet either. Keep one polite chase per two weeks in a single thread, escalate to your client sponsor at day 45, and treat the acquiring company's finance team as a brand-new relationship: you need to be re-onboarded as a vendor, and that is a normal process, not an insult.

The freelancer who survives an acquisition is the one who acted in the announcement window and documented everything. The one who lost the invoice is the one who waited for the dust to settle.
