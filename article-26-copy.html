The Float Game: Why Big Companies Pay Late on Purpose, and How Freelancers Beat It

There is a special frustration reserved for freelancers who invoice large companies. The work was excellent. The client loved it. The invoice was perfect. And the payment arrives 75 days later, with nobody at the company feeling even slightly bad about it.

That is not an accident. It is a system.

Late payment is a corporate strategy, not a mistake

Big companies manage something called working capital: the difference between what they owe and what they are owed. Finance teams are measured on how long they can hold cash before paying suppliers. Every day a supplier is not paid is a day the company keeps earning interest on that money. Multiply by thousands of suppliers, and slow payment becomes a serious line item.

This is why your $4,000 invoice sat for two months while the company's marketing team praised your work in meetings. Nobody lost the invoice. The approval chain just moved at the speed that suited their cash position, not yours. Understanding this changes everything, because you stop taking late payment personally and start designing around it.

The three moves companies use

1. Unilateral payment terms in vendor portals. To get onboarded, you accept their standard terms: Net 60, Net 90, sometimes worse. You click accept because you want the work. From their side, your signature is the whole justification for paying you three months late.

2. The approval-chain ping-pong. Your invoice goes from your contact to procurement to accounts payable to a manager who is on leave. Each handoff resets the clock. Some of this is genuine bureaucracy. Some of it is bureaucracy being useful to someone.

3. Paid-when-paid and contingent approvals. Some contracts tie your payment to their client paying them, or to an internal approval that nobody schedules. You did the work in week one and your payment is hostage to someone else's timeline.

How freelancers beat the float game

You cannot outspend a corporate treasury. You can out-structure it.

Negotiate payment terms at contracting, not at chasing. When the vendor portal says Net 60, ask your actual contact: "Can we make this Net 15 for invoices under $10,000?" Often the answer is yes, because the portal terms are a default, not a law. Anything agreed in writing beats the default.

Split large invoices into smaller milestones. One $20,000 invoice is a treasury decision. Four $5,000 invoices are routine expenses that clear through normal approval. Smaller invoices also get approved by people with actual authority, faster.

Put a price on speed. Add an early-payment line to your terms: a small discount for payment within 10 days, or a stated late fee after the due date. Many AP departments will prioritize invoices that cost more when delayed. You are not being aggressive. You are giving them a reason to put you in the fast pile.

Get the AP contact's name at kickoff. Do not send invoices to your champion and hope they forward them. Ask on day one: "Who should invoices go to, and what do they need on it?" A named billing contact and a PO number collapse the approval chain.

Schedule the follow-up before you send. Put day-7, day-14, and day-30 follow-up dates in your calendar the moment the invoice goes out. With big companies, the freelancer who follows up on schedule gets paid first. The silent one gets paid whenever.

Keep a pause clause in your terms. "Work pauses if any invoice is more than 30 days overdue." You will almost never need to use it, but its presence changes how seriously your due date is treated.

Pick your battles

Not every slow payer is worth fighting. If a big client pays at Net 45 like clockwork and the work is great, that is a predictable cash-flow input. Price it in and enjoy the stability. Reserve the pressure tactics for the ones that drift from 45 to 90 with no explanation. The goal is not to punish companies. It is to make your payment arrive on the schedule you planned your life around.

The float game only works on freelancers who accept the default terms, send one big invoice, and wait quietly. Structure the engagement differently and the same company pays you in two weeks.
