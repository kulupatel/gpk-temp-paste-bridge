Befriend the AP Desk: How the Client's Finance Person Gets You Paid Faster

Your client contact loves your work. They approved the invoice the day you sent it. And then nothing happened for six weeks, because your contact does not pay you. Somewhere in the client's company sits a person in accounts payable who has never heard your name, and your invoice is one line in their queue.

Most freelancers chase the wrong person. They email the project manager, who forwards it to finance, who asks for the PO number nobody mentioned, and the invoice restarts its journey from zero. The fix is embarrassingly simple: find the person who actually releases the money, and make their job easy.

Your contact is not the payer

On any project with a company bigger than a handful of people, the person who hired you and the person who pays you are different humans with different inboxes. The hirer approves; accounts payable processes. Your invoice has to survive both, and the second one is where invoices go to wait.

This is good news once you see it. The AP person is not your adversary. They are a professional whose job is paying vendors, and they pay the easy vendors first. Be the easy vendor and your invoice rises to the top of the queue without a single chase email.

Get the AP contact at kickoff, not at crisis

The single highest-leverage question you can ask at the start of a project: "Who should I send the invoice to, and what do they need on it?" Ask it in the kickoff call, in writing, before you do any work. You want four things: the AP email address (often something like ap@ or finance@, sometimes a named person), their payment cycle (many companies run payments on fixed days), whether they need a purchase order number, and whether you must register as a vendor first.

Vendor registration is the silent killer. Some companies cannot pay anyone who is not in their system, and registration takes days or weeks. If you discover this after delivering the work, your Net 30 starts whenever the paperwork finishes, not when you sent the invoice. Ask on day one: "Is there a vendor form or tax form I should complete now?" For US clients, have your W-8BEN (individuals) or W-8BEN-E (companies) ready before the first invoice; without it, payment can be held or tax withheld.

Get an email introduction to the AP contact if you can. A two-line intro from your project contact ("This is Maya, she will be invoicing us for the website redesign") turns you from a stranger into a known vendor. Known vendors get paid; strangers get "we will look into it."

Learn their batch cycle

Most companies do not pay invoices the day they arrive. They batch them: payments go out on specific days, often weekly or twice monthly. An invoice that arrives the day after a payment run waits a full cycle. An invoice that arrives the day before gets paid this week.

You cannot control the cycle, but you can time yourself to it. Once you know the client pays on, say, the 1st and 15th, send invoices to land a few days before. And invoice on a schedule the client expects, the 1st of the month for retainers, the day a milestone is approved, so your invoice is never a surprise.

Make your invoice processable on first touch

Every back-and-forth with AP adds days. The invoice that gets paid fastest is the one that needs no reply. That means: the PO number on the invoice (exactly as issued, no paraphrasing), your legal business name matching whatever is in their vendor system, the client's official billing address, a clear invoice number and date, the amount matching the contract or PO to the cent, and your payment details on the invoice itself, not in a separate email.

If the client uses a vendor portal like Coupa, Ariba, or Bill.com, ask for the account setup link before your first invoice. Portal invoicing has more upfront friction but usually pays faster than email billing, because it skips the human routing entirely.

Consistency compounds. Use the same name, same format, same details every time. The first invoice sets you up in their system; every invoice after that should look like it came from the same vendor, because it did.

The polite persistence rules

When an invoice goes quiet, email AP directly, not your project contact. Keep it to one thread per invoice so the history is visible. Reference the invoice number and the PO in the subject line. Be specific about what you need: "Could you confirm this is scheduled for the next payment run?" beats "Just checking in."

And say thank you when they pay. AP people process hundreds of invoices from vendors who only ever contact them to complain. A short "Received, thank you" makes you memorable in the right way. The next time your invoice and a stranger's invoice are both in the queue, guess which one gets a friendly nudge forward.

What never to do

Do not go around AP to the CEO when an invoice is late. It poisons the one relationship that controls your money, and executives forward it straight back to AP with an annoyed note attached to your name. Do not change your bank details by email without a verification call; finance teams are trained to treat that as fraud, and yours will be the invoice that triggers a security review. Do not send the invoice only to your project contact and assume it reaches finance. And never be rude to AP, no matter how late the payment is. They did not cause the delay, and they are the fastest path to ending it.

The bottom line

You do not need leverage over the finance department. You need a name, an email address, their payment cycle, and invoices that need no reply. Get those four things at kickoff and most of your "late payment" problems turn out to have been routing problems, which are free to fix.
