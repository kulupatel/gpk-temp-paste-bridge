The Client Who Asks You to Resend the Invoice Every Month: How to Break the Resend Stall

The invoice went out on the 1st. On the 15th, the client replies: "Can you resend that? I can't find it." You resend it. On the 30th: "Sorry, still can't locate it, can you send it again?" You resend it again. By the third resend, the invoice is two months old and the client has never once said they cannot pay. They have said, every time, that they cannot find it. This is the resend stall, and it works because every resend resets the clock in the client's mind. Here is how to break it.

1. Stop resending the same invoice into the same void

The first resend is reasonable. The second is a pattern. From the second request on, change what you send: not just the invoice, but the invoice plus a short paper trail in the body of the email. "Attached again: invoice #1042 for $X, originally sent March 1, resent March 15, due March 15." That single sentence changes the dynamic. The client can no longer treat each resend as a fresh start, because the email itself records the history. You are not accusing anyone of lying. You are simply making the record visible, and a visible record is much harder to stall against than a fresh attachment.

2. Change the channel, not just the attachment

If an invoice keeps "going missing" by email, stop relying on email alone. Send it through a second channel the client actually uses: their project management tool, their accounts portal, or a shared drive folder with a link you can track. Then confirm in writing: "Invoice #1042 is now also uploaded to [portal], and I have emailed it to you and to [accounts contact]." The point is not to be difficult. The point is to remove "I never got it" from the list of available excuses. Every payment delay needs a reason, and "lost invoice" is the cheapest reason there is. Make it unavailable, and the real reason surfaces, whether that is cash flow, approval delays, or simple disorganisation.

3. Ask for the name behind the inbox

The resend stall often hides a routing problem: your invoice is landing with someone who cannot approve it. After the second resend request, ask directly: "Who handles invoice approval on your side, and what email should future invoices go to?" Then send the invoice to that person, copy your original contact, and note the change: "Future invoices will go to [name] at [email], as confirmed." Clients who were genuinely losing the invoice will be relieved. Clients who were stalling lose their cover, because the invoice is now sitting with the one person whose job is to pay it. Either way, you have fixed the routing, and routing fixes are permanent in a way that resends never are.

4. Put a read receipt on the conversation, not the email

Chasing a read receipt on an email is a weak move, and most clients ignore it. What works better is a confirmation request inside a normal message: "Could you confirm you have invoice #1042 this time, so I know it reached you?" A one-line confirmation turns the next "I can't find it" from a plausible accident into a contradiction of their own message. Keep the tone light and practical; you are asking for a favour, not building a court case. But once a client has confirmed receipt in writing, the stall loses its power, because every future delay needs a new reason, and new reasons are harder to invent than old ones are to repeat.

5. Make the next invoice stall-proof from day one

The permanent fix is structural. On the next project, agree the invoice route before work starts: the payer's name and email, the portal or system they use, and a confirmation step within two days of sending. Write it into your onboarding email: "Invoices go to [name] at [email]. Please confirm receipt within two working days; if I do not hear back, I will follow up on day three." Then follow up on day three, every time. A client who knows the follow-up is coming on day three rarely needs it. The resend stall only works on freelancers who wait a month before asking. A three-day confirmation rhythm makes "lost invoice" a two-day problem, not a two-month one.

The pattern underneath

A lost invoice is a solvable problem. A repeatedly lost invoice is a delay tactic, whether the client means it that way or not. The fix is the same either way: make the record visible, change the channel, get the payer's name, confirm receipt in writing, and lock the route in before the next project starts. You do not need a better invoice. You need an invoice that cannot get lost twice.
