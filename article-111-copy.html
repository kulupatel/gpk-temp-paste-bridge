The Client Who Pays Late But Keeps Ordering More: How to Handle the Growing Delinquent Account

There is a particular kind of client who is both your best source of work and your worst source of cash. They love what you do. They keep ordering more. And they pay every invoice 45 days late. The pipeline looks fantastic and the bank balance looks thin, and saying anything feels risky, because who argues with their biggest client?

You do. A growing delinquent account is not a good problem; it is a concentration risk wearing a friendly face. Every new order increases the amount of money they owe you while the old invoices sit unpaid. If you keep this up for six months, you will have a client who owes you five figures and total leverage over your cash flow. Handle it now, while the amount is small enough to survive a disagreement.

Separate the new work from the old debt

The first rule: new orders do not reset old invoices. Clients will act as if they do, usually without saying it. The project manager who sent you the new brief is often not the person who pays your invoices, and they may not even know the old ones are overdue. Treat every order as a separate transaction with its own terms, and treat every overdue invoice as a separate debt with its own chase schedule. Conflating them is how a $2,000 overdue balance quietly becomes $14,000.

Make current payment a condition of new scope

The cleanest mechanism is the simplest: work does not continue until the account is current. This sounds aggressive until you say it plainly. "Happy to start phase two. Before we do, I need the phase one invoices brought current, since the new agreement builds on the old one." Deliver it in writing, warmly, at the moment they order more. Most clients understand this instantly; it is how every supplier they work with operates. The ones who push back hard are telling you something valuable about how the relationship will end.

Require deposits on the new orders

Even with the old debt being paid down, change the structure going forward. A client who pays 45 days late gets milestone billing with a kickoff deposit, not Net 30 on the full amount. Frame it as standard: "For engagements of this size, I work with a 40 percent deposit and milestone payments." A deposit does two things: it cuts your exposure in half, and it tests whether the late payment is a process problem (they can pay a deposit fast) or a policy problem (they cannot pay anything fast). You need to know which one you are dealing with.

Put the account in writing, once

When the pattern is established, send one summary email. "I wanted to flag the current account position: invoices #123 ($X, due May 1) and #124 ($Y, due May 15) are outstanding, totaling $Z. Phase two is ready to kick off once the account is current." This is not a threat; it is a statement of fact with a number in it. Numbers make the situation concrete in a way that "a few invoices are late" never does. Keep a copy. This email is the foundation of every escalation step if things go sideways.

Watch for the growth trap

The real danger is not the current overdue balance; it is the trajectory. A client ordering more while paying late is expanding your exposure every month. Set a hard ceiling for yourself: decide the maximum unpaid balance you will carry for this client, write it down, and stop starting new work when you hit it. If the ceiling is $8,000 and they owe $8,000, the next order waits. Freelancers who do not set a ceiling always discover their number in hindsight, when the client owes them an amount they cannot afford to lose.

When growth and payment finally align

Handled well, this conversation strengthens the relationship. Clients who value your work will usually fix their payment process once they realize it is the only thing standing between them and your capacity. Get the agreement in writing: current terms, deposit structure, the ceiling, the consequence (work pauses at 30 days overdue). Then keep delivering great work. The goal was never to punish your biggest client. It was to make sure your biggest client pays like one.
