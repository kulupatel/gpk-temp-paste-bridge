The Client Who Pays the Wrong Amount Every Time: How to Stop Short Payments and Mystery Deductions

The invoice says $1,200. The payment arrives for $1,150. No note, no explanation, no apology. Or the payment arrives for the full amount minus a fee you never agreed to, or minus an hour the client decided not to count, or rounded down to a number that looked tidier to them. When it happens once, it is an annoyance. When it happens on every invoice, it is a slow leak in your income, and chasing the difference often costs more time than the difference itself.

That last point is exactly why the pattern survives. A $50 shortfall is too small to fight over and too large to ignore across a year of invoices. The client knows it, even if they never say so. They are not necessarily dishonest. Often they are careless, or their finance team applies a standard deduction, or someone on their side genuinely believes the fee was different. Your job is to make the correct amount the easiest amount to pay, and to make a short payment more work for them than paying in full.

1. Treat every short payment as a pattern worth one conversation

The first time a payment arrives short, do not just accept it and move on. The silence is the agreement. A short payment that passes without comment becomes the new price, and the next invoice will be short by the same amount, because the client's system now believes that amount is correct.

Raise it on the same thread, promptly, and with the numbers visible. Something like: "Thanks for the payment. I received $1,150 against invoice 42 for $1,200, so $50 is still outstanding. Could you let me know what the difference relates to?" This is not a confrontation. It is a reconciliation question, and it forces the difference into the open where it can be named: a bank fee, a currency conversion, a withheld hour, a discount the client assumed.

What you are listening for is whether the reason is a one off error or a policy. An error gets corrected and the conversation is over. A policy, such as "we always deduct a processing fee" or "we pay net of bank charges", is a term you never agreed to, and it needs to be dealt with as a term, not as a series of small errors.

2. Put the exact amount beyond argument before the work starts

Most short payments are born in ambiguity. The fee was agreed in a call, the scope shifted slightly, the invoice rounds differently from the quote, and the client picks the version that favours them. The fix is boring and it works: put the exact amount, in writing, before the work starts.

Your quote or agreement should state the fee, the currency, whether tax is included, who pays bank or transfer fees, and what happens if the scope changes. Your invoice should match that agreement line for line, with the same numbers, the same description, and the same reference. When the invoice and the agreement say the same thing, a short payment has nowhere to hide. The client cannot plausibly claim they thought the fee was lower, because the lower number exists nowhere in writing.

This also protects you on scope. If the client deducts an hour because they decided part of the work was unnecessary, the written scope is your answer: the hour was in the agreed scope, it was delivered, and it is payable. Without that written scope, the deduction becomes a debate about memory, and memory always favours the person holding the money.

3. Make deductions require a written reason

Add one sentence to your payment terms, and enforce it every time: any deduction from an invoice must be notified in writing, with the reason, before the payment date. This single rule changes the economics of short paying. A client who deducts $50 silently pays $50 less for free. A client who must write down "we deducted $50 for bank charges" has to defend a charge you never agreed to, in a message you can answer.

When a deduction arrives without that written reason, your reply is short and repeatable: "I received $X against invoice $Y. No deduction was notified before the payment date, so the balance of $Z remains due. Please either pay the balance or send the deduction reason in writing." Do not debate the deduction in the abstract. Ask for the reason, in writing, every time. Most unexplained deductions stop at this point, because the reason, once written down, is usually not defensible.

If the reason is bank or transfer fees, the answer is in your terms: the client pays the transfer fees, or you quote a fee that absorbs them, but the choice is yours and it is made in advance, not deducted after the fact. If the reason is a disputed item, the disputed item is handled as a dispute, which means the undisputed balance is paid now and only the disputed part is discussed. A dispute about one hour never justifies short paying the whole invoice.

4. Stop the leak at the source: change how you are paid

If the same client shorts every invoice despite the written agreement and the deduction rule, the payment method itself may be the problem. Bank transfers invite deductions, because the sender controls the amount and can adjust it on the way out. A payment link, a card payment, or an invoicing platform that presents the exact amount and collects it as stated removes that freedom. The client clicks and pays the invoice total, not a number they typed.

Where a transfer is unavoidable, invoice in the client's currency where possible, and state the amount due in that currency, so conversion cannot quietly shave the total. And invoice promptly, while the work is fresh and the agreed amount is undisputed. An invoice sent weeks after delivery, when memories have faded, is far easier to short than one sent the day the work is approved.

There is also a structural option worth considering for repeat offenders: payment in advance, or a deposit that covers the first invoice in full, so that the short payment problem moves to a smaller balance or disappears entirely. A client who shorts invoices rarely shorts a deposit, because the deposit is the price of starting the work, and starting the work is something they want.

5. Decide when the difference is worth pursuing, and when it is worth ending

Not every shortfall deserves a full escalation. A $5 rounding difference on a $5,000 invoice is not worth an hour of your time, and saying so is good business. But the decision should be yours, made once, not made for you by silence every month. Set a threshold in advance: below it, you note the difference and move on; above it, you chase the balance on the same schedule as any late payment, because a short payment is a late payment for the unpaid part.

Track it. A simple note on each invoice, paid in full or short by how much, turns a vague feeling that "this client always shorts me" into a number: $340 short across six invoices this year. That number is what you take into the rate conversation, or the terms conversation, or the decision to stop working with the client. Without it, you are arguing from irritation. With it, you are arguing from a ledger.

If the total keeps growing after you have fixed the agreement, enforced the deduction rule, and changed the payment method, you have your answer. This is a client who pays less than agreed, on purpose, repeatedly. Raise the rate to cover the expected shortfall, move them to payment in advance, or let them go. A client who pays the wrong amount every time is not a client who made a mistake. They are a client who found a discount you never offered, and the only way to close it is to stop offering it.

The pattern underneath

A short payment is a late payment for the unpaid part, and it deserves the same system: the amount agreed in writing before the work, a rule that deductions need a written reason, a payment method that collects the exact total, and a ledger that tells you what the pattern actually costs. Fix the ambiguity first, because most short payments live there. Then enforce the rule every time, because a rule enforced once in a while is not a rule at all. The goal is simple: the invoice amount is the payment amount, and anything less is a balance, not a rounding error.
