The Client Who Asks You to Remove the Late Fee Clause Before Signing: How to Hold the Term Without Losing the Deal

It usually comes up politely, near the end of a negotiation that is otherwise going well. The client likes your work, agrees your price, and then asks for one small change to the contract: take out the late fee clause. They will pay on time, they say, so the clause is unnecessary, and removing it shows trust. It sounds reasonable, and it is tempting to agree, because the deal is right there and the clause feels like a detail. It is not a detail. The late fee clause is the one term that makes every other payment term enforceable, and removing it before signing tells the client that your payment terms are negotiable.

Holding the term does not mean losing the deal. It means explaining, briefly and calmly, why the clause exists, and offering a version the client can accept without gutting it.

1. Understand what the client is really asking

Most clients who ask for the clause to be removed are not planning to pay late. They are reacting to how the clause feels: punitive, distrustful, or one-sided. Some have been burned by suppliers who used fees aggressively. Others simply dislike signing anything that mentions penalties. A few are testing whether your terms bend under pressure, and those few are exactly the clients the clause protects you against, though you cannot know which kind you are dealing with yet.

So do not treat the request as an accusation or as a confession. Treat it as a question about fairness, and answer it on that level. The clause is not a punishment you hope to use. It is a shared rule that makes prompt payment the normal outcome, and it applies to you as much as to them, because it is written into a contract both sides sign.

2. Explain the clause in one plain paragraph

A long legal defence will lose the client. A short, plain explanation usually wins them. The version that works is something like this: the clause exists so that late payment has a defined cost, which means neither side has to argue about what happens if a payment slips. It keeps the relationship professional if something goes wrong, because the consequence is already agreed rather than invented in the middle of a dispute. And it is rarely triggered, precisely because it is there.

Keep the numbers modest and standard when you explain them, because a modest fee is easy to defend and a large one invites exactly the argument you are trying to avoid. A small monthly percentage, or a fixed administrative fee after a grace period, reads as a business term. A large penalty reads as a threat, and threats are what make clients ask for removal in the first place.

3. Offer a softer version, never a removal

If the client still hesitates, offer to soften the clause rather than remove it. There are several honest softenings that keep the protection intact. A grace period of seven days before any fee applies is often enough, because it removes the fear of being charged for a one-day banking delay. A cap on total fees gives the client certainty about the worst case. A first-late-payment waiver, recorded in writing, shows goodwill without setting a precedent for every future delay.

What you should not offer is a clause that only applies if you chase, or a fee that needs a separate agreement each time it is triggered, because both turn an automatic term into a negotiation you will have to win again on every late invoice. The line to hold is simple: the fee stays in the contract, automatic and modest. The grace period, the cap, and the first-time waiver are all negotiable. The existence of the clause is not.

4. Watch what the request tells you about the client

How a client responds to your explanation is useful information, and you should pay attention to it before you sign. A client who accepts the clause after a short explanation is usually a client who pays on time anyway, and the clause will never matter. A client who accepts a softened version has negotiated in good faith and will probably respect the terms they signed.

A client who insists on full removal, after a plain explanation and a reasonable softening, is telling you that they want your payment terms to be unenforceable. That is a risk signal, not a deal breaker on its own, but it should change how you structure the engagement: a larger deposit, milestone payments, shorter payment terms, or a work-pause clause become more important, not less. If the client refuses both the clause and the deposit, you have your answer about the deal, and it is better to learn it before signing than after the first invoice goes unpaid.

5. Put the agreed version in writing, and keep it consistent

Whatever version you agree, write it into the contract before signing, in the same plain language you used in the conversation, and use the same version for every client. A clause that varies wildly from client to client is hard to defend and easy to resent, while a standard term, applied to everyone, is simply how you work. If the client later claims they did not understand the fee, your consistent wording and your earlier explanation are your answer.

Consistency also protects you commercially. Clients talk to each other, and a supplier whose terms bend for whoever pushes hardest will be pushed by everyone. A supplier whose terms are standard, modest, and explained once is a supplier clients take seriously, and that seriousness is worth more than any single deal.

The pattern underneath

A request to remove the late fee clause is a request to make your payment terms unenforceable, dressed as a gesture of trust. So understand what the client is really asking, explain the clause in one plain paragraph, offer a softer version but never a removal, read the client's response as information about risk, and keep the agreed version consistent for every client. The clause is not there because you expect late payment. It is there so that prompt payment stays the easy, normal outcome for everyone.
