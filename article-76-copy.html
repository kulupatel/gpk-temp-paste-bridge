The Chargeback Defense: What to Do When a Client Reverses a Payment

You delivered the work. The client paid. The money landed. And then, weeks later, it un-landed. A chargeback is when the client's bank or card network pulls a payment back, and for freelancers who take PayPal or card payments, it is one of the nastiest surprises in the business. The client keeps the work, you lose the money, and you get a fee for the privilege.

Chargebacks were designed for stolen-card fraud, not for freelancers. But clients abuse them as a free refund mechanism. Here is how to defend yourself, and how to make your next invoice chargeback-proof.

What actually happens in a chargeback

When a client disputes a card or PayPal payment, the processor debits your account immediately and opens a case. You get a short window (usually 7 to 20 days depending on the processor) to submit evidence. If you win, the money comes back. If you lose or miss the window, it is gone, plus a dispute fee (PayPal charges around $15 to $30 depending on the plan; Stripe charges $15 per dispute).

The most important thing to understand: the processor is not judging who is right in any moral sense. It is checking whether the transaction looks legitimate under its rules. Your evidence packet has to answer three questions: did this person agree to pay this amount, did you deliver what was promised, and was the buyer who they claimed to be.

Build your evidence packet the day the dispute arrives

Do not wait. The clock starts immediately, and a thin submission loses. Your packet needs:

  • The written agreement. The contract, proposal, or even the email thread where the client agreed to the scope and price. A signed contract is best; a clear "yes, let's do it" in email is good enough.
  • Proof of delivery. The files you delivered, the live URL, the sign-off email from the client saying the work looks good. Anything where the client confirmed receipt and satisfaction is gold.
  • The communication trail. Every invoice, every reminder, the client's responses. If the client paid and then went quiet, that silence is evidence too.
  • Proof the cardholder is your client. The email address on the payment matching your client's email, the IP logs if your invoice tool shows them, the client's name on the contract.

Most freelancers lose chargebacks because they submit nothing, or submit a one-line "the client is wrong." Processors decide on paperwork, not on who is more upset.

The representment letter, in plain language

You submit a short written statement with the evidence. Keep it factual and boring:

The buyer engaged my services on [date] for [scope], agreed in writing to the price of [amount], received the deliverables on [date], and confirmed satisfaction on [date]. The dispute reason given does not match the transaction history. I request the dispute be resolved in my favor.

No emotion, no accusations about the client's character. One page of facts plus the attachments. Processors read hundreds of these a day; the ones that win are the ones that make the decision easy.

Win the war before the dispute: prevention

The best chargeback defense is never needing one. Three structural habits:

  • Take deposits through the same channel every time. A client whose card paid the deposit is a verified cardholder for the balance. Mixed channels (deposit by bank transfer, balance by card) weaken your identity evidence.
  • Get written sign-off on delivery. A one-line "all looks great, thanks" from the client is the single strongest piece of chargeback evidence. Build it into your workflow: deliver, then ask for confirmation in the same thread.
  • State your refund and revision terms up front. Chargebacks often hide behind "services not as described." If your contract defines exactly what the client gets and what revisions are included, that excuse collapses.

One more prevention move: for large projects, split the payment across milestones. A $3,000 chargeback hurts less as three $1,000 payments, and the client's earlier paid milestones are evidence that the work was accepted.

Know when to cut the channel, not the client

If a specific client has reversed a payment once, do not take their next card payment. That is not hostility, it is policy. Offer bank transfer, Wise, or an escrow arrangement instead. One reversal is a dispute; a second one on the same card is a pattern you allowed to repeat.

And if the chargeback was clearly fraudulent, a real refusal to pay for delivered work with no dispute about quality, say so in your representment and consider reporting it. Payment fraud is rare among real business clients, but when it happens, the paper trail you built for the dispute is the same trail you would hand to anyone else.
