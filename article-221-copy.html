The Client Who Asks You to Combine Two Projects on One Invoice: How to Keep Separate Debts From Becoming One Dispute

It sounds like a favour. You have finished two projects for the same client, and they ask you to put both on a single invoice to make their accounting easier. One document, one payment, less paperwork for everyone. It feels efficient, and refusing feels awkward, like you are making a simple thing complicated.

The problem is that two projects on one invoice are no longer two debts. They become one amount, and one amount is much easier to dispute, delay, or partially pay than two separate ones. If the client is unhappy with one project, they can hold the whole invoice. If they pay part of it, you cannot tell which project they paid for. If the matter ever reaches a formal chase, you have to explain why one document contains two different scopes, two different approval dates, and possibly two different payment terms.

A combined invoice is not forbidden. It is just a structure that needs care, and the care has to happen before you send it, not after the client starts picking at it.

1. Keep the projects separate inside the invoice

If you agree to combine, never merge the line items into a single lump sum. List each project as its own section, with its own description, its own amount, and its own approval reference. "Project A, brand refresh, approved 3 November, $2,400" and "Project B, landing page build, approved 12 November, $1,800" are two facts sitting on one page. A single line that reads "Design work, $4,200" is one fact that hides two.

This matters because disputes are almost always about one project, not both. A client who is unhappy with the landing page will point at the combined total and say the whole invoice is in question. Your answer, if the invoice is structured properly, is simple: "Project A is listed separately at $2,400 and was approved on 3 November. Project B is the disputed item, and we can discuss that on its own." Separation inside the document gives you the same protection as separate invoices, while still giving the client the single payment they asked for.

Add a subtotal per project, then a grand total. That small formatting choice is what turns a combined invoice from a merged debt back into two clear debts that happen to share a page.

2. Give the combined invoice one due date, and say so

Two projects often have two different payment terms, especially if they were agreed weeks apart. One might be Net 14, the other Net 30. When you combine them, you must pick one due date and state it plainly on the invoice, or the client will quietly apply the longer term to the whole amount.

The cleanest approach is to use the earlier date, and to tell the client you are doing it. "Combined as requested. Both projects are due 28 November, which is Net 14 from this invoice." If the client objects, negotiate that point openly, before the invoice is sent, rather than discovering after the fact that they paid on the later date and considered themselves on time.

Never leave two due dates on one invoice. A document with two deadlines reads as two invoices stapled together, and it invites the client to treat each part on its own schedule while paying neither on time. One invoice, one due date, stated in the same sentence as the total.

3. Handle partial payment by project, not by amount

A combined invoice almost guarantees a partial payment at some point. The client pays $2,400 of a $4,200 total and considers the matter half settled. Your job is to make sure everyone agrees which half that is.

When a partial payment arrives, confirm it in writing the same day, naming the project it covers. "Received $2,400 today, applied to Project A, brand refresh, in full. Project B, landing page build, $1,800, remains due 28 November." If the client intended the payment differently, they will correct you, and that correction is valuable, because it forces the allocation into the open.

Never let a partial payment sit unallocated. An unallocated payment on a combined invoice becomes a weapon in a later dispute: the client claims they paid for the disputed project, you claim they paid for the other one, and the invoice itself cannot settle the argument because it merged the two. A one-line allocation email, sent the day the money arrives, prevents that argument entirely.

4. Do not let one project's dispute freeze the other

This is the real risk of combining, and it is the one to name to the client before you agree. Say it plainly: "Happy to combine them, on the understanding that a question about one project does not hold up payment for the other." Most reasonable clients will accept this, because it is fair, and because they asked for the combination in the first place.

If a dispute does arise on one project, chase the undisputed project on its normal schedule, without apology and without waiting. "Project A is undisputed and was due 28 November. I am chasing that amount separately while we resolve Project B." The client may still try to link them, but your record now shows that you separated them from the start: separate sections, separate subtotals, and a written understanding that one dispute does not freeze the other.

If the client refuses that understanding, that refusal is your answer. Do not combine. Send two invoices, and let their accounting department handle the paperwork they were trying to avoid. Your payment security is worth more than their convenience.

5. Set the combination rule before the next pair of projects

For the next client with two projects, set the rule at the start, not at invoice time. "I am happy to combine invoices when it helps your accounting. When I do, each project stays listed separately with its own subtotal, the combined invoice carries one due date, and a dispute on one project never holds up the other."

Clients who ask for combined invoices are usually organised people trying to reduce their own admin, not people trying to trap you. Giving them a clear structure makes their job easier too: their finance team can see exactly what each amount is for, which is usually why they asked in the first place. The structure protects you without costing them anything.

Add one more line to your terms for repeat clients: combined invoices are available on request, but the default is separate invoices per project. That way, combining stays a favour you grant with conditions, not a habit that quietly merges every debt you are owed into one disputable lump.

The pattern underneath

A combined invoice is a convenience that turns two debts into one amount, and one amount is easier to dispute, delay, or pay in part than two clear ones. Keep each project separate inside the document with its own subtotal, give the combined invoice one due date stated plainly, allocate every partial payment to a named project the day it arrives, never let one project's dispute freeze the other, and set the combination rule before the next pair of projects. Used that way, a single invoice stays convenient for the client and safe for you, and your payment chase, if you ever need one, starts from two clear debts instead of one blurred total.
