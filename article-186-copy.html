The Client Who Sends Payment With No Reference: How to Match Mystery Payments Without Chasing Your Own Money

The payment lands in your account, and your first feeling is relief. Then you look closer: no invoice number, no client name you recognise, no project reference, just an amount that is close to an invoice but not quite exact, from an account name that could be a client, a client's company, or a client's accountant. Now you have a new problem, and it is an odd one: you have been paid, and you still cannot close the invoice, because you cannot prove which invoice the money belongs to. Mystery payments are a small administrative mess that quietly creates real risk, because an unmatched payment can be chased twice, credited to the wrong client, or disputed months later by a client who insists they paid a different invoice.

The fix is unglamorous, and that is why it works: a short matching routine you run the moment money arrives, plus one change to how you ask for payment, so the next payment arrives with its own label attached.

1. Do not guess, and do not spend it yet

The temptation with an unlabelled payment is to match it to the invoice that looks closest and move on. Resist it, especially when the amount is close but not exact. A payment that is slightly less than an invoice might be a partial payment, a payment with a bank fee deducted, or a payment for a different invoice entirely. A payment that is slightly more might include a tip, a reimbursement, or an overpayment on another job. Guessing wrong creates two errors at once: one client gets credited for money that was not theirs, and the real payer stays marked as overdue and gets chased for money they already sent, which is the fastest way to damage a good relationship.

Treat an unmatched payment as unallocated until you have evidence, not as a puzzle to solve by feel. Note the date, the exact amount, the sender name as shown by your bank, and the invoices that are open for similar amounts. Then ask, do not assume. A short message to the most likely sender, asking which invoice the payment relates to, resolves most mystery payments in a day, and it reads as careful bookkeeping, not as an accusation.

2. Ask the right question, once, in writing

When you ask, be specific about what you need and why, and keep the tone light. A message like this works: you received a payment of a certain amount on a certain date, the payment carried no invoice reference, and you want to allocate it correctly so their account shows as paid and they are not chased in error. Ask them to confirm the invoice number, and if the amount differs from the invoice, ask whether the difference is a fee deduction, a partial payment, or a payment covering more than one invoice.

Ask in writing, by email, even if you also mention it on a call, because the answer becomes part of your paper trail. If the client replies that the payment was for a different invoice than you expected, you have saved yourself a double chase. If the client says the payment was a mistake or was meant for someone else, you have caught an overpayment before you spent it, which is far cheaper than returning it later. One written question, asked promptly, prevents a whole chain of corrections.

3. Fix the cause: make the reference unavoidable

Most mystery payments happen because the invoice never asked for a reference in a way the payer could not miss. Fix that on every invoice from now on, in three places. First, put the invoice number in the payment instructions themselves, not just in the header: a line that says "Please include invoice number INV-0142 in your payment reference" is much harder to skip than a number sitting at the top of the page. Second, put the same instruction in the email that sends the invoice, because many clients pay from the email, not from the PDF. Third, if your payment link or bank details allow a reference field, pre-fill it or name it clearly, so the payer has to delete your reference to pay without one, rather than having to invent one.

For clients who pay through an accountant or a finance team, add one more line: if someone else makes the payment on your behalf, please ask them to include the invoice number. Finance teams pay dozens of suppliers a day, and they will use whatever reference you give them, but only if you give them one in the place they actually look.

4. Keep a simple unmatched-payments list

If you handle more than a handful of invoices a month, keep a short list of unmatched payments, separate from your invoice tracker, with four columns: date received, amount, sender as shown, and status. Review it once a week, on the same day you review overdue invoices. A payment that sits unmatched for more than a week gets a second, firmer message, and a payment that sits for a month gets treated as a real problem: either the sender is not your client at all, in which case you need to return it, or the client is unresponsive, in which case you need to decide whether to hold it as unallocated credit or to escalate.

The list matters because mystery payments are easy to forget once the relief of being paid fades, and a forgotten unmatched payment has a habit of resurfacing at the worst moment, usually when a client you thought was paid asks why you are still chasing them, or when your year-end accounts do not reconcile. A weekly five-minute review keeps a small mess small.

5. Handle the awkward cases by rule, not by mood

Three awkward cases come up often enough to deserve a rule each. First, a payment with no reference from someone who is not your client: do not spend it, and contact your bank about returning it, because keeping money that is not yours creates a liability even if the sender never notices. Second, a payment that covers two invoices at once: allocate it in writing to the client, showing the split, and ask for confirmation, so both invoices close cleanly. Third, a payment that arrives after you already sent a final notice or started an escalation: close the escalation in writing the same day, confirm the invoice as paid, and apologise for any overlap, because a client who paid and still got chased will remember the chase longer than the delay.

Rules for awkward cases protect you from the most common mistake in all of this, which is handling money by mood: being generous with a client you like and strict with one you do not. Money handling should be the most boring, most consistent part of your business, because consistency is what makes it defensible.

The pattern underneath

A payment with no reference is not a windfall, it is an unallocated liability, and the way to handle it is to refuse to guess, ask the sender in writing which invoice it covers, make the reference unavoidable on every future invoice, review unmatched payments weekly, and handle the awkward cases by rule rather than by mood. Getting paid is only half the job. Knowing, provably, which invoice was paid is the other half, and it is the half that keeps a good client relationship intact after the money has moved.
