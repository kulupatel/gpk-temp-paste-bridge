Chasing Through a Chain: Getting Paid When You Are a Subcontractor

As a subcontractor, your late payment problem has an extra layer: the client you invoiced is not the client with the money. You invoice the agency. The agency waits on the end client. The end client is slow. You starve, and everyone in the chain shrugs.

The standard response, "we will pay you when the end client pays us," feels like a weather event. It is not. It is a term you can negotiate, document, and beat.

"Pay when paid" is the trap to name

A pay-when-paid clause says the middleman only pays you when they get paid. Read literally, it can mean you never get paid: if the end client defaults, your invoice dies too. Some versions soften this to pay-IF-paid, which is even worse.

Before you sign any subcontract, find this clause. If it is there, do not just accept it. Push for one of two fixes: a time cap ("payable within 30 days of my invoice regardless of end-client payment") or a named deadline ("payable by the 15th of the following month"). A time cap converts an infinite wait into a dated obligation. The agency will resist; most accept the cap because every other vendor asks for it too.

The pressure only works in one direction

Here is the practical truth about subcontract chains: your pressure only travels one way, toward the person who pays you. You cannot chase the end client directly unless your contract allows it, and demanding payment from someone you have no contract with usually just annoys them and embarrasses your agency contact.

So aim every follow-up at the agency contact, and make each message give them ammunition to chase their own client:

  • Send the invoice with the exact PO or job reference the agency uses internally, so it slots into their system without translation.
  • Ask for the name of the person at the agency who owns the end-client follow-up, and ask to be told the date they expect their client to pay. A named date is a commitment; "soon" is not.
  • When you escalate, escalate against the time cap in your contract, not against the end client. "My invoice is now 45 days past your payment terms" is enforceable language. "Your client has not paid you" is not your sentence to write.

The one line that changes the conversation

When the agency says they are still waiting on the end client, answer with the cap:

"I understand the end client is slow. My terms with you are 30 days from invoice, and we are at day 41. I need payment released by Friday so we are back on schedule."

Short, contractual, and it gives them a reason to put your invoice at the top of their own chase list. Agencies pay the vendor who is loudest, most documented, and contractually armed first.

Prevention: the subcontractor starter pack

  • Kill pay-when-paid or cap it at a date, in writing, before the work starts.
  • Invoice the moment your milestone completes, never in a batch at month end. Aging starts at invoice date.
  • Keep a parallel relationship with the end client if the agency permits it (project check-ins, shared Slack). If the agency ever collapses or stalls, that relationship is your bridge.
  • On first engagements, demand a deposit or milestone billing even harder than usual: you have two links of trust to price, not one.
