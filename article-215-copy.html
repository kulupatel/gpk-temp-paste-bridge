The Client Who Routes Every Invoice Through an Accountant You Cannot Reach: How to Get Past the Gatekeeper

The work is approved, the client is happy, and the invoice goes nowhere. "I have sent it to my accountant," the client says. Then silence. You ask for an update and hear the same line again, with a new date attached. You cannot call the accountant, because you have no name, no email, and no permission. You cannot chase the client, because the client insists the payment is out of their hands. The invoice sits in a gap between two people, and neither of them treats it as urgent.

This is the gatekeeper stall. The client is not necessarily avoiding payment. They have handed the decision to someone whose job is to slow money down, and they have handed you no way to reach that person. Your job is not to force your way past the gatekeeper. It is to make the gatekeeper's job easy, to put a name and a date on the process, and to keep the client responsible for the outcome even though someone else holds the pen.

1. Get the gatekeeper's name before the invoice exists

The time to learn who pays you is before the project starts, not after the invoice is overdue. At the quote stage, ask one plain question: "Who should invoices go to, and who approves them?" Most clients will give you a name and an email without thinking twice. If the answer is "just send it to me and I will pass it on," that is your warning. The pass-it-on step is where invoices die, quietly and without a paper trail.

Write the answer into the quote: invoices sent to the named contact, payment due on the stated terms, with a copy to the client. A copy is not pushy. It is how every supplier works, and it gives you a second route when the first goes quiet. If the client refuses to name anyone, agree a fallback in writing: if the invoice is not acknowledged within five working days, the client confirms receipt personally. That one line keeps the gatekeeper from becoming a black hole.

2. Make the invoice accountant-proof on arrival

An accountant's first job with an unfamiliar invoice is to find a reason to set it aside. Missing purchase order number, wrong legal name, no VAT or tax reference, vague description, no bank details in the expected place: any one of these sends your invoice to a query pile, and a query pile has no deadline. So send the invoice complete, the first time, in the format the accountant expects.

Ask the client what their accountant needs: purchase order number, cost code, legal entity name, tax details, payment method. Then put all of it on the invoice and in the covering email, in plain text, not only inside a PDF attachment. An accountant who can copy the bank details from the email and match the purchase order in one glance has no reason to delay. An accountant who has to email you for a missing reference has a reason to wait a week, and that week becomes your cash flow problem.

3. Ask for acknowledgement, not payment, in the first chase

Chasing payment from a client who has passed the invoice on feels like blaming the wrong person, and clients react badly to it. So chase acknowledgement instead. Five working days after sending the invoice, write: "Just checking this reached your accountant safely. Could you confirm they have it, and let me know their name so I can answer any questions directly?" This message is friendly, it asks for something small, and it moves the invoice from an unknown state to a known one.

If the client confirms receipt, you now have a date: the invoice entered the system on that day, and the payment terms run from the invoice date, not from the day the accountant gets around to it. If the client cannot confirm receipt, you have learned something important: the invoice was never sent, or was sent to the wrong place. Either way, you now resend it to the right address, with the client copied, and the clock starts from a real date, not from a claim.

4. Put a deadline on the gatekeeper step itself

Payment terms alone do not fix a gatekeeper stall, because the client treats the terms as the accountant's problem. So put a deadline on the handoff, not just on the payment. In your terms, or in the covering email for a first project, write: "If payment is not received by the due date, please let me know who is handling this invoice so I can help clear any questions." Then, when the due date passes, your chase has a specific ask: a name, not money.

Once you have the name, write to the accountant directly, with the client copied, and keep it short: invoice number, amount, due date, bank details, and one question: "Is there anything missing that is holding this up?" Accountants answer specific questions. They ignore vague payment pleas. And once the accountant replies, even with a query, you have broken the silence: the invoice is now a live item in someone's inbox, not a rumour in someone else's.

5. Keep the client as the owner, whatever the accountant does

The most important line in this whole pattern is the simplest: the client owes you, not their accountant. The accountant is their agent, not yours, and a delay inside their company is still their delay. Say so politely and plainly when the due date passes: "I understand this is with your accountant. The invoice is now past its due date, so could you confirm when payment will be released, or put me in touch with them directly so I can clear any questions?"

That message does two things. It refuses the framing that the payment is out of the client's hands, and it gives the client a choice: confirm a date, or introduce you. Clients who genuinely want to pay will pick one. Clients who were hiding behind the accountant will feel the cover thinning, and that is the point. You are not being difficult. You are refusing to let a third party you never met become the reason you do not get paid.

The pattern underneath

An unreachable accountant turns a simple invoice into a mystery, and mysteries do not get paid. Get the payer's name before the project starts, send an invoice that needs no follow-up questions, chase acknowledgement before you chase money, put a deadline on the handoff itself, and keep the client as the owner of the debt no matter who processes it. The gatekeeper stops being a wall when you treat them as a step with a name, a date, and a specific question, and the client stops hiding behind them when the only choices left are a date or an introduction.
