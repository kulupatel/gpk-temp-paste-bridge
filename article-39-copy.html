The Invoice Reissue Game: When a Client Keeps Asking You to Resend, Backdate, or Change Your Invoice

Most invoice reissue requests are innocent. Someone joined the project late and wants a clean copy. The entity name was wrong. The PO number arrived after you sent the invoice. You fix it, resend, and the invoice gets paid.

But there is a second kind of reissue request. The kind that arrives on day 29, asks for a small change, and resets the payment clock back to zero. Learn to tell them apart, because one costs you an afternoon and the other costs you a month of cash flow.

The legitimate reissues: say yes fast

Fix and resend these within one business day, and your payment date does not move. Three common ones:

Wrong entity or address. The client was acquired, or the contract names the parent company. Correct it, note "reissued for corrected entity name" on the invoice, keep the original invoice number and date.

Wrong line items or quantities. You billed 40 hours and it was 42, or a milestone line is missing. Fix it, keep the same invoice number and date, note "reissued with corrected line items."

Missing PO number or cost center. Their procurement process needs a reference. Add it, keep the original date, send it back the same day.

The pattern in all three: the change is factual, the request is specific, and the client supplies the correction. When those three hold, reissue and move on.

The delay-tactic reissues: recognize the pattern

The tactical reissue looks similar but behaves differently. Watch for these five moves:

1. The reset. A change request arrives just before the due date, and the client treats the reissue date as the new invoice date. "We received your corrected invoice on the 28th, so payment will go out 30 days from then." The correction took two days; the delay costs you a full cycle. Prevention: every invoice carries the original issue date and a version number (INV-1042 v2). The due date never moves on a reissue.

2. The serial resend. The same invoice is requested three times by three different people, each claiming they never received it. Each resend buys another week of "we just got it." Fix: send invoices to one named billing contact with read receipts or delivery tracking, and keep a resend log. When the third request arrives, reply: "Sending again now, this is the third copy. The original went out on [date] to [contact], and payment remains due [date]."

3. The backdate ask. "Can you date this last month? It helps our books." This is a request to falsify a financial document. It can implicate you in their tax or audit games. The answer is always no, stated once, politely, in writing.

4. The round-down or re-format loop. "Can you round to the nearest hundred? Our system only takes whole numbers. Actually, can you split it into two invoices? Actually, can you merge them?" Each round trip is another five business days. Small legitimate requests are fine; four in a row is a tactic. After the second round, say: "Happy to make this one final adjustment. To keep payment on schedule, please confirm the payment date for this invoice."

5. The contact shuffle. "Send it to our new AP lead." Two weeks later: "That person left, send it to me instead." The invoice keeps moving but never lands. Counter: confirm the correct billing contact in writing at project kickoff (before you invoice), and cc the original contact when you redirect.

The fraud line you never cross

A reissue request crosses from delay tactic into fraud when it asks you to create a false record. Never:

  • Backdate or forward-date an invoice at a client's request.
  • Change an invoiced amount so it reads differently on their books.
  • Issue two invoices for the same work so the client can claim a double expense.
  • Remove or add line items that misrepresent what was delivered.

If a client pressures you on any of these, put your refusal in writing and keep a copy. A client who asks you to falsify an invoice will ask you to eat the invoice later. That relationship is already over; you just do not know the date yet.

Three one-line refusals that keep it professional

For the backdate ask: "I cannot alter invoice dates; my books have to match my records."

For the reset trick: "Reissues keep the original invoice date and due date. The corrected copy is attached; payment is still due [date]."

For the fifth re-format: "I have made two adjustments already. Let us lock this version and confirm the payment date."

Each is one sentence, no accusation, no lecture. The tone is warm and boring, which is exactly how payment paperwork should feel.

Prevention: make every invoice reissue-proof

Four habits that end the game before it starts:

Version numbers. INV-1042, INV-1042 v2, INV-1042 v3. Versions prove the invoice existed before the reissue.

Original dates frozen. State the payment terms on every version: "Due [date], 30 days from original issue [date]."

One billing contact, confirmed in writing. Get the name, email, and AP process at kickoff. Redirects go back to that person.

Resend log. Date, who asked, what changed. Three rows deep and the pattern is visible to everyone, including the client when you quote it.

When a client asks you to resend an invoice, assume goodwill the first time, assume a tactic the third, and never assume fraud is harmless. The invoice is the paper trail your whole escalation ladder stands on. Keep it clean, keep it dated, and keep the due date exactly where you put it.
