The Client Who Asks You to Extend the Payment Terms After Signing: How to Hold the Terms You Agreed

You signed the contract on Net 14. The work is underway, the first invoice is nearly due, and then the message arrives: "Can we move to Net 60? Our finance team needs more time." It sounds small. It sounds reasonable. It sounds like the kind of thing a good partner agrees to without a fuss. But a payment term is not a courtesy. It is part of the price, and extending it after signing is the same as cutting your fee, except the cut is hidden in time instead of money.

Sixty days instead of fourteen is forty-six days of your money sitting in someone else's bank account. On a $3,000 invoice, that is forty-six days where you have paid your own costs, your own rent, your own subscriptions, while the client holds the cash your work earned. Multiply that across a year of invoices and the cost is not small at all. It is the difference between a business that pays its bills on time and one that borrows from itself to cover a client's cash flow. So treat the request as what it is: a renegotiation of the deal, not a favour, and answer it the way you would answer any renegotiation.

1. Remember what the terms were priced on

Payment terms were part of the price you quoted, even if neither of you said so out loud. A freelancer who quotes $3,000 on Net 14 is quoting a different price from a freelancer who quotes $3,000 on Net 60, because the second one waits six extra weeks to be paid and carries the cost of that wait. You chose your rate, your schedule, and your other clients around the terms you agreed. Changing the terms after signing changes the deal you priced, and the client asking for the change should understand that, even if they have never thought about it that way.

So before you answer, work out what the extension actually costs you. How many days longer will you wait? What does that do to your cash flow in the month the invoice falls due? Would you have quoted a higher price, or asked for a larger deposit, if you had known the terms were Net 60 from the start? Most freelancers find the answer is yes to at least one of those, and that finding is the whole argument. You are not being difficult. You are holding the terms you priced the work on, and any change to those terms is a change to the price.

Say that plainly when you reply. "I priced this project on Net 14, which is what we signed. Moving to Net 60 changes the deal we agreed, so I need to hold the signed terms for this project." That sentence is not a negotiation. It is a statement of fact, and it puts the request back where it belongs: as a change the client is asking for, not a favour you owe them.

2. Never agree verbally, and never agree for free

If you do decide to consider an extension, the two rules are absolute: nothing is agreed in a chat message or a phone call, and nothing is given away for nothing. A verbal "sure, Net 60 is fine" is the worst possible outcome, because the client will remember the agreement and forget the context, and you will have no record of what you gave up or what you got in return. If the terms are going to change, they change in writing, as an amendment to the signed agreement, with the new terms, the invoices they apply to, and the date they take effect all stated clearly.

And never agree for free. An extension is worth something to the client, usually a great deal, because it improves their cash flow at no cost to them. Your price for that improvement should be something real: a larger deposit on the next milestone, payment in two parts instead of one, an early-payment discount removed, or a rate increase on future work that reflects the longer wait. The exact trade matters less than the principle. A term that costs the client nothing will be asked for again, by this client and by the next one who hears about it. A term that costs something real gets asked for only when it is genuinely needed.

If the client will not trade anything for the extension, that tells you everything about how much they value it versus how much it costs you. A client who needs sixty days because their finance cycle genuinely runs that way can usually offer something in return: a deposit, a partial payment on delivery, a signed commitment to a fixed payment date. A client who wants sixty days simply because it is nicer for them will offer nothing, and that is your answer. Hold the signed terms.

3. Trade time for something real

When an extension is genuinely needed, the trade should match the cost. A client moving from Net 14 to Net 60 is asking for forty-six extra days, and the things worth trading for that time are the things that reduce your risk: money sooner, money in parts, or money guaranteed.

The strongest trade is a larger deposit or a milestone payment on delivery. "I can move this invoice to Net 60 if the next milestone is paid 50 percent on delivery." That keeps your cash flow moving even while the headline terms get longer. The next best is splitting the invoice: half on the original date, half on the extended date, so the wait applies to only part of the money. A third option is a fixed payment date in writing, not a vague promise: "Net 60, paid on the 15th of the month following delivery, confirmed by your finance team in writing." A fixed date you can plan around is worth more than a shorter term you cannot rely on.

What you should not trade for is a promise of future work. "We will have plenty more work for you next quarter" is not payment, and it is not security. It is a sentence, and sentences do not pay rent. If the client offers future work as the trade, thank them, and hold the signed terms. Future work is welcome when it arrives, as a new project with its own terms. It is not a substitute for being paid on time for the work you have already done.

4. Put any change in a written amendment, or it did not happen

If you agree an extension, even a temporary one, write it down as an amendment to the contract and get it signed, or at least confirmed in writing by the person who can authorise payment. The amendment needs four lines: which invoices it applies to, the new due date for each, what you received in return, and the date the change takes effect. Without those four lines, you have a conversation, not an agreement, and conversations do not survive a change of finance staff.

The amendment should also state what it does not cover. An extension for one invoice is not an extension for every future invoice. An extension granted because this quarter is difficult is not a permanent change to your terms. Write that down too: "This amendment applies to invoice 47 only. All other invoices remain on the signed Net 14 terms." Clients who genuinely need a one-off extension will accept that wording without argument, because it matches what they asked for. Clients who were hoping the extension would quietly become permanent will push back, and that pushback tells you what the request really was.

Keep the amendment with the contract, and chase against the amended date, not the original one. The worst outcome is agreeing an extension, forgetting you agreed it, and chasing on the old date, because then you look disorganised and the client feels harassed for a deadline they thought had moved. One document, one date, one chase. That is the whole system.

5. Grandfather the old terms for work already delivered

Whatever you decide about future work, the terms on work already delivered do not move. An invoice already issued on Net 14 stays on Net 14. The client cannot extend terms retroactively, because the terms were part of the agreement under which the work was done, and the work is already in their hands. If a client asks to extend terms on an invoice that is already out, the answer is simple: "That invoice was issued on the signed Net 14 terms, and those terms stand. We can discuss different terms for future work."

This matters most when the request arrives close to a due date. A client who asks on day twelve of a fourteen-day term to move to sixty days is not asking for a scheduling change. They are asking to avoid paying a bill that is about to fall due. The closer the request comes to the due date, the firmer your answer should be, because the cost to you of agreeing is immediate and the benefit to the client is that they do not have to pay this week. Hold the date. Offer to discuss future terms separately, in writing, with a trade attached. Do not let a term agreed after the work was done rewrite the terms the work was done under.

The pattern underneath

Payment terms are part of the price, and extending them after signing is a renegotiation, not a favour. Hold the terms you priced the work on, never agree verbally or for free, trade time only for something real, put any change in a written amendment that names its limits, and never let a new term reach back to an invoice already issued. The client can ask. You can listen. But the signed terms stand until a written amendment with a real trade replaces them, and that is what keeps a fourteen-day deal from quietly becoming a sixty-day loan.
