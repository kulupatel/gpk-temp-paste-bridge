The Client Who Asks You to Start Before the Contract Is Signed: How to Hold the Start Line Without Losing the Job

The client is enthusiastic, the brief is clear, and the work is urgent. There is just one problem: the contract is not signed yet. "Can you just start, and we will get the paperwork sorted this week?" It sounds reasonable, especially when the client is friendly and the deadline is real. Most freelancers say yes, because saying no feels like losing the job before it begins.

Saying yes is how freelancers end up doing unpaid work for clients who were never actually committed. An unsigned project has no agreed price, no agreed scope, no payment term, and no late fee. If the client changes their mind, disputes the price, or simply goes quiet, you have no paper to point to, only a chat history and hours you cannot bill. The answer is not to refuse every early start. It is to make the start conditional on one small piece of paper, and to hold that line the same way every time, so no single client can talk you past it.

1. Separate enthusiasm from commitment

A client saying "let us go" is not a commitment. A commitment is a signature, a deposit, or a written acceptance of price, scope, and terms. Everything before that is interest, and interest evaporates: budgets get frozen, managers change their minds, a cheaper option appears. Work done on interest alone is a gift you did not mean to give.

Name this to yourself before you reply, because the pressure is designed to blur it. The client is not asking you to trust them. They are asking you to carry their risk: if the project dies, you lose the hours; if it survives, they pay the same price they would have paid anyway. There is no upside for you in starting early, only downside. Once you see the asymmetry, holding the line stops feeling rude and starts feeling obvious.

When the client pushes, answer the enthusiasm and hold the line in the same message: "I am excited to start, and I start the moment the agreement is signed. I can have it to you today, and once it is back, your slot is booked." You have said yes to the project and no to the risk, and a client who genuinely wants the work will sign within a day.

2. Offer a paid first step, not a free head start

Sometimes the client truly cannot sign this week: procurement is slow, legal is reviewing, the budget holder is travelling. That is a real constraint, and it still does not justify free work. What it justifies is a smaller, paid first step that both sides can agree in writing quickly.

Offer a paid discovery or kickoff phase: a fixed fee for the first deliverable, a brief, a plan, or a first draft, agreed in a short email rather than the full contract. "I can start the research phase this week on a short written agreement for [amount], due on delivery, and we fold it into the full project once the main contract is signed." The client gets momentum, you get paid for the hours, and the project has a paper trail from day one.

What you must not do is rebrand free work as a "goodwill gesture" or a "quick look." A quick look becomes the first draft, the first draft becomes the revision round, and by the time the contract appears, half the project is done and your negotiating position is gone. If the work is worth doing, it is worth a written price, even a small one.

3. Put the start condition in writing, once, before the work

If you agree to any early start, even a paid one, write the condition down before the first hour: what is included, what it costs, when it is due, and what happens if the full contract never arrives. One email is enough, but it must exist, and the client must reply to it.

A workable version: "Confirming our start: I will deliver [first deliverable] by [date] for [amount], due [date]. If the full project agreement is not signed by [date], this phase ends and the balance of the project is not started. Please reply confirming and I will begin." That message does three jobs at once: it prices the work, it dates the decision, and it gives the client a deadline for their own paperwork, which is usually what actually gets the contract signed.

Never start on a verbal "we will sort it." Verbal agreements about start conditions are exactly what gets forgotten when the project gets busy. If the client will not reply to one short email, they were never going to sign the contract either, and you have learned that before spending your week.

4. Hold the same line when the client is big, friendly, or urgent

The line breaks most often for three kinds of client: the big name ("this could lead to lots of work"), the friend ("you know I am good for it"), and the urgent one ("we need this tomorrow"). Each one has a story for why the paperwork can wait, and each story is a version of the same request: carry my risk for free.

Big names are the most dangerous, because the promised future work almost never arrives on the promised terms, and the unpaid early hours become the precedent for every later negotiation. Friends are covered by the same rule, in warmer language: "I do this for everyone, including friends, so it never gets awkward." Urgency is the easiest to answer, because urgency is a reason to sign faster, not to skip signing: "If it is urgent, I can send the agreement now and start the hour it comes back."

The test is simple: would you hold this line for a stranger? If yes, hold it here too. Exceptions do not stay exceptions. The first time you start unsigned for a good reason, the second client hears about it, or you hear yourself making the same exception again, and soon the line is gone and you are back to chasing payment for work that was never properly agreed.

5. If you already started unsigned, stop and paper it now

Many freelancers reading this have already started a project with no signature, and stopping feels like admitting a mistake. It is not. It is fixing one, and the sooner you fix it, the cheaper the fix.

Send the message today: "I want to make sure we are both protected on this project, so I am pausing new work until we have the agreement signed. Here it is, with the work done so far listed at [amount] and the remaining scope priced as agreed. Once it is back, I continue straight away." You are not threatening the client. You are giving them a clean way to confirm a project they already want, and you are putting a price on the work already done before it grows any larger.

If the client signs, you have lost nothing but a day. If the client stalls, argues, or disappears, you have learned the truth about this project at the cost of the hours already spent, not at the cost of the whole engagement. Either way, you have stopped the bleed, and you will never have to chase payment for work that was never agreed in the first place.

The pattern underneath

Starting before the contract is signed feels like helpfulness, but it is really risk transfer: the client keeps the option to walk away, and you keep the hours. The fix is a single rule, held every time: no signature, no start, with a paid first step as the only bridge when paperwork genuinely cannot move fast enough. Enthusiasm is not commitment, a short written agreement beats a long verbal promise, and the clients who respect the line are the ones worth building a business around. Hold the start line, and the projects that begin will be the ones that actually pay.
