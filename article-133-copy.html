The Invoice Sent to the Wrong Person: Getting Your Bill to the One Who Can Pay It

You did the work, sent the invoice, and waited. Two weeks later the client replies, "I never saw this," or worse, nothing at all. Then you discover the truth: your invoice went to the person who hired you, not the person who pays. The hiring manager loved the work and never forwarded the bill. Accounts payable never knew it existed. Your invoice has been sitting in the wrong inbox the whole time, and no amount of chasing the wrong person will move it.

Getting paid starts with routing. Send the invoice to the person who can actually release the money, and half the late-payment problem disappears before it starts.

1. Ask for the payer at kickoff, not at invoice time

The moment a project is agreed, ask one question: "Who should the invoice go to, and what do they need on it?" Get a name, an email, and any requirements: a purchase order number, a vendor portal, a cost code. Clients expect this question; it signals professionalism, not pushiness. The freelancers who chase for months are usually the ones who sent the invoice to their day-to-day contact and assumed it would find its way. It rarely does.

2. Send to two people, not one

Even with the right payer named, send the invoice to both the payer and your day-to-day contact. The payer releases the money; the contact confirms the work is approved. When both see the same invoice, neither can say they never got it, and the contact becomes your internal ally instead of your bottleneck. One email, two recipients, and the "I never saw it" excuse is gone before it can be used.

3. Confirm receipt within 48 hours

An invoice that nobody confirms is an invoice nobody owns. Two days after sending, send one short message to both recipients: "Confirming you received invoice 104 for $2,400, due November 14. Please let me know if anything is missing." This is not chasing; it is receipt confirmation, and it is expected in business. If the payer replies that the purchase order number is missing or the vendor form was never filed, you learn it on day two, not day thirty.

4. Fix the routing the first time it fails

When an invoice stalls because it went to the wrong person, do not just resend it to the same address and hope. Ask the contact directly: "Who approves payment on your side, and can you introduce me?" Then send the corrected invoice to the payer, copy the contact, and note the change: "Resending to accounts payable per your note; same invoice, same terms." One corrected routing fixes every future invoice with that client, because the payer is now in your address book.

5. Put the payer on the contract

For any client above a threshold you set yourself, write the payment contact into the agreement: the payer's name and email, the payment terms, and the consequence for late payment. It feels formal for a freelance engagement, and that is the point. When a payment stalls, you are not asking a favor; you are pointing at a term the client already agreed to, addressed to a person they already named.

The pattern underneath

Most "late" invoices are not late at all. They are lost in someone else's inbox, waiting for a person who never knew they existed. Routing is unglamorous work, but it is the cheapest payment insurance there is: one question at kickoff, two recipients on every invoice, one confirmation message at 48 hours. Do that, and the client who "never saw it" stops appearing in your inbox, because the invoice finally reaches the one person who can pay it.
