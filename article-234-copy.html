The Client Who Wants to Pay You With a Gift Card: How to Turn Store Credit Into Cash

The invoice is $800, and the client has an idea. "I have a $800 gift card for a big online store. Same value, no fees, no waiting for the bank. Can I just send you that?" It sounds equivalent. It sounds convenient. It sounds like the client is solving a problem for both of you. But a gift card is not $800. A gift card is $800 that can only be spent in one shop, that loses value the moment you try to convert it, that expires, that can be cancelled, and that your landlord will not accept as rent. The client is offering you store credit and asking you to call it payment, and the gap between those two things is the whole story.

This article is about how to answer that offer without sounding ungrateful, how to value a gift card honestly if you ever do accept one, and how to make sure the next invoice is paid in money, which is the only thing that pays your bills.

1. A gift card is worth less than its face value, always

Start with the honest math, because the client is offering you $800 and you need to know what you are actually being offered. A gift card with a face value of $800 is worth $800 only if you were already going to spend exactly $800 at that exact store, on things you actually need, before the card expires. For almost everyone, that is not true. You will spend it on things you half need, or you will sell it, and selling a gift card typically returns 70 to 85 percent of its face value, which means your $800 invoice just became a $560 to $680 payment, minus the time you spent converting it.

Then there are the risks the client never mentions. A gift card can expire. A gift card can be reported lost or stolen and cancelled after you have accepted it. A gift card bought with a stolen card can be voided by the store. A gift card for a store that goes out of business becomes worthless overnight. None of those risks exist with a bank transfer, and all of them land on you the moment you accept the card as payment. The client has transferred the risk to you along with the card, and the price of that risk is yours to carry.

So when the client says "same value," the answer is that it is not the same value, and you can say so plainly. "A gift card is worth its face value only at that one store, and I need money I can use for rent, tax, and bills. I need the $800 paid to the account on the invoice." That is not a negotiation. It is a statement of what money is, and a gift card is not money.

2. If you ever accept one, price it like a discount you chose

There are rare cases where accepting a gift card makes sense: the store is one you genuinely shop at every week, the amount is small, and the client is someone you trust. If you do accept one, treat it as what it is, a payment at a discount, and price it that way, in writing, before you accept it.

The rule is simple: a gift card settles an invoice only at its resale value, not its face value. If the card is worth $800 at the store and $640 if you sell it, then it settles $640 of the invoice, and $160 remains outstanding. Write that down before you accept the card: "I will accept the $800 store card as $640 toward invoice 52, with $160 still due by the original date." A client who genuinely wants to pay you will accept that math, because it is honest. A client who wanted the card to count as full payment will push back, and that pushback tells you the card was never about convenience. It was about paying less.

Never accept a gift card for the full invoice amount, and never accept one for a large invoice. A small card for a small invoice, at resale value, with the balance in money, is a favour you can afford. A large card for a large invoice is a pay cut dressed up as a payment method, and it should be refused the same way any other pay cut is refused: politely, firmly, and in writing.

3. Offer the client a way to pay that costs them nothing extra

Clients usually offer gift cards because they think they are being helpful, or because the card is sitting in a drawer and they would rather give it to you than let it expire. The fastest way to end the conversation is to give them a payment method that is genuinely free for both of you, so the card stops looking like a solution.

A bank transfer is the obvious answer. It costs the client nothing extra, it arrives in full, and it can be used for anything. "A bank transfer to the account on the invoice costs nothing, arrives in one working day, and settles the invoice in full. The gift card would leave me short, so please use the transfer." That reply does two things. It shows the client you are not asking for anything extra, and it removes the only reason the card was offered, which was convenience. Most clients will switch to the transfer without argument, because the card was never a principle. It was a drawer full of store credit looking for a home.

If the client genuinely has no money and only the card, that is a different conversation, and it is not about payment methods at all. It is about a client who cannot pay, and the answer there is a written payment plan with dates, or a pause on the work, not a gift card. A card from a client who cannot pay is still not a payment. It is a present from someone who owes you money, and the rules for that are in the article about gifts instead of payment.

4. Check the card before you count it, every time

If you do accept a gift card, even a small one at resale value, verify it before you mark anything paid. Check the balance with the store, in writing or through the store's own balance checker, and confirm the expiry date and any restrictions on what it can buy. Do this before you tell the client the card is accepted, because a card with a lower balance than claimed, or one that expires next week, or one that can only be used in a country you do not live in, is worth less than the client said it was worth, and you need to know that before you agree a value for it.

Keep the card details and the balance check with the invoice record, alongside the written agreement of what the card settles. "Store card received 12 November, balance confirmed $800, accepted as $640 toward invoice 52, $160 still due 20 November." That record protects you if the card turns out to be void, and it protects the client too, because it proves what was agreed. A gift card accepted without a record is a handshake deal, and handshake deals are exactly how $800 invoices turn into $0 payments with a story about a card nobody can find.

And never let a gift card change the due date. A card accepted today settles its agreed value today. The balance is still due on the original date, and the follow-up continues on the original schedule. A card that pushes the due date back is not a payment. It is a delay with a bow on it.

5. Put the payment methods in your terms before the next invoice

The lasting fix is a line in your terms that names the ways you accept payment, so a gift card is never offered as a serious option again. The line is simple: "Payment by bank transfer. Other methods by agreement in writing before the invoice is issued." Three sentences, and the entire conversation disappears, because the client agreed to the terms before the work started.

Add that line to your quote template, your contract, and the footer of every invoice, so it is visible at every stage. When a new client asks how to pay, send them the bank details and the methods line together, in the same message, so the choice is made before the first invoice exists. And when a client offers a gift card, a voucher, store credit, or any other non-money payment, you can point to the line instead of arguing about the value: "My terms are bank transfer, so I need the invoice paid that way." A rule that lives in your terms is a policy. A rule that lives only in your head is an argument waiting to happen.

The same line protects you from the next variation too, because gift cards are only one member of a family: vouchers, store credit, loyalty points, crypto from an exchange you do not use, and barter dressed up as payment. All of them are worth less than their face value, all of them carry risks a bank transfer does not, and all of them are answered the same way: bank transfer, as per the terms, in full, by the due date.

The pattern underneath

A gift card is store credit, not money, and it is worth less than its face value the moment you try to use it for anything else. Refuse it as full payment, price it at resale value if you ever accept one, offer a bank transfer that costs the client nothing extra, verify any card you do accept before you count it, and put the accepted payment methods in your terms so the offer never comes up again. The client can keep the card for their own shopping. You need the $800 in your account.
