The Invoice That Gets Lost in a New System: How to Get Paid When a Client Changes Software

Your invoice was approved, the payment was on its way, and then the client changed accounting software, or merged with another company, or moved to a new finance team. Suddenly nobody can find your invoice, nobody owns it, and every reply is some version of "we are still setting things up." This is not a client refusing to pay. It is a client whose payment machinery broke, and your invoice fell through the gap. Getting paid here is about rebuilding the paper trail faster than the new system can lose it.

1. Assume the old approval is gone and rebuild it

The first instinct is to forward the old approval email and wait. That rarely works, because the people who approved it may no longer be there, and the new team has no reason to trust a forwarded thread they did not create. Treat the old approval as useful evidence, not as a live approval. Send a fresh, complete package to the new contact: the original invoice, the delivery confirmation, and a one-line summary of what was delivered and when. Make it possible for a stranger to approve your payment in one sitting, without hunting through an old system they cannot access. The faster a new approver can say yes without asking anyone else, the faster you get paid.

2. Find the one person who owns the transition

In a system change there is always one person who owns the migration: a finance lead, an operations manager, or the person who signed off on the switch. That person is not necessarily your old contact, and they are not necessarily the person replying to your emails. Ask directly, in one sentence: "Who owns invoice migration for your new system, so I can resubmit this in the format they need?" This question works because it does not blame anyone and it gives the new team a clean task: point you at the owner. Once you have that name, send the package to them and copy your old contact. From then on, chase the owner, not the general inbox.

3. Resubmit in the new format, not the old one

Clients changing systems often change what an invoice must contain: a new purchase order number, a vendor registration form, a different portal, or a cost code that did not exist before. Arguing that your old invoice was fine wastes the one thing you need, which is a place in the new queue. Ask what the new system requires and resubmit exactly in that format, with the original invoice date and the original due date clearly shown, plus a note that this is a resubmission of an approved invoice, not a new charge. Resubmitting feels like starting over. It is actually the fastest way forward, because a correctly formatted invoice in the new system gets paid, while a perfectly formatted invoice in the old system gets ignored.

4. Set a date that survives the transition

"Once the system is live" is not a payment date. It is a reason to wait forever, because system migrations always run late. Your follow-up should convert the vague transition into a dated commitment: "Please confirm this resubmission is in the first payment run after go-live, and the date of that run." If the new system has a payment run schedule, get your invoice into the next one by name. If the client cannot give you a date, ask for a manual payment outside the system for this one invoice, on the grounds that it was approved before the migration. Manual payments are how finance teams clear exactly this kind of stuck invoice, and asking for one is reasonable, not pushy.

5. Protect the next invoice before the next change

You cannot stop clients changing systems, but you can make your invoices harder to lose. From now on, send every invoice with three things attached: the delivery confirmation, the name of the approver, and a purchase order or reference number if one exists. Keep your own copy of every approval, outside the client's system, with dates. And when a client tells you a change is coming, resubmit your outstanding invoices before the switch, while the old team can still approve them. An invoice that is paid before a migration never has to survive one.

The pattern underneath

A lost invoice in a new system is not a dispute. It is a break in the chain between approval and payment, and your job is to rebuild that chain for a stranger: a complete package, the transition owner, the new format, and a dated payment run. Do not wait for the new system to find your old invoice. It will not. Put a fresh, complete, correctly formatted invoice in front of the person who can approve it now, and the payment follows the paper trail you rebuilt.
