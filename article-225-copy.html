The Client Who Asks You to Expense It Instead of Invoicing: How to Keep Your Payment a Payment

The request arrives sounding like a convenience. Instead of invoicing, the client asks you to submit your fee through their expenses system, or to be reimbursed like a member of staff, or to attach your costs to someone else's claim. It will be faster, they say. Less paperwork. The same money, just a different route.

It is not the same money. An invoice is a commercial document with a due date, a payment term, and a paper trail that says a business owes another business an agreed amount for delivered work. An expense claim is an internal request that says an employee spent money and would like it back. The moment your fee becomes an expense, you lose the status that makes it collectable, and you gain a queue you cannot see and a set of rules you did not agree to.

Keep your payment a payment. Invoice normally, and treat the expenses route as a favour you decline politely, every time.

1. Understand what changes when a fee becomes an expense

An invoice creates a payable. The client's accounts team records it, ages it, and pays it on a schedule, and if they do not, you have a dated document showing what was agreed, when it was due, and what remains unpaid. A late fee clause, a stop-work clause, and a small claims filing all attach to that document.

An expense claim creates nothing of the kind. It sits inside an internal approval chain built for staff travel and stationery, not supplier payments. It may need a manager's sign-off, then a finance review, then a payroll run or a reimbursement batch, and at any of those stages it can be queried, returned, or quietly deprioritised without anyone owing you an explanation. There is no due date to miss, because expenses do not have due dates. There is no late fee to apply, because no payment term was ever agreed. If the claim is rejected, you are not a creditor with an unpaid invoice. You are a person whose form came back.

Worse, an expense claim often strips your fee of its commercial character. A finance team that sees "contractor fee" on an expenses form may treat it as a discretionary reimbursement, or ask for receipts you do not have, or route it to a budget that has already closed. Your work becomes a cost someone is doing you a favour by repaying, rather than a debt someone is obliged to settle.

2. Name the real reason the client prefers the expenses route

Clients rarely propose this route to harm you. Usually it solves an internal problem: they have no supplier onboarding for you, their procurement process takes weeks, or their expenses system is the one channel that moves without a purchase order. The person asking is often trying to get you paid quickly through the only door they can open.

That is a sympathetic motive, and it still does not make the route safe. The client's internal convenience is not your protection. If the expenses door closes, if the manager who promised to approve it leaves, if finance decides contractor fees do not belong on expense forms, you are the one left without a document. The person who meant well will apologise, and the apology will not pay the invoice you never issued.

So answer the motive, not just the request. "I understand the expenses route is faster on your side, but I need to invoice, because an invoice is what protects both of us if anything is queried later. I can make the invoice easy to process: I will include your cost code, your project reference, and the exact description your finance team needs." You have acknowledged their problem and offered a solution that keeps your payment a payment.

3. Never let an expense submission replace the invoice

The most dangerous version of this request is the one where the client asks you to skip the invoice entirely, because the expense claim "covers it". Refuse that version outright, in writing, before any work starts.

If a client genuinely cannot raise a supplier payment without an internal step first, the safe structure is invoice plus expense, never expense instead of invoice. Issue your invoice on your normal terms, and let the client attach a copy of it to whatever internal form they need. Your invoice remains the controlling document: it carries the amount, the due date, and the payment terms, and the internal form is just their way of routing it. If their system cannot accept an attached invoice, that is their process problem to solve, and solving it is part of hiring a supplier.

Put the arrangement in one sentence, in the same thread as the request: "To confirm, I will invoice the full amount on my usual terms, and you are welcome to attach the invoice to any internal form you need." Now the record shows an invoice exists, the amount, and the terms, regardless of what happens inside their system.

4. Watch for the expense route hiding a second problem

Sometimes the expenses request is not about convenience at all. A client who wants your fee on an expense form may be avoiding supplier onboarding because they do not want a record of hiring you, or keeping the spend under a threshold that triggers approval, or paying you from a budget that was never meant to cover contractor work.

Those motives change the risk. A client who avoids a paper trail for hiring you is a client who may also avoid a paper trail for paying you, and a payment routed through a budget it does not belong in can be reversed or questioned months later. If the expenses request comes with vagueness about who approves it, or reluctance to put the arrangement in writing, or pressure to start before any document exists, treat those as warning signs, not quirks.

Ask one direct question before you proceed: "Who approves this, and what happens if they decline it?" A client with a genuine process answer will name a person and a fallback. A client who cannot answer is telling you the route is a hope, not a system, and a hope is not a payment method.

5. Set the rule once, and apply it to every client

The expenses request is easy to accept once, because it arrives framed as a small favour for a friendly client. But a rule you break for one client becomes a precedent you will be asked to repeat, and each repeat adds another payment sitting in someone else's internal queue with no due date attached.

Make it a standing policy, stated the same way every time: "I always invoice. It keeps both our records clean, and it means your finance team has a proper document if they are ever audited." Framed as your standard practice, the refusal carries no accusation and no negotiation. Most clients accept it immediately, because they recognise a business rule when they hear one.

If a client insists, and the work depends on accepting the expenses route, that is information about the engagement, not a reason to bend. Require a deposit paid by normal bank transfer before work starts, so at least part of the fee travels through a proper channel, and keep the balance on an invoice with your usual terms. And if even that is refused, walk away. A client who will not let you issue an invoice is a client who will not let you prove you are owed money, and that is a risk no single project is worth.

The pattern underneath

An expense claim is not a faster invoice, it is a different kind of document, and the difference strips your fee of its due date, its payment terms, and its standing as a debt. Solve the client's internal problem by making your invoice easy to process, never by replacing it, put the arrangement in writing in the same thread, and treat vagueness about approval as a warning, not a detail. Your invoice is your evidence, and evidence only protects you if it exists in the first place.
