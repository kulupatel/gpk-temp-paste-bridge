The Client Who Says They Cannot Pay Until Their Funding Lands: How to Hold the Invoice While Their Investor Decides

The story sounds completely reasonable, which is what makes it dangerous. The client is a startup. They are raising a round. The term sheet is nearly signed, the investor is nearly committed, the money is nearly in the bank, and the invoice will be paid the moment the round closes. They are not refusing to pay. They are asking you to wait, and the wait has a reason, and the reason has a date, or at least the shape of a date, and the whole thing feels like a normal part of working with a young company.

Sometimes it is. Startups do raise money, rounds do close, and a client who pays late because the round slipped by two weeks is a client having a normal cash flow problem, not a client trying to avoid payment. But the funding story is also the perfect stall, because it has everything a stall needs: a sympathetic reason, a third party to blame, a future event that will fix everything, and no way for you to check whether any of it is true without looking like you do not trust the client. The freelancer who accepts the funding story at face value has handed the payment schedule to a person they have never met, an investor whose name they may not even know, and a round that may or may not exist.

The way through is not to disbelieve the client. It is to treat the funding story the way you would treat any other promise about future money: ask for facts, agree a bridge with a hard date, and keep the escalation on your own schedule, so that the round closing is good news, and the round not closing is not your problem.

1. Why the funding story is so believable

The funding story works because most of it is true in general, even when it is not true in particular. Startups do run on investor money. Rounds do take longer than planned. A seed round that was meant to close in March can easily close in May, and a founder who tells you the money is coming is usually telling you what they believe, not what they know. The story feels honest because the person telling it often believes it, and a sincere person telling a sincere story is very hard to argue with.

It also works because it gives you a reason to wait that flatters your judgement. Waiting for a funded startup feels like patience, and patience feels like professionalism, and the freelancer who waits is the freelancer who understands how startups work, while the freelancer who chases is the freelancer who does not get it. The story turns your own professionalism into the reason you should not chase, which is a neat trick, and it works best on freelancers who pride themselves on being easy to work with.

And it works because the money, when it arrives, is real money. A funded startup pays its invoices, usually promptly, because the investor expects clean books and the founder wants the freelancer available for the next phase. The promise of payment after the round is not a fantasy. It is a plausible future, and plausible futures are much harder to refuse than obvious lies, because refusing them feels like refusing a real business.

But plausibility is not the same as certainty, and the difference matters, because the freelancer who waits for a round that never closes has waited for nothing, and the invoice has aged while they waited, and an aged invoice is a harder invoice to collect. The question is never whether the funding story could be true. The question is whether you have any evidence that it is true in this case, and what happens to your invoice if it is not.

2. The test: is the funding real

There is a simple test, and it is the same test you would apply to any claim about money that is not yet in the bank: ask for the facts that a real round would produce, and listen to whether the answers are specific or vague.

A real round has a name. The client can tell you which round it is, seed or Series A, and roughly how much it is for, and when it is meant to close. They do not have to share the term sheet, and you should not ask for it, but they should be able to say "our seed round, $800,000, closing at the end of November" without hesitating, because a founder who is living inside a fundraise knows these numbers the way you know your own rates.

A real round has an investor, or at least a lead. The client can name the fund or the angel, or can say "our lead is a London seed fund, we have two others in diligence", and the answer sounds like a person describing a real process. A client who cannot name anyone, or who says "we have several interested parties" without a single name, date, or stage, is describing a hope, not a round.

A real round has a date that has moved before. Ask when the round was originally meant to close, and listen to the answer. A founder who says "it was meant to close in September, then October, now the end of November" is giving you a real history, and the history tells you the close date slips, which tells you to plan for the slip. A founder who says "it has always been the end of November" about a round that was mentioned to you in September is giving you a story that has been tidied up, and tidied stories are the ones that slip again.

And a real round has a partial payment in it somewhere. A client who genuinely expects $800,000 in three weeks can almost always pay part of your invoice now, from the money they have, or from a bridge, or from the founder's own pocket, because a business with a round about to close is not a business with no money at all. If the client says they cannot pay anything until the round closes, that tells you more than the round story does. It tells you the business has no cash buffer, no bridge, and no fallback, which means your invoice is not waiting on a round. It is waiting on a business with no money.

None of these questions is rude, and none of them accuses the client of lying. They are the questions a supplier asks a customer about a payment plan, and a client who is telling the truth will answer them easily, because the answers are facts they already know. A client who cannot answer them is not necessarily lying. They may simply be further from the money than they told you, and that is exactly what you need to know.

3. The bridge that works

If the funding story checks out, or checks out enough, the right move is a bridge: a short, written agreement that gives the client time without giving them an open-ended wait. The bridge has four parts, and all four need to be in writing, in the same message, because a verbal bridge is a conversation, and a written bridge is a record.

First, a partial payment now. Ask for a third, or a half, of the invoice immediately, as a sign of good faith and as proof that the client has some money, even if the round is the main event. A client who can pay a third now is a client with a real business. A client who cannot pay anything now is a client whose whole payment depends on the round, and you should know that before you agree to wait.

Second, a hard date, not a round date. "The balance is due on 30 November, whether or not the round has closed." The date is the point. A bridge that ends "when the round closes" has no end, because the round can always slip again, and the invoice will slip with it. A bridge that ends on a named date gives the client a deadline to work toward and gives you a date to escalate from, and the date should be short: two weeks, three at most, not two months, because a bridge that lasts two months is not a bridge. It is a loan.

Third, a pause on new work. "No new work starts until the balance is paid." This is the clause that stops the debt growing while you wait, and it is the clause clients resist most, because it turns the wait into something with a cost. But the cost is the point: a client who is waiting for funding should not be ordering more work on credit, and a freelancer who keeps working for a client who has not paid is funding the round themselves, with unpaid labour, which is the most expensive way to invest in someone else's startup.

Fourth, a consequence for the date passing. "If the balance is not paid by 30 November, the late fee applies, and work pauses until it is paid." The consequence does not have to be harsh. It has to be stated, in advance, in writing, so that the 30th of November is not another conversation but a date with a known outcome. A client who agrees to the consequence in writing is a client who takes the date seriously, and a client who refuses the consequence is telling you the date was never real.

Send the bridge as a single message, and ask the client to confirm it in reply, because a confirmed bridge is an agreement, and an unconfirmed bridge is a hope with better formatting. "To confirm: $800 now, the $1,600 balance by 30 November whether or not the round has closed, no new work until the balance is paid, and the late fee applies from 1 December." That message takes a minute to write, and it is the difference between waiting with a plan and waiting on a promise.

4. When the funding never lands

Sometimes the round does not close, and the date passes, and the story changes. The investor pulled out, the round is being restructured, a new lead is in diligence, the close is now January. The story will always have a next chapter, because a founder who is fundraising always has a next investor, and the next investor always has a next date, and the next date is always just far enough away to ask for another wait.

This is the moment the bridge earns its keep. The date passed, the consequence was agreed, and the next step is not another negotiation but the escalation you already wrote down: the late fee applies, the work pauses, and the final notice goes out on your schedule, not theirs. Do not agree to a second bridge on the same terms, because a second bridge is the first bridge with a new date, and a client who has already missed one date will miss the second one the same way.

Instead, ask for a payment plan that does not depend on the round at all. "Pay $400 a week for four weeks, from revenue, not from the round, and the work restarts when the first payment arrives." A client who can pay from revenue is a client with a business. A client who cannot pay anything except from a future round is a client whose business has no money, and no amount of waiting will fix that, because the round is not the only problem. The business is the problem, and the business is not yours to fix.

And if the client cannot pay anything, stop the work, send the final notice, and decide whether the debt is worth pursuing, with the same honest maths you would apply to any unpaid invoice: the amount, the cost of chasing it, the chance of recovering it, and the value of the time you would spend. A startup that never raises is a startup that fails, and a failed startup pays nobody, and the freelancer who keeps working for it is the last person to find that out, because everyone else stopped working months earlier.

5. Prevention: never start on a funding promise

The best time to deal with the funding story is before it is told, which means before the work starts, when your terms are still yours to set.

First, take a deposit, and take it before the work starts, not after the funding lands. A deposit from a startup is the same as a deposit from anyone else: it is the price of the booking, and a startup that cannot pay a deposit is a startup that cannot pay an invoice, and the deposit is how you find that out cheaply, before the work is done.

Second, split the work into milestones, and tie each milestone to a payment, so that no single invoice is ever large enough to sink you if the round slips. A $6,000 project split into three $2,000 milestones, each paid before the next starts, is a project where the most you can lose is one milestone, not the whole fee.

Third, put a funding clause in your terms, in plain words: "Payment terms are not dependent on the client's funding, investment, or third-party payments. Invoices are due on the dates stated, regardless of the client's funding status." That sentence does two jobs. It tells the client the rule before they sign, so the funding story is not a surprise to either side. And it gives you something to quote when the story arrives, so the answer is the contract speaking, not you being unsympathetic to startups.

Fourth, ask about funding at the scoping stage, not at the chasing stage. "How is the project funded, and is the budget approved and available now?" A client who answers that question easily is a client with a real budget. A client who answers it with a story about a round is a client you should quote a deposit and milestone terms to, before you agree to anything, because the answer tells you the risk, and the terms are how you price it.

The funding story is not a reason to refuse startup clients. Startups are good clients, when they pay, and many of them pay well and on time. It is a reason to treat the funding story as what it is: a claim about future money, made by a person who benefits from you believing it, which means it deserves the same treatment as any other claim about future money. Ask for facts, agree a bridge with a hard date, and keep the escalation on your own schedule. The round closing is good news. The round not closing should not be your problem.

The pattern underneath

A funding story is a promise about future money, and future money is not a payment, because it has no date you control, no amount you can verify, and no penalty for not arriving, and it is made by a person who benefits from you waiting. Ask for the facts a real round would produce, a name, an investor, a close date with a history, and a partial payment now. Agree a bridge with a hard date that does not depend on the round, a pause on new work, and a stated consequence, and put it in writing in one message. When the date passes, escalate on your schedule, and do not agree a second bridge on the same terms. And prevent the next one with a deposit, milestone splits, and a funding clause in your terms, because a startup that cannot pay a deposit is a startup that cannot pay an invoice, and the deposit is how you find that out cheaply.
