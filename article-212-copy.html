The Client Who Says "Finance Is On It" for Weeks: How to Turn a Stall Phrase Into a Deadline

Every freelancer knows the phrase. The invoice is overdue, you chase, and the reply comes back smooth and empty: "Finance is on it." No date, no name, no reference number. Just a reassurance that sounds like progress and commits to nothing. A week later you chase again, and the answer is the same phrase, slightly reworded. The invoice is not being disputed. It is being held in a verbal waiting room with no exit date.

"Finance is on it" is not an update. It is a sentence designed to end the conversation, and it works because it sounds reasonable. Your job is to turn it into something measurable: a named person, a date, and a consequence if that date passes. You do that without accusing anyone, because the contact passing the phrase along may genuinely believe it.

1. Answer the phrase with a question, not an argument

Do not argue with "Finance is on it." Arguing makes you the difficult supplier, and it gives the contact an excuse to stop replying. Instead, treat the phrase as the start of an answer, not the end of one, and ask the one question it leaves open: "Thanks, could you confirm who in finance has it and the date it is scheduled to be paid?"

That question is polite, specific, and hard to dodge twice. A real payment in progress has a name and a date attached to it. A stall has neither, and asking for both exposes the difference without you having to say so. If the reply gives you a name and a date, you have turned a phrase into a plan. If it gives you another phrase, you have your answer about what is actually happening, and you can act on it.

2. Set the date yourself when they will not

If the second reply is still dateless, stop asking for their date and give them yours. Something like: "I will treat [date, seven days out] as the payment date unless I hear otherwise. If it has not cleared by then, I will send the formal final notice." This is not a threat. It is a freelancer replacing a missing deadline with a stated one, and giving the client a fair chance to correct it.

The power of this move is that silence becomes agreement. If the client has a real payment date, they will correct yours with theirs, and you have a date either way. If they stay silent, your date stands, and your next chase is not another request for an update. It is a follow-up on a date they were told and did not dispute. That is a much stronger position than chasing a phrase for a third week.

3. Go around the phrase, not through it

When one contact keeps repeating the phrase, the problem may be that they are not the person who can move the payment. They are relaying, and the relay has no lever. Ask, directly and without blame, for the person who does: "Could you connect me with the finance contact handling this invoice, or share their email so I can send the invoice details directly?"

Most contacts will pass you on once you ask plainly, because being the middleman for an overdue invoice is work they do not want either. If they refuse, note the refusal and the date, and move to the formal route: a written final notice sent to the contact and, where you have it, the company's general or finance address. A formal notice does not need the contact's permission, and it changes the conversation from a chase into a record.

4. Keep a dated log of every phrase

Write down every "Finance is on it" with its date, exactly as it arrived. After three such replies over three weeks, the log itself is your evidence: the client has acknowledged the debt repeatedly while paying nothing, and no date has ever been given. That record matters if you escalate to a formal demand letter or a small claim, because it shows a pattern rather than a single delay.

The log also protects your own judgment. Chasing feels personal after a while, and it is easy to start doubting whether you are being unreasonable. A log with dates replaces feeling with fact: three reassurances, zero payments, zero dates. That is not an unreasonable freelancer. That is an unpaid invoice with a stall attached.

5. Name the stall in your terms, for the next client

The best time to defeat this phrase is before it is ever used on you. Add one line to your payment terms or your invoice footer: "Payment queries must include a named contact and a scheduled payment date." It sounds formal, and it is, but it gives you a clean reference the first time the phrase appears: "Per the terms on the invoice, could you confirm the finance contact and the scheduled date?"

You can also prevent the phrase by setting the expectation at the start of the project: "If payment ever slips, I will need a name and a date from finance, not just a status." Clients who hear that early rarely offer a dateless reassurance later, because they know what answer you will accept. The phrase survives on vagueness. A term that demands specifics starves it.

The pattern underneath

"Finance is on it" is a stall phrase, and stall phrases are defeated by turning them into measurements. Ask for a name and a date, set your own date when they will not, go to the person who can actually pay, log every dateless reply, and write the specificity requirement into your terms for next time. The client keeps their process, and you keep a deadline, which is the only thing a reassurance was ever supposed to protect.
