The Client Who Pays Short: What to Do When the Payment Is Less Than the Invoice

You invoiced $4,200. The transfer arrives for $3,850. No note, no explanation, no apology. Congratulations, you have been short-paid, and the worst move you can make is doing nothing about it. Silence reads as agreement, and the next invoice will arrive short too.

Short payment is not the same as late payment. It is a unilateral edit of your contract, made after the work is done. Handle it like one.

First: check whether the gap is innocent

Not every short payment is hostile. Before you escalate, look for the three innocent explanations:

  1. A transfer fee or currency conversion ate the difference. Common with international clients and wire transfers. If your terms say "client covers all transfer fees," this is still their problem, but solve it with a one-line fix, not a fight.
  2. A withholding tax was deducted. Some countries require clients to withhold a percentage on cross-border payments. Ask for the withholding certificate; you can often claim it against your own tax bill.
  3. They deducted an expense or credit you actually agreed to. Check the contract before you react.

If none of these explain the gap, you have a real dispute.

Demand the deduction in writing

Reply on the same thread as the invoice, within 24 hours. Keep it factual, not emotional:

"Thanks for the payment of $3,850. The invoice was $4,200, so there is a $350 difference. Can you send me the itemized breakdown of what was deducted and the clause it is based on?"

This does two things. It puts the $350 discrepancy on the written record, and it forces them to articulate a reason, which is much harder than vaguely paying less and hoping you do not notice.

Split the dispute: take the undisputed, fight the rest

This is the single most useful short-payment rule, and most freelancers never use it. If the client claims part of the deduction is legitimate, accept the part you both agree on and isolate the rest.

"The $200 currency fee is on me. The remaining $150 still does not match any agreed term. Please pay that by Friday, and we can discuss the invoice wording for next time."

Why this works: it shows good faith, it shrinks the argument to a concrete number, and it stops the client from holding the whole payment hostage over a disagreement about a slice of it.

Know the four classic short-pay excuses

The "we applied our standard discount." You never agreed to a discount. Say so: "No discount was agreed in our contract. The full balance stands."

The "we rounded it." Rounding is not a recognized accounting principle. "The invoice total is exact, not approximate. Please pay the difference."

The "the work was only worth this much." Pricing was set before the work, not judged after it. If there is a genuine quality complaint, they get to dispute it formally, not price-cut by wire transfer.

The "we deducted the cost of fixing your mistake." Maybe. But they do not get to be the judge, jury, and accountant. "Send me the repair invoice and we will resolve it as a separate item. The original balance is unaffected."

Never let a short payment sit

A short payment you accept quietly becomes the new price. The client learns that your invoices are opening bids. Even a $40 gap matters, because the pattern it sets is worth far more than $40.

If the remaining difference survives the conversation, treat it like any other unpaid balance: formal sequence, demand letter, and the full chase.
