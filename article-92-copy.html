The Rush Revision Problem: Pricing the "One Quick Change" Without a Fight

"Just one quick change." It is the most expensive sentence in freelancing, because it is never one and it is rarely quick. The change takes forty minutes, the review round takes two days, and the invoice for your revision time gets a puzzled reply: "I thought revisions were included."

The problem is not the client. The problem is that "revisions included" was never defined, so both sides are negotiating the price of your time after the time is already spent. Here is how to price rush revisions before they happen.

Separate rush from normal at the start

Every project has two speeds: normal and rush. Normal revisions ride the existing schedule. Rush revisions jump the queue, happen after hours, or arrive with a deadline that forces you to rearrange other work. If your terms do not name both, every revision defaults to normal, and rush work gets paid at the normal rate, which is to say not at all.

Name the difference in your terms before the project starts: standard revisions follow the project timeline; rush revisions (turnaround under 48 hours, or work outside agreed hours) are billed at the rush rate. Clients accept this easily at the start because nobody believes they are the rush client.

The rush revision rate

Pick one formula and use it every time:

  • 1.5x your hourly rate for rush revisions, or
  • a flat rush fee added to the invoice (for example, 25% of the project fee for any revision round under 48 hours).

The flat fee is simpler for fixed-price projects and easier to quote mid-project. The hourly multiplier is fairer for hourly engagements. Whichever you choose, it must exist before the rush request arrives. A rush rate invented in the middle of an argument feels like a penalty. The same rate stated in the terms feels like a menu.

The mid-project quote script

When the "one quick change" arrives with a tight deadline, do not start working. Send this:

"Happy to do this. Standard turnaround on revisions is 3 working days, so I can have it back to you Thursday. If you need it today, my rush revision rate applies, which would be [amount]. Which would you like?"

This does three things. It states the normal option first, so the client sees a choice, not a demand. It prices the rush option precisely, so there is no argument later. And it makes the client choose in writing, which is the entire battle. Most clients pick standard turnaround and the "urgent" revision turns out to be less urgent than advertised. The ones who pick rush pay you for the disruption.

When the revision is not actually a revision

Watch for the revision that is really new work wearing a costume: a "small tweak" that changes the scope, a "quick version" in a different format, a "tiny addition" that requires new research. The test is simple: would this have been part of the original brief if the client had asked for it then? If no, it is new work, and it gets quoted like new work, not squeezed into the revision budget.

Say it plainly: "That one is outside the revision rounds we agreed on, so let me quote it separately: [amount], [timeline]." No apology, no debate. Clients who meant it as a revision adjust. Clients who were fishing for free work learn the boundary.

The revision-rounds limit

Define revision rounds in the quote: two rounds included, additional rounds at your hourly or day rate. Unlimited revisions is a pricing error, not a selling point. A client with infinite revisions has no incentive to give consolidated feedback, so you get eleven scattered messages instead of one clean round, and each one costs you an afternoon.

When you quote the third round, the client gets focused fast. The limit protects their budget too, because scattered revision chaos is how projects double in cost and halve in quality.

Prevention: the feedback format clause

Most revision pileups are actually feedback-format problems. Add this line to your terms: "Revision requests must be consolidated into a single message per round; additional rounds are billed at the standard rate."

One message per round forces the client to organize their thoughts, which cuts your revision count in half. The billing line behind it means you are paid when they cannot. This single clause has prevented more unpaid revision work than any pricing formula I know.
