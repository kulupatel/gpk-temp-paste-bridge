Should You Offer a Discount for Early Payment? The Real Math

"2/10 net 30" is one of the oldest lines in business invoicing: pay within 10 days, get a 2% discount, otherwise pay the full amount in 30 days. Freelancers see it in vendor paperwork and wonder if they should offer it too. It sounds generous. It sounds like it would make clients pay faster. The math says something more complicated.

What the 2% actually costs you

Take that 2% discount at face value: the client pays 20 days early and you give up 2% of the invoice. Annualize it and the number is startling. There are roughly eighteen 20-day periods in a year. Giving up 2% eighteen times a year is the equivalent of paying about 36% annual interest for the privilege of being paid early. No bank on earth charges you that for a short-term loan.

That is the first honest framing: an early-payment discount is not a gift to the client. It is you buying your own money back at a terrible rate. If your cash flow is genuinely desperate, it can be a rational short-term move. But offered as a standing policy, it quietly taxes every invoice.

When it does work

There are two situations where early-payment discounts earn their keep.

Chronic slow payers with functioning AP departments. Some large clients simply will not pay before 60 days unless something forces the issue. A 1.5 to 2% discount for 10-day payment gives their accounts payable team a reason to fast-track your invoice. You are not buying speed from the company. You are giving the AP clerk a metric to hit. In that context, the discount often pays for itself by converting a 75-day payment into a 10-day one.

New client relationships where you want to set a rhythm. Offering a one-time early-payment discount on the first invoice trains the client's payment behavior from day one. Once the habit of fast payment is established, you can let the discount expire and most clients keep the rhythm. Think of it as onboarding, not policy.

The cheaper alternatives

Before you offer a discount, try the moves that cost you nothing.

State a late fee instead. A 1.5% monthly late fee on overdue invoices changes behavior just as effectively as an early discount, and it costs you zero on the invoices that pay on time. Psychology backs this: people respond more to avoiding a penalty than to earning an equivalent reward.

Make payment effortless. Invoices with a payment link get paid faster than invoices with bank details the client has to type. Same-day PDF, two payment methods, one click. A surprising share of "slow payers" are just busy people who kept meaning to get around to your invoice.

Ask for a deposit. 30 to 50% upfront does more for your cash flow than any discount ever will, and it filters out the clients most likely to pay late in the first place. Deposits are the single highest-leverage payment term a freelancer can use.

The rule for using discounts honestly

If you use an early-payment discount, make it small, make it time-limited, and never make it automatic. "1% if paid within 7 days on this invoice" is a nudge. "2/10 net 30 on everything" is a standing tax on your own revenue that your slowest clients will happily collect while still paying late, because nothing in the discount punishes lateness.

And never offer the discount and waive the late fee in the same terms. That combination tells the client exactly what they already suspected: you are flexible about when you get paid. The whole point of payment terms is to make the due date real.

The fastest payment comes from structure, not generosity: clear terms, deposits, effortless payment, and a late fee you actually enforce. Discounts are a tool for specific situations, not a philosophy. Use them like a scalpel and they work. Use them like a blanket policy and they cost you money every single month.
