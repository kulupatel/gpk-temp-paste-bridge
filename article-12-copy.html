Why Net 30 Is Killing Your Cash Flow (And What to Use Instead)

Net 30 is the default payment term almost every freelancer copies onto their first invoice. It looks professional. Every big company uses it. And it quietly turns you into your client's interest-free lender.

Think about what Net 30 actually means: you do the work in week one, send the invoice, and then wait at least a month to get paid for work you already finished. If the client pays on time. If they pay late, and many do, that month stretches further. Meanwhile your rent, your software subscriptions, and your groceries are all on Net 0. Nobody gives you 30 days.

Large companies can afford Net 30. They have finance departments, credit lines, and predictable revenue. You have one bank account and a calendar full of deadlines. Copying enterprise payment terms as a solo freelancer is like a rowboat copying a cruise ship's fueling schedule. The scale does not match.

The psychology problem: long terms signal that you can wait

Payment terms are not just logistics. They are communication. A Net 30 invoice tells the client, in writing, that your money is not urgent. Clients triage their payments, and the freelancer whose invoice says "whenever, within a month" goes to the bottom of the pile, below the vendors who charge late fees and the landlord who does not negotiate.

Shorter terms do the opposite. An invoice that says "due within 7 days" tells the client this is a professional operation with a real billing cycle. It does not sound desperate or aggressive. It sounds like every other serious business they pay.

What to use instead

You do not need one perfect term. You need a small menu you can match to the situation:

Net 7 or Net 14 for new clients. This is the single highest-leverage change most freelancers can make. Seven or fourteen days is completely normal in freelance work, and it halves your exposure to slow payers.

50% upfront, balance on delivery. For any project over a few hundred dollars, a deposit is not optional equipment. It filters out the clients who were never going to pay on time, and it means the worst case is chasing half the invoice, not the whole thing.

Milestones for big projects. Break a $5,000 project into three payments tied to deliverables. Each milestone is a small invoice that is easy to pay quickly, and a client who stalls on milestone two cannot quietly accumulate a giant unpaid balance.

The "standard terms" framing. When you introduce shorter terms, never make it personal. Do not say "I need the money sooner." Say "My standard terms are Net 14" or "I take 50% to book the project." Standard terms sound like policy. Personal needs sound like problems, and clients do not pay faster for your problems.

How to change terms with existing clients

You do not need to renegotiate every relationship on Monday. Use the grandfather approach: keep current terms for current projects, and put the new terms on the next quote or the next invoice. One sentence does it: "Quick heads-up: my payment terms are Net 14 starting with this invoice." No apology, no long explanation. Most clients will not even comment. The ones who push back are giving you useful information about how they will treat your future invoices.

The one rule underneath all of this

Your payment terms should match your cash flow, not someone else's. Every day between "work done" and "money received" is a day you financed the client's project for free. Shorten that gap first, and half your late-payment problems shrink before a single chase email is ever needed.
