When to Send an Unpaid Invoice to Collections: The Real Costs and Cutoffs

Every freelancer eventually gets one: the invoice that will not die. Months overdue, client gone quiet, every email ignored. The question stops being "how do I get paid" and becomes "is this worth pursuing." This is the honest decision framework for collections, small claims, and walking away.

First, the cutoffs: how late is too late to act normally

There is a rough timeline that debt-recovery professionals use:

  • 30 to 60 days overdue: still a normal collections conversation. Polite-to-firm emails and one phone call usually resolve it.
  • 60 to 90 days overdue: this is the zone where professionals hand it off. After 90 days, each passing month sharply reduces the odds of full recovery, because the client has mentally written you off as a creditor they can ignore.
  • 90 to 120+ days overdue: recovery now requires leverage, not reminders. That leverage is a collections agency, small claims court, or a formal demand from someone other than you.

The practical takeaway: if an invoice passes 60 days with no payment and no credible payment plan, stop sending reminder emails and choose a real recovery path. Hope is not a strategy past 60 days.

Option 1: a collections agency

A commercial collections agency takes over the chase. Here is how it actually works:

  • They charge a percentage of what they collect, typically 25 to 50 percent, on a contingency basis (no upfront fee from you). The percentage depends on the debt size and age; older debts cost more.
  • They report to business credit bureaus, which is the real leverage. Many clients ignore you but pay fast once a collections agency with reporting power is involved.
  • They are regulated (in the US, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act limits their behavior), which means they cannot harass, but it also means they are professional about it.

The math question: on a $2,000 invoice, you would likely recover $1,000 to $1,500 through an agency after their cut. That is real money, and it costs you nothing unless they collect. Many agencies have minimums (often $500 to $1,000 per debt), so check before you sign.

Option 2: small claims court

Small claims court is designed for exactly this situation, no lawyer needed in most jurisdictions:

  • Filing fees are modest, usually $30 to $100 depending on the state or country.
  • Limits vary: in many US states it is $5,000 to $10,000; the UK equivalent (Money Claims Online) handles up to 10,000 pounds; Canadian provinces range from roughly $5,000 to $35,000.
  • Your paper trail does the work for you: the contract, the invoice, the delivery records, and the follow-up emails are the entire case.

The honest limitation: winning a judgment and collecting a judgment are two different things. If the client is broke rather than difficult, a court order does not print money. Collections agencies are sometimes better at actual recovery even after you win.

Option 3: the formal demand letter before either

Before you pay anyone or file anything, send one final letter that is different from your emails. A demand letter on paper (or from a lawyer, which costs $150 to $300 for a single letter in many markets) does two things: it signals you are serious enough to spend money, and it starts a clock, usually 10 to 14 days, after which you state exactly what happens next.

Many debts end here. The client who ignored six emails pays within the week, because the cost of being ignored just went up.

When walking away is the right call

Not every invoice is worth recovering. Walk away when:

  • The amount is less than the recovery cost (a $200 invoice is not worth a $150 filing fee plus a day in court)
  • The client is genuinely insolvent, not just difficult (you cannot collect from someone who has nothing)
  • Your paper trail is weak (no signed contract, no delivery proof, verbal-only agreements) and a judge or agency has little to work with
  • The time and stress cost more than the money (your hours have a rate too)

Walking away is not losing. It is a business decision, and it should be logged as one: write the debt off for tax purposes (talk to your accountant about bad-debt deductions in your jurisdiction), update your contract so the next client cannot do this, and move on.

The one move that makes all of this easier

Every option above gets dramatically easier with a strong paper trail: a signed contract with a due date and late fee, delivery records, and all communication in one thread. The freelancer with documentation gets a collections agency to take the case and a judge to rule quickly. The freelancer without it gets told there is nothing to work with.
