Don't Start Until the Paperwork Exists: The Pre-Work Checklist That Prevents Payment Fights

Most payment fights are lost before the work begins. Not at the invoice stage, not in the chase emails, but in the cheerful first week when everything feels easy and nobody wants to "slow things down with paperwork." Two months later, when the invoice lands, both sides remember a different agreement. The client remembers "I thought that was included." You remember "they said they would pay."

The fix is a pre-work checklist that takes about twenty minutes per client and prevents most of the disputes that become late payments. Here it is.

The one-page terms note

Before you start, write down in one short message or document: the fee, what is included, what is explicitly not included, the payment terms (Net 15, say), the late fee, and the deposit. Send it to the client and get a written "looks good" back. This is not a formal contract, though it is the single most useful page of your whole engagement. If a dispute ever arises, this is the document that ends it, because it records what both of you actually agreed to, in plain language, before money was at stake.

Who exactly is going to pay you

"Just bill us" is not an answer. Before starting, get four things in writing: the name and email of the person who approves your invoice, the name and email of the accounts payable contact (or confirmation there is no separate AP person), the PO number or reference required on the invoice, and how they pay (bank transfer, PayPal, Wise, a vendor portal). Every missing answer here is a week of delay later. The AP contact question alone is worth the whole checklist, because an invoice sent to the wrong person is an invoice that sits.

The scope in writing, with revision limits

Payment disputes often wear a scope costume. "This is not what we asked for" becomes "we are not paying until it is fixed." Before starting, confirm in writing: what the deliverables are, how many revision rounds are included, and what extra rounds cost. The number does not matter as much as its existence. Three documented rounds turn a fourth-round complaint into a paid invoice for additional work instead of an unpaid standoff.

Your late fee, stated before it applies

A late fee mentioned for the first time on an overdue invoice reads as a penalty. A late fee in the pre-work note reads as a term. State it now, while everything is friendly: "Invoices are due within 15 days; a 2 percent monthly late fee applies after that." Most clients will never trigger it, but the ones who habitually pay late behave differently when the consequence is already on record. If you want a stronger version, add the work-pause rule: "Work pauses if an invoice is 30 days overdue." Again, friendly when stated early, explosive when introduced late.

The 10-minute kickoff email

Combine everything above into one kickoff email you can reuse as a template: the fee and terms recap, the billing contact confirmation, the PO number, the revision limit, the late fee line, and a request for written confirmation. Save it as a template and it takes ten minutes. Send it before any real work begins, and do not treat the client's confirmation as optional. No confirmation, no start.

What if the client says "just start, we'll sort the paperwork later"

This is the line that costs freelancers more money than any other sentence in business. There are two answers, depending on the client. For a client you trust, do a tiny paid start: one milestone, one small invoice, full terms on it. If the paperwork never materializes, your exposure is one milestone, not the whole project. For a client you do not know yet, hold the line: "I can start the moment we have the terms confirmed, which takes you two minutes to reply to." A client who cannot spend two minutes on terms is telling you exactly how much your invoice will matter to them.

The bottom line

None of this is legal work, and none of it is confrontational. It is ten minutes of boring clarity that saves you months of chasing. The freelancers who get paid on time are rarely the ones with the best chase emails. They are the ones whose paperwork existed before the work did.
