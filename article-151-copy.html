The Client Who Reopens a Closed Project to Delay Payment: How to Keep Closed Work Closed

The project is done, approved, and delivered. Then, days before the invoice is due, a message arrives: one more thought, one small change, one thing they just noticed. The work is not really reopened. The payment clock is. A reopened project gives the client a reason to hold payment that sounds reasonable, because who pays for work that is still being changed? The freelancer who keeps closed work closed does it with a definition of done that was written before the work started, not argued after it ended.

1. Define done before the work starts

The only definition of done that survives a payment dispute is the one in the proposal. It should name the deliverables, the number of revision rounds included, what counts as approval, and what happens after approval. "Delivery of the final files plus written approval closes the project. Changes after approval are a new task, billed separately." This sentence is worth more than any chase email, because it moves the argument from taste to contract. Without it, "done" means whatever the client feels on the day the invoice arrives, and that feeling always arrives late. With it, a reopening request is not a judgement call. It is a new task with its own price.

2. Separate the tweak from the payment

When the reopening message arrives, answer the tweak and the payment in separate sentences, and never let one hold the other. "Happy to look at that change as a separate small task. The invoice for the approved work is still due on the 14th, and final files release on payment." This matters because the client's real leverage is the link between the two: as long as the project feels open, payment feels premature. Breaking the link removes the leverage without refusing the work. Clients with a genuine small fix accept the separation easily, because they never intended the fix to delay payment. Clients who were using the fix as a delay object to the separation, and that objection is the whole story.

3. Price the reopening, even a small one

Free reopenings teach clients that closed work can be reopened at no cost, which means it will be reopened every time an invoice is due. Price every post-approval change, even a small one, and state the price before doing the work. The price does not need to be large. It needs to exist, because a price turns "one more thought" from a delay tactic into a purchase decision, and most delay tactics evaporate once they cost something. If the change is genuinely tiny and you want to do it free as goodwill, say so explicitly and once: "I will make this one change free, and the project stays closed after it." Goodwill with a boundary is generosity. Goodwill without one is a new revision round.

4. Use the approval record, not your memory

A reopening usually comes with a rewritten history: the client remembers approving something different, or not approving at all. Your answer should quote the record, not your recollection. The approval email, the signed-off draft, the message that said "this looks great, go ahead." Quote it briefly and neutrally: "On the 3rd you approved the final version in writing, which closed the project under our agreement." This is not combative. It is the difference between two people remembering differently and one document settling it. Keep approvals in one place from the start, because the freelancer who has to search for proof has already lost the week the client was trying to buy.

5. Decide what a repeated reopening means

One reopening near an invoice date might be coincidence. A reopening near every invoice date is a payment strategy, and it should change how you work with that client. Options that follow from the pattern are all legitimate: payment before final delivery on the next project, a shorter revision window with a written expiry, a clause that approval after a set number of days counts as acceptance, or simply declining the next project. None of these punish the client. They remove the mechanism that made delay profitable. The freelancer who keeps accepting reopenings while complaining about late payment is funding the very tactic that delays them.

The pattern underneath

Closed work gets reopened near invoice dates because an open project is the most polite excuse for an unpaid invoice. The defence is not suspicion. It is structure: a definition of done written before the work starts, payment separated from any later tweak, a price on every reopening, an approval record you can quote, and a structural change when the pattern repeats. The freelancer who keeps closed work closed does not lose flexible clients. They stop paying for flexibility with their own cash flow.
