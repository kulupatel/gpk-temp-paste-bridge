The Client Who Went Bankrupt Owing You Money: What Freelancers Can Actually Recover

The email arrives on a Tuesday. Your client, the one with the $6,000 invoice sitting at 40 days overdue, has "entered administration" or "filed for insolvency" or "ceased trading." The contact who approved your work has gone silent. The money you were chasing is now trapped inside a legal process you never signed up for. Here is what actually happens next, what you can realistically recover, and how to make sure it never costs you this much again.

This is general information, not legal advice. Insolvency law varies by country, and the sums here justify a real lawyer before you spend anything.

1. Stop everything in the first 48 hours

Do not deliver another file, another revision, or another hour. Anything you hand over after the insolvency starts may be unbillable by definition. Pause all work the moment you hear the word, and tell the client in writing that work is paused pending payment. Then assemble your paper trail: the contract or terms note, every invoice (paid and unpaid), every delivery confirmation, every email where they acknowledged the work. Administrators deal in documents, not memories.

2. File your claim with the administrator, properly and fast

When a company enters a formal insolvency process, an administrator or liquidator takes over, and creditors are invited to submit a "proof of debt" or claim form. Get your claim in early and make it complete: every unpaid invoice, any late fees your contract entitles you to, and the supporting documents from step one. Claim deadlines are strict, and late claims can be rejected outright. If no formal process has started yet and the company has simply "ceased trading," send a formal demand letter immediately, because the formal process may follow.

3. Understand where you stand in the queue

Here is the honest math. When an insolvent company is wound up, creditors are paid in a legal order: secured lenders first, then employee wage claims, then tax authorities, and only then unsecured creditors. Freelancers are almost always unsecured creditors, near the back of the line. By the time the queue reaches you, there is often little or nothing left. Recoveries of a few cents on the dollar are common; total losses are normal. Do not make financial plans around this money coming back. Grieve it, file the claim, and move on.

4. Do not throw good money after bad

A demand letter to a dead company is just paper. Filing a small claim against an insolvent company usually wastes the filing fee, because a court judgment does not create money that is not there. And hiring a lawyer typically costs more than the average freelance invoice is worth. There are narrow exceptions: if a director signed a personal guarantee, or traded while knowingly insolvent, there may be a route to the individual. But that is genuinely lawyer territory, get real advice before spending a dollar on it.

5. Claim the tax write-off, correctly

An unpaid invoice from a dead client may be deductible as a bad debt, which softens the blow at tax time. The rules depend on how you account: on cash-basis accounting you generally cannot deduct what you never received, while accrual-basis filers often can. Keep the insolvency notice, your filed claim, and the administrator's correspondence as proof. This is not tax advice; run the specifics past your accountant, ideally before year end so the loss lands in the right tax year.

6. Build the bankruptcy-proof habit for next time

You cannot prevent clients from failing, but you can cap what any single failure costs you. Deposits on every new engagement, milestone billing on anything over a few thousand dollars, and 50 percent upfront from any client you have not worked with before. Watch the early warnings too: a client who goes suddenly silent on invoices, whose AP contact "left the company," who starts talking about "restructuring" or asks you to hold off invoicing. Those are the weeks to tighten terms, not extend them. No single client should ever owe you more than you can afford to lose.
