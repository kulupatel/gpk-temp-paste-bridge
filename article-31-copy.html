Getting Paid Across Borders: Currency, Fees, and Timing Traps for International Clients

International clients are some of the best-paying work a freelancer can win. They are also the slowest, most expensive clients to actually collect from, unless you set up the payment mechanics correctly before the first invoice goes out. Here are the traps and how to beat each one.

Invoice in your currency, or state the rate date

The single biggest international-payment mistake is invoicing in the client's currency with no exchange-rate terms. Between the invoice date and the payment date, currency markets move. If the client's currency slides 4% in six weeks, you just took a 4% pay cut without negotiating anything.

The fix is simple. Invoice in your own currency whenever you can, and say so in your contract: "Invoices are issued in [your currency]." If the client insists on their currency, add one line to the invoice: "Exchange rate fixed at the invoice date per [your bank's] published rate." Never let the conversion float on your side of the ledger.

Decide who eats the transfer fees, in writing

Cross-border transfers are not free. Wire fees, intermediary bank deductions, and platform cuts quietly shave invoices, and when nobody agreed who pays, the client deducts the fee from your payment and you discover it 60 days later.

Write it into the contract and restate it on the invoice: "Client covers all transfer and conversion fees; the invoiced amount must arrive in full." Services like Wise make the fee visible and low. PayPal makes it invisible and high. Picking the rail is part of picking the fee, and the contract should name it.

Expect the timing traps

International payments have three built-in delays freelancers forget.

Weekend and holiday cutoffs. A wire sent Friday afternoon crosses time zones and banking holidays. "I sent it Tuesday" can mean your money arrives Friday. When a client says they paid, ask for the transfer reference and date, and expect two to five business days of transit.

Approval layers. The country-tone article covered the cultural side; the operational side is that international AP departments batch foreign payments on schedules, weekly, biweekly, monthly. Ask at kickoff: "When does your team process international vendor payments?" Then invoice so your due date lands in the batch, not after it.

First-transfer verification. The first payment to a new recipient often triggers a bank compliance hold. It is normal, it is not a scam, and it adds three to seven days. Tell new international clients your bank details in the very first invoice, not the third, so the hold happens early.

Make payment effortless across borders

Everything in the payment-setup article applies double internationally. Two payment methods minimum, because a method that is easy in one country can be blocked in another. PDF invoices, never editable formats. And a named billing contact with a direct email, because chasing "accounts@bigcorp.de" across language barriers is how invoices die.

One more rule: for a first engagement with an international client, take a 30 to 50% deposit. Currency risk, compliance delays, and distance all stack on a first invoice. The deposit both protects you and tests whether their payment machinery actually works before you do the full job.

The sentence that prevents most of this

Add this paragraph to your contract before any international project starts: "Invoices are issued in [your currency] and payable within [N] days. The client covers all transfer and conversion fees. International payments are expected to allow five business days of transit after the client's payment confirmation."

Five lines. No drama. It answers every question that usually becomes a dispute three months later: which currency, who pays the fees, when "paid" actually means paid.

International clients are worth the effort. Their budgets are bigger, their pipelines are steadier, and once your payment mechanics are right, they pay like clockwork. The freelancers who get burned are the ones who invoice the same way they invoice local clients and learn about currency slides and fee deductions from experience. Set the mechanics once, in the contract, and the border stops being a trap.
