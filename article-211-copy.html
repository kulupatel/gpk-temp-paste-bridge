The Client Who Wants the Invoice in Their Portal: How to Get Paid Through a System You Did Not Choose

The email arrives after the work is done: "Please upload your invoice to our vendor portal." There is a link, a login you do not have, and a form that asks for fields your invoice has never carried. The client is not refusing to pay. They are routing your payment through a system built for their procurement team, not for you, and every field you get wrong is a reason for the payment to sit in a queue you cannot see.

A portal is not a payment. It is a gate in front of one, and gates have rules that nobody explained to you. The work is getting the invoice through the gate in one pass, with the same due date you agreed, and a paper trail outside the portal so a silent queue never becomes a silent non-payment.

1. Register once, and treat registration as billable setup

Portal registration is work, and it is work you did not quote. Do it once, properly, and record everything it took: the login, the vendor ID it assigns you, the tax form it demands, the bank details it wants, and the name of the person who approves vendors. That record is worth more than the first payment, because the second invoice through the same portal takes minutes instead of an afternoon.

If registration asks for something you do not have, such as a tax form for a country you do not operate in, ask the client contact, not the portal help desk. The contact can usually confirm which fields are optional for a one-off supplier, or route you to the right form. Do not guess at tax or bank fields. A wrong bank detail entered in a hurry is a payment sent to the wrong place, and fixing it takes longer than asking did.

2. Match their fields, not your invoice layout

Your invoice is a document. Their portal is a database, and the database only reads what it is built to read. If it asks for a purchase order number, a cost centre, and a goods-received reference, those are not optional extras. An invoice without them is an invoice that never reaches the approver, however correct its total is.

Before you submit, ask your client contact for the three values that matter most: the purchase order number, the approver's name, and the cost centre or project code. Put them on the invoice itself as well as in the portal fields, so the paper document and the system record agree. If there is no purchase order, get that in writing too: "No PO required for this engagement, confirmed by [name] on [date]." A missing PO is the single most common reason portal invoices stall, and a one-line confirmation prevents it.

3. Keep your own due date, not the portal's clock

Here is the trap: the portal starts its own payment clock when the invoice is approved inside the system, not when you sent it. If approval takes three weeks, a 14-day term quietly becomes a 35-day payment, and the client can truthfully say they paid on time by their system's measure.

Protect yourself in the covering message that accompanies the upload: "Invoice [number] uploaded to the portal on [date], due [date] under our agreed 14-day terms." Then chase on your due date, not on the portal's approval date. If the client says the payment is still in the portal queue, your answer is the agreement: the terms were set when the work was commissioned, and a slow internal queue does not extend them. Most clients will escalate internally once they see the delay framed that way, because the queue is their problem, not a term you agreed to.

4. Keep a paper trail outside the portal

Never let the portal be the only place your invoice exists. Send the same invoice by email to your client contact on the day you upload it, with the portal submission reference in the subject line. That email does three jobs: it proves the date you submitted, it gives the contact something to forward when the queue stalls, and it means you are not locked out of your own evidence if the portal login stops working.

Screenshot the submission confirmation too, including the reference number and the date. Portal confirmations have a habit of living only inside the session that created them, and a reference number quoted in a chase email is much harder to ignore than "I think I uploaded it." If the portal offers a status page, check it once a week while the invoice is outstanding, and note the status and date each time. A status that has not moved in two weeks is a chase trigger, not a reason to wait quietly.

5. Decide, before the next project, whether the portal is worth it

Some portals are a one-hour setup for a client who will pay you ten times. Others are a three-hour setup, a tax form, and a quarterly re-verification for a single small invoice. The second kind is a cost, and costs belong in the price or in the decision to decline.

For the next project with a portal client, ask about the portal before you quote, not after you deliver. If the setup is heavy and the project is small, say so plainly: "I can work through your portal, and the setup time is reflected in the quote," or offer your normal invoice route with portal upload as a paid extra. Clients who genuinely need the portal will accept that. Clients who were using it as a convenient delay will suddenly find an email invoice acceptable after all.

The pattern underneath

A vendor portal is a gate, not a payment, and gates are crossed by matching their fields, keeping your own due date, and holding a paper trail outside the system. Register once and record it, get the purchase order and approver before you submit, chase on your terms rather than their queue, and price heavy portals into the next quote. The client gets their process followed, and you get paid on the date you agreed, not on the date their system got around to you.
