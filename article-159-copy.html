The Client Who Pays You, Then Asks for a Refund: How to Hold a Completed Payment

The money landed. The invoice closed. Then, a week later, or a month later, comes the message: "We would like a refund." Sometimes the reason is a changed mind. Sometimes it is a new manager who did not approve the spend. Sometimes it is a plain attempt to get the work for free, dressed as dissatisfaction. The freelancer who panics refunds the money and loses both the payment and the work. The freelancer who holds the payment does not argue about quality. They point at the record: the work was delivered, it was approved, the payment was made, and a refund is not part of the deal. Holding a completed payment is not stubbornness. It is the natural end of a transaction that already finished.

1. A refund request is a new negotiation, not an undo button

The first thing to understand is that a refund request does not rewind the project. The work exists. It was delivered, reviewed, and in most cases used. The client is not asking to return a product in a box. They are asking you to work for free, retroactively. Treat the request as a new negotiation, and answer it that way: warmly, slowly, and in writing. "Thanks for letting me know. Could you tell me specifically what is not working, and what outcome you are looking for?" This question does two things. It forces a vague complaint into a specific claim you can actually answer, and it slows the conversation down, which is exactly what a pressure tactic hates. Many refund requests collapse at this question, because the real reason was never quality. It was a budget cut, a changed priority, or a hope that you would fold quickly. The freelancer who answers a refund demand with a calm request for specifics has already changed the dynamic from demand to discussion.

2. Your defence is the approval record, not your opinion

Never defend your work with "I think it was good." Defend it with the record. The approval email. The message that said "this looks great, go ahead." The delivery receipt. The weeks the client used the work without complaint. Gather them before you reply, and quote them briefly: "The final files were approved on 12 March, and the website went live on 18 March." The approval record is powerful because it is not your opinion against theirs. It is their own words, dated, against a new story. If there is no approval record, that is the lesson for the next project, not a reason to refund this one. Even without a formal sign-off, a record of delivery plus use is strong: a client who used the work for a month cannot credibly claim it was worthless. Build the approval step into every project from now on, a short written sign-off before final files are released. It takes one email, and it is the single best refund defence a freelancer can own.

3. Offer a fix, never a refund, for a real problem

If the complaint is specific and real, offer to fix the specific thing, not to refund the project. "Happy to revise the homepage headline, that is covered." A fix is proportionate. A refund is not. The difference matters, because a client with a genuine small problem will accept a fix gladly, while a client running a free-work play will reject a fix and keep demanding the money. That rejection is useful information: it tells you the complaint was never about the work. Set the boundary clearly and once: "I do not offer refunds on completed, approved work, but I will put right anything that does not match the agreed brief." Then hold it. Every freelancer who has refunded once to "keep the peace" knows what happens next: the same client asks again, other clients hear about it, and the peace that was bought lasts about a week. A fix protects the relationship and the payment. A refund protects neither.

4. Watch the chargeback clock, and keep the evidence ready

A refund request that fails sometimes becomes a payment dispute: a card chargeback, a PayPal claim, a bank reversal. These are decided on evidence, not on who shouts loudest, and the evidence window is short. Keep the file ready from the day you are paid: the agreement, the brief, the delivery proof, the approval messages, and the record of the client's use of the work. If a dispute arrives, respond within the deadline with a short factual summary and the documents attached. Do not write an essay. Payment processors decide on documents, and a clean file wins more disputes than a passionate letter. The best defence, though, is structural: for larger projects, take payment by bank transfer where possible, and for card payments, never release final files before the payment clears. A dispute over money already in your account is an annoyance. A dispute over money that was never really yours is a loss.

5. Put the no-refund term in writing before the next job

The refund conversation is ten times easier when the answer is already in the agreement. Add one sentence before the next project starts: "Fees are non-refundable once work has been delivered and approved." Clients rarely object to it, because it is fair and it is standard, and the clients who do object are telling you something useful about how they handle payments. Repeat the term on the invoice, so it is seen twice. When the request later arrives, you are not inventing a policy under pressure. You are pointing at a term the client already accepted. The term does not make you inflexible. You can still fix a real problem, still offer a goodwill revision, still be generous where generosity is deserved. What the term does is stop generosity from being mistaken for an obligation, and that distinction is worth more than any single payment.

The pattern underneath

A completed payment is not a deposit on a future argument. It is the end of a transaction, and it should be treated that way. Answer a refund request with a calm request for specifics, defend the work with the approval record rather than your opinion, offer a fix for a real problem and never a refund for a vague one, keep the dispute evidence ready, and put the no-refund term in writing before the next job. The freelancer who holds a completed payment is not the one who argues hardest. They are the one whose record is cleanest, whose boundary was set earliest, and whose first reply is the calmest in the conversation.
