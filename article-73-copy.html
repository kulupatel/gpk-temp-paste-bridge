Getting Paid Across Borders: Currency, Fees, and Who Pays What

Your client is in another country. The work is done, the invoice is agreed, and then the payment arrives a few percent lighter than expected and three days late, and nobody can say exactly whose bank took the cut.

International payments do not fail because clients are dishonest. They fail because nobody decided the details in advance. Currency, fees, exchange timing, and banking delays each need one written line before the invoice goes out.

Name the currency in the contract

This is the single most skipped line in freelancer contracts, and it causes the most arguments.

Pick one: you invoice in your currency, or you invoice in theirs. Write it down. Then pick the exchange-rate rule: is the amount fixed at the invoice date, or at the payment date? Write that down too.

Invoicing in your own currency is usually the safer choice for you. The client’s bank does the conversion, the client absorbs the rate movement, and the number on your invoice is the number you expect to receive. If you invoice in the client’s currency, you are quietly accepting exchange-rate risk on every invoice, and a 3% currency swing during a 30-day payment term comes straight out of your pocket.

Whatever you choose, "we will figure it out later" is the most expensive sentence in international freelancing.

Who absorbs the transfer fees

The hidden tax on cross-border payments is the transfer fee, and it varies wildly by method. A bank wire can cost a flat $25 to $45 on each end. PayPal’s cross-border fees run several percentage points once currency conversion is included. Services built for transfers, like Wise, typically charge a small transparent percentage. The gap between the cheapest and most expensive route on a $2,000 invoice can be over $100.

So decide the rule before the first invoice, and put it in writing. The three standard options:

  • Client covers all transfer fees: you receive the full invoiced amount. Cleanest for you; state it as "all transfer fees are the client's responsibility."
  • Fees split: each side pays its own bank’s charges. Common and fair, but confirm both banks’ charges in advance.
  • Baked into your rate: you quote slightly higher and absorb the fees silently. Simplest for the client, but only works if you actually did the math.

What does not work: saying nothing, then arguing about a $38 wire fee after the money has moved.

The exchange-rate timing problem

You invoice $1,000 on the 1st. The client pays on the 28th. Between those dates, the exchange rate moved 2%. Who eats the difference?

If your contract says the rate is fixed at the invoice date, the answer is clear: the client owes the equivalent of $1,000 at the invoice-date rate, and any shortfall is theirs to top up. If it says payment-date rate, the risk is yours.

Most freelancers do not need anything fancy here. One sentence works: "Amounts are fixed in USD at the exchange rate on the invoice date; any shortfall caused by rate movement is the client's responsibility to cover." Adjust the currency to yours.

Banking delays are not late payments

An international wire takes one to five business days. A transfer service is usually faster but not instant. Your payment terms need to account for this, or every cross-border invoice will look "late" through no one's fault.

Define what the due date means: is it "funds in my account by this date" or "payment initiated by this date"? Pick one and write it down. "Payment initiated" is friendlier and more realistic across borders; "funds received" is stricter and simpler to enforce.

Either way, add the 48-hour proof rule: on the due date, the client sends the transfer receipt or confirmation screenshot. A client who has genuinely paid will always have proof. A client who has not will have excuses, and now you know which one you are dealing with on day one instead of day ten.

Chasing late payment across time zones

The escalation ladder works the same internationally, with three adjustments.

First, be explicit about dates and time zones in every message. "Please pay by Friday" means nothing across a nine-hour gap. "Please pay by Friday, October 24, end of day London time" does.

Second, confirm receipt windows before escalating. If the client sent proof of transfer on the due date, give the banking system its full five business days before your first chase email. Chasing a payment that is legitimately in transit burns goodwill for nothing.

Third, keep the paper trail in one thread, same as domestic work. International disputes are harder to resolve, which makes documentation more important, not less.

Keep records your accountant will thank you for

Save every transfer receipt alongside its invoice. Note the amount you actually received in your own currency and the date it landed. If a client tops up a shortfall from exchange movement, keep that receipt too.

This is not just good hygiene. When tax time comes, your records need to show what you were owed, what you received, and when. A folder of transfer confirmations turns a painful reconciliation into a ten-minute job.

Cross-border clients are some of the best clients you can have: bigger budgets, longer engagements, interesting work. They just need one extra page of payment terms. Write it once, reuse it forever.
