The Bounced Payment: What to Do When a Client's Check or Transfer Fails

A client tells you the payment is sent. Then it is not. The check bounces, the transfer reverses, the wire never arrives. Failed payments are rarer than late ones, but they cost more: your time, your bank fees, and a false sense of closure that lets the real delay grow. Here is the exact protocol for the moment "sent" turns out to mean nothing.

First: confirm it actually failed

Before you accuse anyone, verify independently. Ask your bank whether anything arrived and whether anything was reversed, and check your payment apps for pending or returned transfers. A surprising number of "bounced payment" scares turn out to be the client's transfer sitting in a pending state for one extra day.

Get the failure in writing from your side: a bank statement line showing the reversal, a returned-check notice, a "payment failed" email from the processor. Screenshots with dates. This is now part of the paper trail for this invoice, and it matters more than any other document in this episode because it is the only proof that "I already paid" is false.

The first response: facts, not suspicion

Failed payments feel like lies. Most of them are not. Checks bounce because of timing, transfers fail because of wrong account details, wires die in correspondent-bank limbo. Assume a systems failure on the first failure and say so:

Subject: Invoice INV-2058: payment did not arrive

Hi [Name],

The payment for invoice INV-2058 ($3,500) did not arrive on my end. My bank shows no incoming transfer on [date], and [payment app / bank] shows no pending payment under your name.

Could you send me the confirmation of the transfer, a receipt, reference number, or screenshot? If there is a reversal on your side, the reference will tell us what happened.

I have re-attached the invoice with my payment details so there is no mix-up. Let me know what you find.

Thanks, [Your name]

You are giving the client two things: a way to prove they tried, and a clean path to try again. If the client sends a genuine confirmation, work the problem together. If the client goes quiet, you have learned something.

Who pays the failed-payment costs

Your bank will almost certainly charge you a returned-payment fee, typically $10 to $35. Someone has to eat it, and it should not be you. State this in the same message where you confirm the failure:

"Please also add the $25 returned-payment fee my bank charged to the re-sent payment, per section 5 of our agreement."

If your contract does not have a failed-payment clause, add one now for every future engagement: "Payments that fail or are reversed incur a $[amount] processing fee, charged to the client." One sentence. It costs you nothing to add and it ends the argument before it starts.

Also: the payment clock does not reset. The invoice was due on its due date. A failed payment is not an extension; it is an unpaid invoice with a story. Late fees, if your contract has them, keep accruing from the original due date. Say this once, plainly, in the same message. "The original due date of October 14 still stands."

The second failure: change the rules

One failed payment is a systems problem. Two failed payments is a policy problem. If the first re-send also fails, stop accepting that payment method from this client. Require cleared funds: a bank transfer that has landed (not one that is "sent"), a cashier's check, or payment through an escrow-style processor where the money is verified before release.

Say it without drama:

"Two payments have failed now, so I need to switch the method. Please pay by [bank transfer to these details], and I will confirm the day it lands. I will pause further work until the balance clears."

Work pause is the correct consequence here, not anger. A client whose money repeatedly cannot move is a client you cannot plan around. Pausing is not punishment; it is risk management.

The pattern that means it was never a systems problem

Watch for this specific sequence: the client claims payment was sent, it fails, the client claims a second attempt was sent, it fails or never shows up, and every explanation shifts (wrong account, bank error, assistant's mistake, system outage). Honest failures come with confirmations and reference numbers. Dishonest ones come with new stories and no paperwork.

If you are three failures in with no confirmation ever produced, stop treating it as a payment problem and start treating it as a non-payment problem: the escalation ladder applies. Final notice, stated deadline, and the demand-letter stage. The failed-payment theater is just the client's version of the stall lines, and it ends the same way every other stall ends: with a written deadline.

Prevention: the three lines that kill this in advance

Add these to your standard terms and your kickoff email, and most of this episode never happens:

  • "Payment is considered received when funds clear in my account, not when the transfer is initiated." This one sentence ends every "but I sent it" argument before it starts.
  • "Failed or reversed payments incur a $[amount] processing fee charged to the client." Puts the cost where it belongs.
  • "Work pauses until failed payments clear." Gives you a real lever on the second failure.

Three lines, no lawyer, and the entire bounced-payment episode collapses into one message with an attached invoice.
